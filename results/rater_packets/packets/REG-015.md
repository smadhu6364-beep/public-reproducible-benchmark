I.  STRATEGIC CONTEXT

A. Country Context
1.
Mongolia’s economic growth has been impressive—though volatile—in the last decade. Mongolia is the 18th
largest country by area with a population of about 3.3 million in 2022. Landlocked between Russia and China, its vast rural
areas lack essential infrastructure and connectivity. Mongolia has demonstrated significant success in reducing poverty
and elevating human well-being since the early 1990s. Mongolia's ongoing mineral export boom is delivering positive
macro-fiscal outcomes for the second consecutive year. Fueled by coal exports, economic growth is projected to remain
strong, though it is expected to moderate to 5.3 percent in 2024, down from 7.2 percent in 2023. Growth is forecast to
average 6.3 percent over 2025-2026, driven by a significant increase in mineral production, particularly copper from the
Oyu Tolgoi (OT) mine. Robust economic growth post-COVID has elevated Mongolia to upper-middle-income status in
2024. Yet, this resource-driven growth highlights Mongolia's growing dependence on the volatile mining sector,
underscores the urgent need for economic diversification and structural reforms to address increasing climate and
development challenges.
2.
The country’s efforts toward decarbonization pose significant challenges given the structurally high-emission
intensity of its economy. While it is not a significant contributor to global emissions, Mongolia’s economy is the most
greenhouse gas (GHG) intensive globally 1 and it has the ninth highest per capita emissions of GHGs. Mongolia’s
unconditional Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) target is to reduce GHG emissions by 22.7 percent compared to
a baseline case by 2030, which would still represent a 122 percent increase in GHG emissions since 2010.2  The production
and use of coal, mainly for electricity and heating, accounts for 45 percent of total emissions (2024 Mongolia Country
Climate and Development Report). Mongolia aims to steer the economy towards a greener growth model. Green
development is included as a stand-alone pillar in Vision 2050, the government’s long term development plan, signaling
its importance in Mongolia’s future development.  Under the pillar, the government restates its commitment to tackling
climate change and emphasizes the importance of proper resource management as part of its climate change adaptation
efforts.
B. Sectoral and Institutional Context
3.
Mongolia’s electric power sector has been undergoing a series of reforms aiming to transform the sector into a
market-oriented system. In 2001, the power sector was unbundled, and the Energy Regulatory Commission (ERC) was
established to regulate the generation, transmission, distribution, dispatch, and supply of energy. ERC oversees a singlebuyer model in which the National Dispatching Center, which is also the system operator, manages a zero-balance account
as it buys electricity at a regulated tariff from the generators and sells it to the distribution companies. Mongolia’s
electricity sector is owned and operated mainly by commercialized state-owned enterprises. The whole sector heavily
relies on government subsidies, due to tariffs only covering the operational cost.
4.
The current electricity price is below the cost of service causing financial difficulties for sector utilities. These
tariffs only cover fuel, operation and maintenance costs, preventing generation, transmission and distribution companies
from making investments with their own funds. With the increased cost of power purchases from a newly commissioned

5.
Mongolia has five regional power systems which are partially interconnected by relatively weak transmission
lines. The Central Energy System (CES)3 is the largest system which serves the main load center of Ulaanbaatar and
accounts for over 80 percent of the total power supply for the country. The peak power demand in CES was around 1,469
Megawatt (MW) in 2022 while other parts of the system such as the Southern Energy System (SES) have less than 100
MW of power demand (not including the demand of the OT mine, which is supplied by China’s power grid). Power demand,
including both peak demand and total energy consumption, has been increasing in the past 20 years. The average growth
rate of the peak load since 2000 was 4.3 percent. According to the future demand growth forecasts shared by the Ministry
of Energy (MOE) with the World Bank, the peak load of CES will continue to grow at 7-12 percent per annum till 2030.
6.
Power supply in the country has heavily relied on coal-fired generation, as well as power imports. As of 2022,
coal-fired combined heat and power (CHP) plants, mainly developed to meet heat demand, accounted for 90 percent of
domestic power generation. Renewable energy (RE) such as hydro, wind, and solar contributed 10 percent of domestic
power generation in 2022. The total installed capacity in 2022 was 1,621 MW, while the total capacity of RE plants reached
268 MW, comprising 23 MW of small hydropower, 90 MW of solar photovoltaic (PV), and 155 MW of wind. In particular,
CES has 1,446 MW (out of which 245 MW is of wind and solar PV) of generation capacity, which is almost equal to its peak
load, with little reserve margin. In the past ten years, domestic generation was falling short of demand and Mongolia’s
dependency on Russian imports reached almost 20 percent in 2022.
7.
The current generation fleet is dominated by CHP plants, making the system inflexible in dispatch. Most of the
CHP units are aging (the youngest one is more than 40 years old), inefficient and highly polluting. The CHP plants make
the power system inflexible, making it indispatchable and difficult to integrate Variable Renewable Energy (VRE) such as
solar and wind energy. An Asian Development Bank financed utility-scale Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) project
with a capacity of 80MW/200MWh was commissioned in 2023, easing the inflexibility issues to some extent.
8.
Transmission losses are high with a concomitant impact on the quality of electricity service. With lengthy
transmission lines spanning several hundreds of kilometers and a relatively low voltage system of 110 kilo-volt (kV), the
combined transmission and distribution losses in CES in 2021 remained high at 13.4 percent. In addition, due to the lack
of redundancy of the power system, with only a single 110kV corridor in many parts of the system, the system average
interruption duration index (SAIDI) in CES reached 16 days and the system average interruption frequency index (SAIFI)
was seven times in 2021. The region served by the existing Baganuur-Choir single circuit line experienced a supply outage
of 33 hours in 2021 caused by a fault on the line.
9.
The 220kV backbone transmission line which runs from the Ulaanbaatar area towards the remote regions plays
a vital role for social and economic development in Mongolia. Among several remote regions in Mongolia, the
southeastern region of the country has abundant potential of VREs as well as large scale industrial load. In particular, key
industries for the social and economic development of Mongolia, such as the Ulaanbaatar railway and the Zamiyn Uud
railway port, are supplied with electricity through the transmission corridor running along Baganuur-Choir-Sainshand. To
power the economic growth along the corridor, a new 220kV Choir-Sainshand double circuit transmission line is under
construction, while the crucial Baganuur-Choir corridor remains as a bottleneck for power transmission because it lacks
redundancy with only a single circuit 220kV line. In addition to the proposed 220kV Baganuur-Choir transmission line,

Very few RE projects have been realized so far in spite of abundant solar and wind resource potential. A previous

that at least 455MW of wind and solar projects can be connected to the Choir substation if the 220kV Baganuur-Choir
corridor is reinforced with around 600 MW of additional transmission capacity.
C. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives
12.
The Project will contribute to the World Bank Group’s Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Mongolia for
the period FY21-FY25 (Report No. 132141-MN), especially Focus Area 2 of the CPF which centers on boosting the
competitiveness of the real economic sectors. Through Objective 2.4 ‘Improving connectivity through sustainable
infrastructure’, the World Bank aims to support government efforts to improve infrastructure to leverage the potential
economic opportunities of the unique geographical features of Mongolia. The proposed Project will help strengthen
interconnection with remote networks in Mongolia, as well as create an enabling environment and conditions to meet
energy demands in a green and sustainable manner by allowing a higher VRE penetration in the power system.
13.
The Project is well aligned with the New Recovery Policy (NRP)6, 10-year strategy of Mongolia, and the new
Government Action Plan (GAP) for 2024-2028. In the NRP, the GoM identified “Energy recovery” as one of the primary
areas in the policy, with actions including establishing new energy sources, expanding transmission and distribution
networks to increase their capacity and improve the reliability and adding more renewable and storage projects to
enhance flexibility of the system.  Furthermore, the NRP is a 10-year roadmap for the effective implementation of the
Long-Term Vision 2050 Policy. The policy target is to increase the share of RE in generation mix to 20 percent by 2023 and
30 percent by 2030. The proposed transmission line is listed as part of the infrastructure that will be developed to achieve
these overarching objectives. The proposed Project is also fully aligned with the GAP for 2024-2028 which was approved

D. Multiphase Programmatic Approach
15.
This Project Appraisal Document (PAD) covers Phase 2 of the Accelerating Sustainable Energy Transition
Program under the Multi-Phase Programmatic Approach (MPA). Phase 1 of the MPA was approved by the World Bank’s
Executive Directors on September 24, 2024 (P181555, Report No. PAD 5623)7. With Phase 1, the Board approved an overall
financing envelope of US$2.5 billion for the six East Asia and Pacific countries listed under the MPA, including US$1.9
billion in IBRD financing and US$600 million in IDA financing.  Phase 2 is proposed to benefit Mongolia. The US$47 million
IBRD loan for Mongolia proposed under Phase 2 does not exceed the approved Program envelope.
16.
The Accelerating Sustainable Energy Transition Program MPA includes several phases which will be
implemented over 10 years. Phase 1 includes Papua New Guinea and the Republic of Marshall Islands alongside a
technical assistance project implemented by the ASEAN Centre for Energy (ACE). On October 29, 2024, the ACE grant
became effective, thereby rendering the overall Program effective. For Papua New Guinea and the Republic of Marshall
Islands, work is underway to expedite project effectiveness and initiate fiduciary and environmental and social (E&S)
activities. The proposed phasing has been determined in consultation with the respective Borrowers and reflects current
level of readiness, political commitment, complementarities with ongoing interventions financed by other international
financial institutions /donors as well as synergies and prioritization along key energy investments. Using these criteria,
Phase 2 is now moving ahead with Mongolia. Operations in Cambodia, the Federated States of Micronesia and Indonesia
are under preparation as future phases of the MPA.  Please refer to Table 1 below for an updated Program Framework.
i.
Revised Program Development Objective (if any) and its Relevance
17.
The MPA’s PrDO remains relevant and unchanged for Phase 2. The PrDO of the MPA is to accelerate the scaleup and grid integration of RE in participating countries across the East Asia and Pacific region. The aim is to enable the
development of 2.5 gigawatts of RE generation capacity, resulting in a net reduction of 60 million tons of CO2 equivalent
GHG emissions. To achieve its PrDO, the MPA activities are organized around three pillars: (i) Pillar One: Enabling policies
and strengthened institutions; (ii) Pillar Two: Expanded and more flexible energy systems; and (iii) Pillar Three: De-risking
clean energy investments.

Updated Program Framework
19.
The MPA has been designed to address critical constraints to RE deployment and is based on extensive and
long-standing client engagement in the region. The design of the MPA is intentionally designed with overlapping phases
to ensure complementarity and incorporation of lessons and experience from preceding phases. Phase 1 of the Program
is currently under implementation (US$265 million in IDA financing), while activities under Phase 2 (US$47 million in IBRD
financing) are detailed in this PAD. Estimated financing for operations in subsequent phases under the MPA has been
updated to reflect the latest discussions, while the overall financing envelope remains unchanged.
20.
The total envelope for Phase 2 is US$47 million, all of which will be for Mongolia. This phase will directly
contribute to Pillar Two. The Project aims to expand and strengthen the 220 kV level transmission system in Mongolia.
The Project expects to increase the transmission capacity to deliver the RE generation from the resource rich areas in the
south to the load center in the north in Mongolia. These initiatives will enable RE projects to operate at full capacity,
ensuring a steady and predictable revenue stream, thereby attracting more private investment.

II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION

A. Project Development Objective
(i) PDO Statement
The Project Development Objective (PDO) is to increase the capacity and reliability of the transmission grid in the
Project Areas for renewable energy integration.
(ii) PDO Level Indicators
The proposed Phase 2 Project is fully aligned with MPA Program level objectives of enabling RE capacity and GHG
emission reduction.
The PDO level indicators for the proposed Project are listed below:
•
Projected lifetime net GHG emissions from results achieved (tons of CO2eq),
•
Increased transmission capacity in Project Areas (MW),
•
Reduced supply outage caused by faults on the transmission grid (MWh/year), and
•
Renewable energy capacity enabled with indirect support (MW).
Results Chain
21.
The Project aims to increase the capacity of the grid to enhance integration of VRE. In line with Pillar Two of the
MPA’s Results Chain8, the objectives will be achieved by strengthening the existing 220kV grid by adding a new 220kV line
to the specific region where there is no high voltage transmission line available to evacuate power generated from the
southern part of Mongolia. These investments were selected based on the assessment of existing and future demand in
terms of: (i) transmission capacity required; (ii) redundancy of the grid configuration; and (iii) solar and wind potential.
The proposed transmission line will contribute to the integration of new VRE and maximize the generation from existing
VRE projects, as well as enhancing resilience to withstand from climate impacts. This line is crucial to evacuate the RE
power to be generated in the southern part of Mongolia, which has the highest solar potential. This will lead to establishing
favorable environments for private sector investments in VRE and results in GHG emission reduction. Together, the Project
will enable Mongolia to shift to low-emission development pathways, reduce dependency on fossil fuels, and improve the
sustainability and resilience of the power sector.
B. Project Components
22.
Component 1: Transmission Line Construction and Associated Substations Expansion (in total US$45.84 million).
This component will finance the construction of one 220 kV double-circuit transmission line from Baganuur Substation to
Choir Substation and expansion of its associated substations (Figure 1), which will strengthen the core 220kV transmission
system in the CES. The investment will enable the integration of large industrial loads, as well as large-scale VREs, to the
Mongolia power grid. This line will, therefore, serve the following purposes: (i) increase the supply capacity of electricity;
(ii) enable VRE generation from the region; and (iii) improve system reliability by enhancing the redundancy of the CES
network. Other 220kV transmission lines may be included at a later stage of the project with additional financing,
depending on the situation and urgency.

35.
The proposed Project is listed as a priority transmission line in the NRP that needs to be put into operation

insufficient. The existing grid is unable to evacuate any additional new RE power generation. The feasibility study of the
transmission line was prepared by the NPTG and approved by the MOE. Furthermore, the Grid Study supported by the
World Bank also confirms the feasibility and priority of the Baganuur-Choir transmission line for strengthening the power
system in Mongolia. The Baganuur-Choir transmission line is especially important and has high priority because: (i) the
corridor runs towards the southeast region in Mongolia where huge potentials of solar and wind exist; and (ii) the existing
corridor has only one circuit with limited capacity, which has hindered the additional development of RE power plants.

36.
The operation is aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement on both mitigation and adaptation.
•
Assessment and reduction of mitigation risks: the operation is not at a material risk of having a negative impact
on the country’s low-GHG-emissions development pathway. Activities financed under this operation are related
to the construction of the electricity transmission network and institutional capacity building, which are
universally aligned. The construction of the transmission network does not involve constructing a transmission
line dedicated to the evacuation of electricity from new fossil fuel power generation plants. Thus, the operation
is aligned on mitigation.
•
Assessment and reduction of adaptation risks: Mongolia is prone to risks from climate hazards such as flooding,
wildfires, and landslides, with exposure to drought and extreme cold identified as the strongest drivers of risk.
The climate and disaster risk screening conducted for the operation indicates a moderate risk of hazards and
disasters impacting the operation. The operation has incorporated measures to reduce these risks, including
enhanced engineering and design standards that consider weather and natural hazards in the tower structures
design, such as wind speed (highest projected wind loading), rated design temperature, snow, and freezing
conditions (improving insulator design), earthquake and flood. A detailed feasibility study of the Baganuur-Choir
transmission line was also prepared to inform environmental and social impact assessment, including
considerations of risks from climate hazards. Furthermore, the corridor identified for the proposed transmission
line comprises of plain fields and small hills with no trees, further reducing the impacts of climate hazards. Thus,
the measures combined reduce risks from climate hazards to an acceptable level, and the operation is aligned on
adaptation and resilience.

12.
Four disbursement methods will be available for the Project including advance, reimbursement, direct payment,
and special commitment. The primary method of disbursement for the Project will be the advance method. Supporting
documents for the Bank disbursements will be statements of expenditures (SOEs). Detailed disbursement arrangements
are laid out in the project Disbursement and Financial Information Letter (DFIL) to be issued by the Bank and agreed with
the recipient.
13.
The MOF and MOE will jointly maintain and manage a segregated USD DA at a commercial bank, on terms and
conditions satisfactory to the Bank, including appropriate protection against set off, seizure and attachments. The ceiling
of the DA will be discussed and agreed between the Bank and the recipient and will be specified in the DFIL.
14.
Funds will be disbursed from the Bank to the Project DA in USD. The DA will mainly be used for USD transactions.

ANNEX 2: Economic and Financial Analysis

1.
This annex discusses the rationale for public financing of the project, the valued added from the Bank support
presents the analysis of the project’s development impact in terms of expected benefits and costs.
Rationale for public sector provision/financing
2.
The transmission utility in Mongolia is wholly owned by the government and are expected to remain in public
ownership over near to medium term. Thus, to service the growing demand, and to transmit and distribute the power
that would be available through the renewable energy sources, significant public investments are needed to upgrade the
transmission and as well as distribution network while simultaneously ensuring that the burden on the consumers (tariffs)
or the state government finances is minimized.
Value added of the Bank’s support
3.
The Bank is expected to add value in this Project by (i) drawing on the Bank's global and local experience in the
implementation of power transmission projects; (ii) tapping into the Bank's experience with institutional development of
utilities, including improved planning and corporate governance; (iii) using transparent procurement processes under
Bank-financed projects to enable discovery of competitive prices; and (iv) bringing credibility to Mongolia's power sector
reform and reducing the risk perception of some investors towards the Mongolia’s power sector.
Development Impact
4.
The development impact of the Project is assessed through a cost-benefit analysis. Mongolia faces significant
economic and environment costs from not having a clean, reliable supply of electricity. The transmission and distribution
have limited capacity and the obsolete infrastructure poses serious constraint to power supply. Poor infrastructure poses
a threat to quality of life and development of industrial/commercial business. Long and constrained transmission lines,
outdated management of the power grid also contribute to significant technical losses. There will be a large economic
payoff from the successful implementation of the Project, including other transmission structures that are under
implementation/construction.

Project benefits
7.
The economic benefits are calculated through the PLEXOS software which is commonly used worldwide to conduct
least-cost hourly dispatch simulation over the evaluation period.  Mongolia’s power system is modeled in the PLEXOS with
methodologies and assumptions based on relevant information from stakeholders of Mongolia power sector, especially

target transmission line, and (ii) without the target transmission line.
8.
The Project’s economic benefits are mainly comprised of reduced generation cost and avoided unserved energy:
•
Reduction in generation cost. The increased capacity of the grid by the completion of the new
transmission line will contribute to enhancing the usage of competitive generation sources, mainly
renewables in the grid, and in turn reduce the overall system cost.
•
Reduction in unserved energy. The increase in grid capacity and redundancy would contribute to
meeting new demand and reducing the frequency of system outages. This will lead to the reduction in
the amount of unserved demand, which valued at the VOLL.
