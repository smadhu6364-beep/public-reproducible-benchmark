I. MPA PROGRAM STRATEGIC CONTEXT
1.
Jobs for Africa’s youth represent the region’s greatest opportunity for economic transformation. With the right
skills and reforms, Eastern and Southern Africa (AFE) can turn demographic pressure into an engine of inclusive growth.
The Multi-Phase Programmatic Approach (MPA) Skills for Economic Transformation and Jobs (SET4Jobs) Program in AFE
(P512820) will support this ambition by scaling regional and national reforms that deliver market-relevant skills and foster
job creation in priority value chains. Phase 1 of the MPA was approved on February 26, 2026; Phase 2 and Phase 3 on March
31, 2026, Phase 4 on June 3, 2026; and Phase 5 on June 16, 2026. Given the short timeline of project phasing under the
horizontal MPA, the strategic context remains the same as described in the Program Appraisal Document (PAD) of Phase
1.
II.
MPA PROGRAM DESCRIPTION
A.
Program Development Objective and Indicators
2.
The PrDO is to strengthen skills eco-systems for more and better jobs in priority value chains. Priority value
chains are selected based on their potential to generate employment, drive productivity, and support inclusive growth. Job
creation refers to the corporate indicator of new and better jobs associated with targeted1 value chains. “Better jobs” are
those with higher wages, greater stability, and improved working conditions, achieved when beneficiaries acquire jobrelevant skills. “Skills eco-system” includes supply, demand and intermediation. The program will track both the quantity
and quality of jobs generated (Table 1).

B.

3.
The Program balances two goals: enabling rapid upskilling and job creation for youth while laying the
foundations of demand-led skills ecosystems that adapt to evolving labor market needs.2 The value chain approach
reconciles these goals by aligning skills investments with sector reforms and demand-side interventions, increasing the
likelihood that supported youth find jobs and that value chains expand with better skills. It delivers early impact by scaling

C.
Program Beneficiaries
4.
The main beneficiaries of this MPA are youth and working-age adults in the AFE region who will gain jobrelevant skills and better employment prospects in priority sectors. Over a decade, the MPA aims to benefit students
and current workers through education, training, and upskilling programs. The initiative prioritizes marginalized groups,
especially women and youth, while also engaging employers, institutions, and policymakers to strengthen demand-driven
skills development. The MPA is expected to equip 18 million youth with job-relevant skills and create 17 million jobs in
selected value chains.
D.
Program Description
5.
This MPA provides a new regional framework for skills engagement, moving from isolated projects to
supporting skills eco-systems that enable job creation at scale. The framework guides country-level operations by
anchoring skills investments in specific value chains with high job growth potential, recognizing that skilling alone cannot
succeed without parallel efforts to expand and transform these sectors. Developing value chains requires coordinated
action to remove multiple constraints; the Program will leverage Development Policy Financing (DPF) and sectoral
Programs for Results (PforRs) to support policy reforms and crowd in investment. The MPA ensures that skills supply aligns
with real labor demand while also supporting productivity upgrading and entrepreneurship within targeted value chains.

Its comprehensive approach addresses both the growth of employment opportunities and the preparation of more youth
to access better jobs in these expanding sectors. System reforms will support sustainability.3
E.
Program Framework
6.
The MPA supports about ten countries over an eight-year implementation period with an estimated closing in
2033. This support will be completed by technical assistance and financing platforms implemented by regional entities.
With an onboarding window of up to three years from effectiveness, flexibility will be provided by allowing each country
to select the priority value chain or value chains according to its strategic sectors, and to sequence interventions based on
the maturity of those value chains or on projections for their strengthening. The MPA allows countries and regional bodies
to join the Program based on their readiness and relevance of the MPA approach to the regional and specific contexts.

F.
Learning Agenda
7.
This MPA adopts a practical learning agenda to address key evidence gaps and guide adaptive implementation
with integrated data, feedback, and verification across all components and the regional platform. Evidence will be
generated through interoperable systems (LMIS/EMIS) combining skills demand, supply, outcomes, employer surveys, and
vacancy tracking to inform policy and investment decisions throughout implementation. Performance-based financing
and contracts will provide outcome data on graduate employment and institutional performance. Sector skills councils
will involve employers to shape learning priorities and align their findings with market needs. Under pillar 4, the ACE
network, facilitated by IUCEA in line with AfCFTA, will support regional collaboration, standard harmonization, and
knowledge sharing for evidence-based decision-making.4

G.
Program Implementation
8.
Implementation arrangements for the SET4Jobs MPA are anchored in a multi-level coordination mechanism to
ensure effectiveness and accountability. The framework establishes: (i) country-specific implementation through national
education, TVET, higher education, labor and youth ministries; (ii) regional coordination led by the IUCEA; and (iii) tailored
selection of national implementing agencies based on each country's institutional context. This strategic approach will
ensure seamless implementation aligned with World Bank guidelines while fostering cross-sectoral collaboration and
embedding accountability mechanisms at every level of project execution.5

I.
STRATEGIC CONTEXT
A. Country Context
1.
São Tomé and Príncipe (STP) is a lower-middle-income island country located off the western coast of Central
Africa, facing structural economic challenges typical of small, remote nations. With a total area of 1,001 km², it is the
second smallest and least populous country in Africa. Among its approximately 230,000 inhabitants, the majority reside
in urban areas, particularly in and around the capital city of São Tomé. The other main island, Príncipe—home to just
about 10,000 people—is more than 170 km away and experiences compounded isolation. STP’s small size and insularity
have restricted access to international markets and hindered industrial development. The economy is dominated by
informal, low-productivity activities, with the service sector, including tourism, contributing 79 percent of GDP.6
Persistently low domestic revenue mobilization has kept STP heavily dependent on Official Development Assistance
(ODA), which has averaged 10 percent of GDP in the past decade, leaving fiscal revenues highly volatile.7 These
constraints are compounded by the country’s exposure to climate hazards, which increasingly affect infrastructure,
economic activity, and livelihoods.

2.
Slow economic growth and covariate shocks over the last two decades have constrained job creation,
undermined progress in reducing poverty, and limited investments in human capital. Over the past 20 years, STP’s real
per capita GDP growth has averaged only 1.3 percent annually, dampened by compounded shocks that have included
the COVID-19 pandemic (which severely hit tourism), geopolitical turmoil (which drove volatility in fuel and food import
costs), recurrent flooding, and an electricity crisis.8 Structural constraints such as costly and unreliable electricity, fragile
institutions, and limited connectivity continue to undermine economic activity. Job creation has remained far below the
needs of the population: at 21 percent, STP’s employment-to-population ratio is significantly lower than the 59 percent
average in peer countries.9 Poverty remains widespread, with 31 percent of Santomeans living below US$4.20/day (LMIC
poverty line at 2021 PPP) and 13 percent below the extreme poverty line of US$3.00/day. Livelihoods depend heavily on
low-productivity activities in agriculture and fisheries, which are highly exposed to climate-related shocks. Working
poverty is common, affecting 51 percent of informal workers and 35 percent of those in formal employment.10 STP’s
Human Capital Index Plus (HCI+ of 113.1 against the full benchmark of 325 reflects persistent gaps in education, health,
and employment-related learning that continue to weigh on future worker productivity and long-term growth potential.11
3.
The STP economy is characterized by a small private sector, with most job opportunities currently arising from
a handful of medium and large firms. Micro and small enterprises make up 92 percent of all firms in STP—well above
the 60 percent average in peer countries—highlighting the limited development and diversification of the private
sector.12 Most are household-run and provide few job opportunities. Informality is widespread, with about two-thirds of
private sector jobs informal. A small number of medium and large firms provide over 60 percent of private sector
employment. According to the 2021 Business Census, most firms operate in services, notably wholesale and retail trade,
which account for 62 percent of firms and 30 percent of private employment. Accommodation and food services, largely
driven by the tourism industry, represent 10 percent of firms and 6 percent of jobs. The public sector absorbs another 16

4.
Economic activities and jobs are increasingly exposed to climate-related hazards. STP is vulnerable to coastal
and riparian floods, severe storms, landslides, and droughts. Climate change is anticipated to exacerbate these threats
through increased rainfall and rising sea levels, a concern since most people and economic activities are concentrated
along the coast and near rivers.13 Sea level rise is already driving coastal erosion, salinization, and more frequent flash
floods.14 The contamination of soil and groundwater from floodings is expected to impact agricultural productivity,
threaten food security, and increase food prices, particularly affecting vulnerable populations in low-lying coastal areas.
Longer dry seasons and extreme rainfall threaten year-round freshwater availability, while warmer seas are pushing fish
stocks into deeper waters, undermining traditional fishing. These climate risks directly undermine the productivity and
stability of livelihoods across key sectors where a large share of Santomeans earn their income. While workers in
agriculture and fisheries are expected to be most affected, impacts may also extend to other sectors, such as tourism.
5.
Despite these challenges, by capitalizing on its competitive advantages in key sectors and on the prospects of
labor mobility, STP has the potential to stimulate economic growth, create more and better jobs, and lay the
foundations for a more resilient and sustainable development path. The country’s geographic remoteness, while posing
constraints, also presents distinct opportunities: its rich biodiversity supports nature-based tourism, enables the
production of specialized agricultural goods, and sustains abundant fisheries. These sectors already contribute to growth
and employment but remain far from reaching their full potential.15  By deepening value chains, enhancing market access,
and improving productivity, STP could position itself as a hub for sustainable tourism, high-value agriculture, and
responsible fisheries, unlocking new avenues for job creation. Ensuring climate-resilient production systems and
sustainable resource management in these sectors will be critical. In addition, forward-looking sectors such as renewable
energy, digital transformation, and climate-resilient construction, which are poised to receive additional investments,
could act as critical multipliers for productivity and job creation across the economy. Finally, STP’s existing migration
dynamics may also present opportunities for Santomeans to access more and better jobs abroad, strengthening
remittance flows and generating additional investments in the local economy.16
B. Sectoral and Institutional Context
6.
Key strategic sectors for more and better jobs in STP include tourism, agriculture, and fisheries/blue economy,

structured around accommodation, food and beverage services, tours and guide operations, arts and crafts, and

challenges relate to infrastructure, sustainable management of natural ecosystems, destination promotion, and the
availability of an adequately prepared workforce. Agriculture remains the backbone of rural employment in STP, with
distinct value chains for subsistence and export crops. Production is dominated by smallholders holding about two-thirds

Energy, digital transformation, and construction are three other strategically important sectors, acting as critical
enablers of productivity and growth across the wider economy, despite more modest direct employment potential.
STP’s Energy sector is dominated by diesel-based thermal generation, with renewables still at an early stage. The value
chain includes fuel imports, power generation, transmission and distribution, and a growing segment of small-scale solar
and off-grid solutions. While its direct job creation potential may be modest, the sector is poised to receive additional
investments that will require a qualified workforce and will be essential to unlock private sector investments in the
country. The Digital sector remains nascent in STP but is strategically important for firm productivity and for connecting
the economy to external markets. Current value-chain activities are concentrated around basic ICT services, digital
content, and the gradual digitalization of traditional sectors. While the domestic market remains small, prospective
improvements in connectivity could open new online employment avenues, including AI-training work, content
moderation, data labelling, and other forms of digital gig jobs. Finally, Construction dominates industrial activity in STP
and is structured around a large number of SMEs, with demand driven by housing, tourism, and public infrastructure. An
emerging niche in climate-resilient and resource-efficient construction using local materials has begun to develop. Job
creation potential in the sector is considered moderate but steady, and necessary to sustain infrastructure development.
8.
Connecting youth to job opportunities in these sectors remains a pressing challenge, as many remain outside
employment or training, and are highly vulnerable to economic and climate shocks. STP has a very young population—
40 percent are under the age of 15, and the median age is 19.5.19 Proper investments in education, skills, and job creation
could turn this youth bulge into a productive workforce, driving economic growth and innovation. However, STP is not
currently benefiting from this “demographic dividend.” An estimated 5,000 young people enter the labor market each
year and often struggle to find a job.20 Youth unemployment (among those aged 15 to 24) is estimated at 9 percent,
although this captures only those who are actively seeking work.21 In contrast, nearly half of all youth are not in
employment, education, or training (NEET), indicating that many young people are outside the labor force altogether—
neither working nor looking for work—reflecting widespread inactivity and discouragement.22 A recent survey found that
68 percent of youth are considering leaving the country in search of better opportunities abroad, underscoring the level
of dissatisfaction with prospects at home.23 As recognized in STP’s Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0), young
people also tend to be disproportionately affected by climate change, with implications for their employment
opportunities. Without decisive action, the country risks losing a generation’s potential and entrenching cycles of
poverty, informality, and climate vulnerability.

The scarcity of job opportunities in STP has fueled rising emigration, which is not currently generating many
benefits for the domestic economy or for destination countries. An estimated 18 percent of STP’s population currently
reside abroad.26 Emigration is growing and primarily directed to Portugal, favored for its perceived economic
opportunities, shared language, and simplified visa procedures.27 Border control data suggests that international
departures have increased two-fold compared to pre-COVID levels.28 The most recent wave of emigrants has been
predominantly young and urban, reflecting strong interest among youth (both men and women). However, emigration
has yielded limited economic benefits for STP. Once abroad, many Santomean migrants end up in precarious jobs with
low wages, often below their skill level, which may not be appropriately recognized.29 Remittances are low and irregular,
constrained by unstable earnings and limited access to affordable remittance channels.30 Destination countries also lose
out, as migration occurs in an unstructured way that does not match labor market needs, brings workers without the
right skills or certifications, and limits migrants’ ability to contribute productively to the economy. Neither STP nor key
destination countries are currently capitalizing on the potential benefits of labor mobility. There could be opportunities
for more structured labor mobility approaches, such as through Global Skill Partnerships (GSPs), to combine skills
development in STP with facilitated access to employment opportunities abroad.31

STP’s skills-building ecosystem suffers from limited capacity, infrastructure constraints, and insufficient
alignment with labor demand. Key public Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) institutions include
the São Tomé Professional Training Center in Budo-Budo (CFP BB), the Polytechnic Center for Professional Training Brazil–

broader skills ecosystem is particularly limited: private training provision is small, on-the-job learning is typically informal,
and many youth rely on self-directed learning with no certification or links to employment opportunities.

Support services to facilitate access to jobs or entrepreneurship remain small in scale, weakly institutionalized,
and dependent on external financing. Active labor market programs are rare, with the only recent notable example
being a UNDP-supported internship pilot currently supporting 50 young jobseekers.37 Services for labor intermediation,
career guidance, and job matching within the Ministry of Labor, Solidarity, and Social Security (MLSSS) remain at an early
stage of development. Support to entrepreneurship is also limited, despite the potential of self-employment as a
promising livelihood pathway for youth in a context where private employers are unlikely to absorb all new labor market
entrants. The number of new limited liability companies in STP has declined over the past decade, while access to startup capital is extremely constrained and the digital entrepreneurship ecosystem remains nascent.38 The National Network
of Business Incubators and Accelerators (REINA), launched in 2022 with UNDP support, is a promising initiative but lacks
sustained government financing and institutional support. The vast majority of firms in STP currently operate at low levels
of productivity, limiting earnings, resilience, and their ability to create additional jobs. Better supporting entrepreneurs
could enable MSMEs to become more productive, expand operations, and hire more workers.

C. Project Design
22.
The project design draws on evidence-based, replicable models for improving youth employment outcomes at
scale, adapted to the specific context of STP. Component 1 will strengthen the institutional ecosystem for technical skills
development and public employment services. Component 2 will directly support youth to unlock productive pathways
to wage employment, self-employment, and entrepreneurship. Component 3 will ensure effective project
implementation and learning, while Component 4 will provide a rapid response mechanism in case of crisis. By reinforcing
systems while delivering scalable interventions, the project aims to improve labor market outcomes at scale for youth,
while reducing their vulnerability to economic and climate shocks in a changing global labor market.
23.
Consistent with the SET4Jobs MPA, the project follows three core principles—private sector alignment, sectoral
integration, and operational convergence—to focus investments on priority value chains that are critical for jobs and
economic transformation. The project will create synergies and complementarities across key sectors and institutions to
address binding labor market constraints. Based on country diagnostics, sectoral analysis, and climate assessments, the
project will focus initially on six priority value chains with growth potential and private sector demand, namely (i)
hospitality and tourism; (ii) sustainable fisheries and the blue economy; (iii) agriculture and transformation; (iv) energy
generation and access; (v) digital transformation; and (vi) climate-resilient construction. Labor-intensive sectors such as
tourism, agriculture, and fisheries will offer immediate job absorption potential, while enabling sectors—energy, digital,
and construction—will support broader competitiveness and growth. This list of priority sectors is not exhaustive and
may be adjusted during project implementation to respond to evolving labor demand or emerging opportunities. All
project activities will be implemented in a sector-sensitive manner, ensuring that training curricula, entrepreneurship
support, labor intermediation, and firm-level interventions respond to the specific needs of the selected value chains.

through contractual agreements made with participating institutions, with funding levels proportionate to the number
of students trained in priority skills.

to deliver high-quality, market-relevant training in identified priority skills. Based on preliminary assessments
conducted with each institution, physical investments will cover a combination of (i) procurement of new modern training
equipment and tools; (ii) rehabilitation of existing facilities to improve learning and student support environments,
incorporating climate-resilient design features where feasible; (iii) connectivity upgrades to enable access to digital
technologies; and (iv) acquisition of group vehicles to deliver trainings outside the capital. These investments will be
limited to equipment procurement and rehabilitation within existing institutional footprints, excluding any significant
new structural works or land acquisition. Rehabilitation works will incorporate gender-sensitive design and universal

Subcomponent 1.2: Modernizing Employment Services and Labor Mobility (US$2.45 million)
32.
This subcomponent will modernize employment services and labor mobility systems at the MLSSS to enable
more responsive employment programming aligned with domestic and international labor demand. The project will
strengthen the capacity of the Direction of Labor, Employment, and Professional Training (DTEFP) within the MLSSS to
better understand labor market dynamics and deliver efficient employment promotion services. Specific emphasis will
be placed on understanding demand for emerging professional activities, including those linked to climate adaptation
and mitigation, such as sustainable and climate-resilient building techniques, renewable energy production, and climatesmart agriculture and food production. Although some tools and processes already exist, they are currently fragmented,
underutilized, and poorly interconnected. For instance, an “Employment Observatory” was previously developed with
support from the International Labour Organization (ILO) but never fully operationalized, and a digital “Employability
Portal” linking jobseekers with employers was conceived but never launched. The absence of an integrated system at
DTEFP has limited the GoSTP’s ability to generate actionable labor market intelligence and tailor its programs accordingly.
33.
The project will help redesign and operationalize an integrated labor market information and employment
services architecture tailored to STP’s institutional capacity and scale. Activities will start with a comprehensive mapping
and assessment of existing tools, processes, and data flows within DTEFP, to guide the design and implementation of a
new information and data management system that connects analytical functions, employment services, and program
monitoring. This will involve revamping the Employment Observatory—including by incorporating relevant information
and statistics from relevant overseas labor markets, especially Portugal—and strengthening staff capacity to collect and
analyze labor market data. Over time, the new system will seek to link with other administrative databases—such as the
social protection management information system (Ké-Non), which hosts the USR, or the social security database—to
improve coordination across programs, support pathways from vulnerability to employment, enhance targeting, and
strengthen monitoring of labor market transitions. To ensure that systems continue to function during climate events,
specifically intense rainfall and floods which may disrupt electricity access, the project will support the purchase of power
generators and the creation of a backup system. The Employment Observatory will be designed as a cloud-based system.
34.
To improve job matching and reduce labor market information frictions, the project will finance the
operationalization of a digital Employability Portal. The portal will allow jobseekers to create profiles, browse
employment opportunities, and access various career development resources. All project beneficiaries will be
encouraged to register, so as to ease their transition into jobs. Employers will be incentivized to publish existing vacancies
and recruit through the platform, including by requiring its use for participation in the Youth Internship Program. To
encourage structured labor mobility pathways, DTEFP will explore partnerships with relevant overseas employers to
advertise opportunities for seasonal or permanent positions. Data generated through the portal will feed into the new
labor market information system and help track placements, identify demand trends, and inform policy decisions. Design
of the portal will draw on lessons learned from similar platforms in other Small Island Developing States (SIDS),
emphasizing accessibility and simplicity.50 The project will also explore opportunities to leverage new AI technologies
and/or to partner with a private company to adapt existing job matching solutions to the STP context, if feasible.

Component 2: Pathways to More and Better Jobs (US$8.90 million)
36.
This component will finance a menu of interventions to create more and better job opportunities for youth by
facilitating transitions into wage employment, self-employment, and enterprise growth. Key interventions include: (i)
youth internships to support transitions into wage employment; (ii) self-employment support packages combining
training, grants, and mentoring; (iii) productivity and incubation support for MSMEs; and (iv) inclusion scholarships to
enable disadvantaged youth to access training and job opportunities. Together, these interventions will offer integrated
pathways to improve access to jobs for youth, raise productivity and earnings, and support enterprise growth in priority
sectors aligned with STP’s development strategy.
37.
First, the project will support training-to-work transitions by offering structured internship opportunities that
enable youth to gain practical experience and connect with employers. Limited work experience remains one of the
main barriers facing recent graduates in STP, constraining their ability to secure employment even in sectors where
demand exists. In 2024, the GoSTP launched a pilot Youth Internship Program in partnership with UNDP, providing sixmonth placements in public and private companies, accompanied by a modest stipend. The pilot is currently in its second
phase of implementation, with 50 interns placed. Following the 2025 ministerial restructuring, the MLSSS assumed
responsibility for the initiative, but currently lacks the financial and institutional resources to sustain and scale it.
38.
This first intervention will institutionalize and expand the existing youth internship pilot by strengthening its
design, scale, and sustainability. The project will increase the number of participating employers, improve selection and
matching processes through the Employability Portal, and formally anchor the scheme within the MLSSS’s employment
promotion mandate. Internship placements will prioritize firms operating in emerging sectors linked to sustainable
resource management, renewable energy, and climate resilience.51 Interns will receive stipends structured across three
tiers based on education level, set marginally below the minimum wage to avoid labor market distortion and
displacement effects (up to a maximum of US$1,800). Internships will be complemented by job-readiness trainings—
including CV preparation, interview techniques, workplace conduct, and basic labor rights—as well as light-touch career
guidance, leveraging emerging AI technologies when appropriate. Participants will also receive basic information on
overseas employment opportunities, covering potential benefits, associated risks, and legal procedures. Students from
training institutions supported under Component 1 will be encouraged to apply. The program is expected to benefit 1,600
youth (200 per semester, over an implementation period of four years), at least half of which will be women.
39.
Second, the project will support youth in launching and sustaining small businesses through a structured
productive inclusion approach, making self-employment a viable pathway to income generation. Building on lessons
learned from the ACPE pilot program, as well as international best practices in economic inclusion, this intervention will
address the multiple and interlinked constraints that limit youth economic participation. It will target primarily youth
who are unemployed, inactive, or engaged in low-productivity activities but demonstrate readiness and motivation to
pursue self-employment. At least half of participants will be selected from households from the USR—typically
representing the bottom 40 percent of the income distribution and often residing in areas exposed to climate risks. Within
this overall target, the project will apply a minimum quota of 50 percent female participation and prioritize those engaged
in informal or home-based economic activities. The curriculum and delivery model will be adapted to address youth51 E.g., solar power companies, water and sanitation service providers, climate-smart agriculture and agro-processing firms, eco-tourism operators,
or waste management or recycling businesses.

specific barriers—including limited work experience, weak professional networks, and soft-skills gaps—as well as genderspecific constraints such as limited asset ownership, restricted mobility, and concentration in low-return sectors.
40.
This second intervention will deliver a sequenced support package combining micro-entrepreneurship training,
small start-up grants, and post-launch mentoring. The package will include: (i) training in entrepreneurship, business
management, financial literacy, and soft skills critical for self-employment; (ii) technical skills training in one of the project
priority sectors; (iii) start-up capital in the form of a small grant of up US$2,000; (iv) facilitation of market access through
participation in artisanal fairs, collective organization through cooperatives or producer groups, or linkages with
institutional buyers; and (v) mentoring and coaching during the initial establishment of the business. Training activities
will be delivered with support from the centers involved in Component 1, with a standardized module for
entrepreneurship training, followed by technical training based on each institution’s area of expertise. Technical modules
will integrate competencies that contribute to climate change mitigation and adaptation, such as climate-resilient
production techniques, sustainable natural resource management, low-carbon technologies, and environmentally
sustainable business. Total training time will be for 150 to 200 hours over a course of three months, while mentoring will
be provided for at least six months post launch. Dedicated measures to promote the participation of young women will
be integrated as core design features, including targeted outreach through women associations, gender-sensitive
communication campaigns, safe and accessible training locations, tailored content promoting personal initiative and
socio-emotional considerations, and provision of childcare support on-site where feasible. This intervention is expected
to benefit approximately 3,000 youth over the project period (around 750 per year), at least half of which will be women.

41.
Third, the project will support existing micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs) to improve productivity,
expand operations, and create new employment opportunities. This intervention will target primarily youth-led firms
(formal or informal) with potential for upgrading operations and creating more and better jobs. The approach will be
tailored to STP’s small-island context, where the entrepreneurial ecosystem is nascent and scalable innovation is limited,
but where MSMEs offer meaningful opportunities for productivity gains and job creation. The primary focus of the
intervention will be on productivity enhancement and competitiveness, as prerequisites for growth and expansion. For
informal firms, when a transition to formality may be justified by business needs, the program will support legal
registration towards gradual formalization and tax compliance. Priority will be given to enterprises operating in sectors
that support low-carbon development—such as renewable energy and green technologies—as well as climate-resilient
value chains. This intervention is expected to benefit 240 MSMEs over the project period (around 60 per year), at least
half of which will be headed by women. It will promote women’s entrepreneurship by integrating successful women
business leaders as mentors and resource persons in different activities. Participating firms will be encouraged to adopt
climate-resilient and sustainable practices to reduce potential disruptions from physical climate risks.
42.
Building on the experience from other relevant countries,52 this third intervention will offer a package of
support to selected MSMEs that combines business management training, hands-on mentoring, and access to finance
opportunities. The intervention will launch with a screening process to select a cohort of existing MSMEs operating in
priority sectors with identified potential to create more and better jobs, including through climate-resilient and low

of gender-responsive screening criteria. Selected firms will enter a structured twelve-month “Entrepreneurship Booster”

quality standards, client orientation, basic digitalization, and market access; (ii) individualized mentoring to address firmspecific constraints; (iii) periodic on-site visits to monitor progress and support application of improved management
practices; (iv) networking and peer-learning opportunities; and (v) facilitation of access to finance, including support in
preparing business plans, loan applications, and related documentation. Delivery of the Entrepreneurship Booster

F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design
48.
Lessons from past initiatives and country diagnostics have informed the design of the project, emphasizing
evidence-based and scalable interventions anchored in existing institutions. The project builds on emerging experience
from recent programs in STP and other relevant SIDS, rather than introducing entirely new delivery models. For example,
the youth internship intervention expands a pilot initiative already implemented with UNDP support, incorporating
lessons learned from the first two cohorts. Similarly, the self-employment intervention builds on the ACPE productive
inclusion model piloted under the SPRRP, adapting its approach to the specific needs of youth. More broadly, the project
design reflects lessons from country diagnostics highlighting the importance of aligning skills development with private
sector demand, integrating supply- and demand-side interventions, and simultaneously addressing multiple constraints
faced by youth.

III. PROJECT IMPLEMENTATION
A. Institutional and Implementation Arrangements
49.
The project will be implemented by the Directorate of Labor, Employment, and Professional Training (DTEFP)
under the oversight of the MLSSS, with fiduciary and safeguards functions carried out by the Fiduciary Agency for
Project Administration (AFAP). This arrangement—where technical implementation is led by a directorate within a line
ministry and fiduciary management entrusted to AFAP—follows the standard World Bank engagement model in STP and
has proven effective in previous operations. The MLSSS is currently overseeing the SPRRP and already has experience
implementing World Bank–financed activities. AFAP has extensive experience with World Bank operational procedures
and requirements. To ensure coordination and knowledge sharing, DPSSF, which is responsible for implementing the
SPRRP, will be closely engaged during project implementation. To promote integration, coordination, and cost efficiency,
the existing Project Implementation Unit (PIU) currently managing the SPRRP will be restructured and strengthened to
serve as a unified PIU responsible for implementing both projects under the responsibility of the MLSSS. The PIU will be
reinforced with additional consultants possessing the technical expertise required for the new intervention. Under this
arrangement, specialists whose functions are relevant across both operations will support both projects with adjusted
terms of reference, while other roles will remain project-specific. At a minimum, the unified PIU will be strengthened
through the recruitment of two additional senior specialists—including a technical/professional training specialist—and
two additional assistants, including a data/IT assistant. A single PIU Coordinator will oversee implementation of both
operations. The costs of shared positions will be allocated across the two projects. AFAP will provide fiduciary and
safeguards support, including for financial management, procurement, environmental and social risks, gender-based
violence (GBV) and sexual exploitation and abuse–sexual harassment (SEA/SH), and engineering/construction. Finally, a
coordination committee will be established under the authority of the Minister of Labor, comprising the MLSSS Cabinet
Director and the Directors of DTEFP and DPSSF. The committee will provide strategic oversight and ensure coordination
across the two operations. If necessary, it may recommend adjustments to project priority sectors in response to evolving
labor market demand or emerging economic opportunities, without the need for project restructuring.

IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY
A. Technical and Economic Analysis
53.
Technical design: The project design is informed by a robust body of global evidence and academic literature on
effective interventions to promote more and better job opportunities for youth. Rigorous evaluations in developing
country contexts show that targeted youth employment and entrepreneurship programs combining skills training, access
to finance, and mentoring can lead to durable gains in employment and earnings, particularly when interventions are
well-targeted and bundled.56 Specifically, productive economic inclusion programs that combine cash grants with
training, coaching, and access to markets have been shown to produce large and sustained increases in income,
consumption, and business assets.57 Entrepreneurship support programs that provide business development training,
mentoring, and competitive start-up capital to MSMEs have been demonstrated to improve business survival and income
generation,58 while market-responsive vocational training and internship programs have been associated with improved
labor market insertion and job quality.59 By promoting both self- and wage employment, the project is expected to
generate higher earnings for youth, greater productivity for MSMEs, and stronger institutional capacity for job creation.
Program costs primarily consist of delivering trainings and incubation services, providing grants and scholarships,
strengthening digital and institutional systems, and supporting project management and monitoring. Global evidence
suggests that project activities, if well targeted and sustained over time, can yield benefits far exceeding program costs.
54.
Economic analysis: The project is expected to generate positive and significant net economic benefits. The analysis
quantifies costs and benefits across components using the latest available household survey, the USR, and administrative
data, and applies standard cost-benefit analysis methods, including net present value (NPV), benefit-cost ratios (BCR),
and economic rate of return (ERR) estimates using a 5 percent discount rate. Component 1 costs include training
infrastructure, training delivery, scholarships (included here considering their connection with the trainings), labor
market information systems, and a proportional share of project management costs. Benefits are estimated from
increased earnings in wage and self-employment among youth who would not have received training without the project,
assuming that half of beneficiaries transition toward wage employment, with conservative employment success rates
and wages aligned with minimum wage levels, while the other half pursue self-employment, with low-end assumptions
on business survival and income growth. This yields an estimated NPV of US$2.3 million, BCR of 1.24, and ERR of 19.1
percent. Component 2 covers internships, self-employment support, and MSME productivity programs, with costs
including stipends, training, mentoring, grants, and operational expenses. Benefits are derived from short-term
internship earnings and subsequent employment transitions from a conservative share of participants, increased
earnings from new micro-businesses supported through the self-employment package, and job creation in supported

Environmental, Social and Legal Operational Policies
58.
Environmental & Social Framework (ESF): The environmental risk rating for this project is assessed as moderate,
based on the scope and nature of planned activities and the environmental context of the country. STP is notable for its
rich biodiversity, coastal ecosystems, and vulnerability to climate hazards, including flooding, erosion, and landslides. It
is also the first country with its entire territory designated as a UNESCO biosphere reserve. Project activities consist of
professional training, business incubation, minor rehabilitation of existing training facilities within their current footprint
in São Tomé, the construction of a new training center in Príncipe, equipment procurement, and digital system
development, with no major civil works anticipated. The environmental risk profile of the project is characterized by
predictable, site-specific, and reversible risks associated with minor rehabilitation works, limited construction activities,
equipment procurement, and support to micro-enterprises in a sensitive island environment. Project activities may result
in the generation of solid waste, obsolete equipment, and minor construction debris. While the project does not directly
affect natural habitats, small business grants could support micro-enterprises in agriculture, fisheries, or tourism that
may indirectly place pressure on sensitive ecosystems or cause localized pollution if not properly managed. The identified
environmental risks are considered manageable through standard mitigation measures, including environmental
screening, good construction practices, waste management, and capacity building for oversight. The project’s emphasis
on capacity building and the absence of major civil works further reduce the likelihood of significant adverse impacts.
59.
The project’s social risk rating is also assessed as moderate, reflecting the manageable nature of anticipated risks.
Key social risks identified relate to: (i) risks of exclusion, which may arise if project benefits are not equitably distributed
or if barriers to participation persist, particularly for marginalized groups—including low-income households, women,
persons with disabilities, youth living outside the capital in remote or underserved areas, and individuals not registered
in existing social protection databases—as well as risks of elite capture in the selection of beneficiaries; and (ii)
community health and safety risks, including risks of sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) and sexual harassment (SH),
particularly for young women participating in training and work placements, some of whom may be in vulnerable
situations and exposed to risks of exploitation in exchange for access to project benefits. Additional risks include accidents
affecting local populations due to the movement of vehicles, as well as temporary access restrictions during rehabilitation
works. The magnitude and intensity of these social risks are not expected to be significant and are consistent with the
moderate social risk rating assigned to this operation.
60.
Paris Alignment: The Ministry of Infrastructure and Natural Resources reaffirmed its commitment to the 2015
Paris Agreement (PA) by submitting STP’s second national climate pledge to the United Nations Framework Convention
on Climate Change (UNFCCC) on July 30, 2021. The project fully aligns with the PA’s objectives for both climate mitigation
and adaptation by incorporating targeted measures to lower greenhouse gas emissions and advancing climate-resilient
economic activities. These include promoting climate-resilient livelihoods across Components 1 and 2, while integrating
climate considerations into skills development and employment pathways. Project interventions address STP’s climate
risks and vulnerabilities by strengthening institutional capacity, supporting climate-resilient infrastructure investments,
and equipping youth with relevant skills. In particular, the project will build human capital in areas such as renewable
energy, climate-smart agriculture, sustainable fisheries and coastal management, and resource-efficient construction,

thereby contributing to a greener, more resilient, and inclusive economy. Overall, the project is fully aligned with the
PA’s objectives and supports STP’s transition towards a more climate-resilient and sustainable development pathway.
61.
Assessment of adaptation risks: The project is aligned with STP’s NAPA61 and NDC62 by reducing climate related
risks, increasing the resilience of communities, and mitigating its carbon footprint. The NAPA highlights the dependency
of STP’s economy on agriculture, fishery, and tourism, making its development pathway highly vulnerable to climate
change without appropriate adaptation. Key climate and disaster risks that may affect the project include floods,
droughts, and rising sea levels. While these climate events are anticipated in STP, they are not expected to present
material risks to achieving the PDO. The project’s nationwide coverage means that increased frequency and severity of
natural hazards and climate shocks may cause disruptions in service delivery, potentially leading to short delays in the
implementation of activities in certain areas, such as trainings. Similarly, there may be localized impact on access to
employment, especially for internships and entrepreneurship support. However, given the nationwide coverage and the
phased implementation approach, this is not expected to pose a risk to achieving project objectives. Construction of the
new school in Principe and rehabilitation of the training centers will employ climate-resilient and disaster-resilient design
to reduce climate risks. As such, the project can be considered to be aligned on climate adaptation.
62.
Assessment of mitigation risks: The project focuses on capacity building for youth by promoting access to
employment and enhancing productivity. This focus is expected to increase youth’s resilience to shocks, including
climate-related ones. Capacity building activities include climate-focused activities, such as understanding climate risks
or adopting climate-smart agriculture practices and are aligned with the goals of the PA. Activities to promote access to
employment opportunities (such as internships and entrepreneurship support) are considered universally aligned, and
the project will not consider firms handling only fossil fuels nor involved in non-aligned activities. Construction works for
the new school in Principe, as well as rehabilitation of existing training centers, are considered to pose low risks, with
emissions impact expected to be negligible. In addition, mitigation of GHG-emissions will be maximized through low
carbon input and locally sourced materials, with several energy efficiency features also considered, such as prioritizing
purchase of energy-efficient equipment, such as LED lights, backup (renewable) energy sources, and rainwater collection
systems. In sum, the project is not expected to have a negative impact on STP’s low GHG-emissions development
pathways, and is considered aligned on climate mitigation.

Gender: The project integrates a strong operational gender lens to expand women’s access to higher-quality
employment opportunities and address structural barriers limiting their economic participation. It will broaden
opportunities for vulnerable young women by enhancing their access to market-relevant training, employment services,
entrepreneurship support, and finance. Project design incorporates targeted measures to close gender gaps in workforce
participation by addressing key constraints faced by women, including time poverty related to unpaid care
responsibilities, financial barriers, limited access to networks, occupational segregation, and restrictive social norms.
Outreach and communication strategies will include tailored messaging for women, and leverage women’s associations
and community networks. Gender-responsive enrollment targets will be applied at course level—particularly in higherproductivity and traditionally male-dominated trades—to promote balanced participation and prevent concentration of
women in low-return activities. Training formats will incorporate flexible schedules, predictable timetables, and safe and
accessible learning environments, including gender-sensitive infrastructure (such as adequate sanitation and lighting).
Where feasible, childcare support will be facilitated to reduce participation constraints. Progress will be monitored
through sex-disaggregated, PDO-level indicators in the Results Framework.
