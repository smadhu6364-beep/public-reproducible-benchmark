I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT
A. Project Strategic Context
1.
Accelerating Mexico’s economic growth and job creation calls for raising productivity. Mexico’s recent
development has been anchored in a modern, export-oriented manufacturing sector integrated into global and regional
value chains. Building on its free trade agreement with the United States and Canada, Mexico’s trade in goods and services
exceeds 80 percent of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), mainly conducted by multinational companies and a few large
domestic ﬁrms, largely located in the northern and well-connected central states. The World Bank’s (WB) ﬂagship report
on Mexico’s productivity trends (2022) describes how this modern sector co-exists with a larger domestic economy marked
by low productivity, high informality, and lack of integration into value chains. Bridging the long-standing gap between the
ﬁrms operating in these two segments is a key to boosting Mexico’s growth, creating more, higher-quality jobs, and
reducing poverty.
2.
The Government of México (GoM) launched Plan México in early 2025 to boost growth, job creation, and more
balanced territorial development, including with more energy-eﬃcient industries. The plan aims to boost investment to
above 28 percent of GDP by 2030, create 1.5 million new jobs, and increase access to ﬁnance to at least 30 percent of
small and medium enterprises (SMEs). The new administration also plans to streamline sustainable ﬁnance regulation,
expand renewable energy, and promote industrial parks in strategic locations, while improving environmental, social and
governance (ESG) practices.  A key objective is to boost national content in strategic sectors and Mexico’s value added in
global value chains (GVCs).
3.
The operation aims to support the goals of Plan México by providing long-term financing and technical
assistance – through Nacional Financiera (NAFIN) and participating financial institutions (PFI) – to help midcaps and SMEs
improve their sustainability performance in line with national and global standards, facilitating integration into GVCs. The
Project will create jobs, boost productivity, and accelerate decarbonization in strategic sectors that are also aligned with
the WBG's Job Strategy: infrastructure and energy, agribusiness, healthcare, tourism, and value-added manufacturing. By
creating frameworks and instruments, using Mexico’s Sustainable Taxonomy to guide eligible investments, and linking
preferential terms to measurable sustainability results, the operation advances government and corporate priorities on
competitiveness, private capital mobilization, jobs, regional development, and climate action, while strengthening
disclosure and supply chain resilience across the economy.
4.
The proposed Project design takes advantage of the One-WBG approach and the Knowledge Bank, drawing on
WBG experience in Mexico and other countries. While the Project represents the ﬁrst systematic application of
Sustainability Linked Loan (SLL) and Green Loan (GL) debt instruments in Mexico's ﬁnancial system, it builds on well-tested
frameworks and lessons from previous WBG engagements, including IFC’s recent experience in structuring GLs and SLLs
with anchor companies in strategic sectors in Mexico, and International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD)’s
green ﬁnance operations with development banks in Brazil and Türkiye. The Project reﬂects a coordinated WBG strategy
from the outset, as IBRD and IFC have engaged jointly with NAFIN during preparation, with IFC focused on anchor
companies and IBRD on SMEs and mid-caps. As the market matures and the ﬁnancial instruments become broadly
accepted, the exit strategy envisions a transition from concessional support toward IFC ﬁnancing and MIGA guarantees,
giving NAFIN access to sustainable international capital markets.
B. Sectoral and Institutional Context
5.
Highly vulnerable to climate change, Mexico is committed to moving to a more sustainable development model.
About one-third of Mexico’s population is exposed to severe climate events and natural disasters have caused over US$37
billion in losses since 2000, mostly from hydro-meteorological events. Investments in green and sustainable technologies
can help achieve the goals of the 2024 National Climate Change Strategy, which calls for decarbonizing industry through

export performance of Mexico’s enterprise sector but also support Mexico’s commitment to net-zero emissions by 2050.
6.
Mexico's energy system is at a crossroads: focused on improving resilience yet marked by high dependence on
fossil fuels, exposure to price volatility, and persistent ineﬃciency. Renewable sources account for about 20 percent of
national electricity generation (hydropower 6.2 percent, wind 5.1 percent, solar 6.2 percent, geothermal/other 2.5
percent), with nuclear adding a further 3.1 percent, to bring clean generation to about 23 percent (IEA, 2024). The
remaining share is dependent on fossil fuels, with natural gas accounting for 61.9 percent, oil 7.4 percent, and coal 7.6
percent (the latter accounting for 21.3 percent of emissions from power generation). No new investments in coal-ﬁred
generation have been announced, although coal-ﬁred plants continue to operate (with a declining share) with no
announced closure plans. Industry accounted for 27 percent of ﬁnal energy consumption and 58 percent of ﬁnal electricity
consumption in 2023. At 2.68 megajoules per US dollar of GDP (2023 PPP-adjusted), Mexico's energy intensity is high: the
economy generates little output per unit of energy, reducing competitiveness and increasing emissions. This combination
of fossil fuels and energy intensity means that ﬁrms are exposed to both energy-transition risks and high energy costs.
Investments in renewable generation, energy eﬃciency, and ﬁrm-level sustainability measures are imperative for
competitiveness.
7.
To promote sustainable ﬁnance and facilitate capital mobilization toward socially beneﬁcial investments,
Mexico has launched a Sustainable Taxonomy and a Sustainable Financing Mobilization Strategy. By classifying 124
economic activities and deﬁning minimum sustainability criteria, the Sustainable Taxonomy (2023) provides reliable market
information, mitigates the risk of greenwashing, tracks sustainable ﬁnancing ﬂows, and creates the basis for developing
sustainable ﬁnance policies in Mexico. The WBG partnered with the government on the development of the taxonomy,
sharing global experience and best practices. Mexico’s Ministry of Finance and Public Credit subsequently launched the
Sustainable Finance Mobilization Strategy (2023). This aims to raise more than US$800 billion by 2030 in public and private
capital to finance the transition towards a low-carbon economy. Nevertheless, the market for sustainable ﬁnance
instruments remains nascent and concentrated among large corporations.
8.
Mexico’s integration into GVCs has driven Mexican exports, productivity, and formal employment for three
decades. According to World Bank (2020) estimates, a one percent increase in GVC participation is associated with more
than a 1 percent rise in per-capita income in the long run, underscoring the development impact of international
production linkages. Mexico’s export share of GDP has more than tripled since 1988, reflecting backward linkages in
assembly and manufacturing. However, the domestic value added in Mexico’s exports remains only about 11 percent,
well below the OECD average.
9.
Mexico’s exporters – particularly its multinational firms – face increasing pressure to integrate sustainability
criteria into their supply chains. Global and domestic regulatory and disclosure requirements,1 market opportunities, and
climate transition risk are prompting large anchor companies to assess the environmental performance of their suppliers
and to require them to move to sustainable practices: A 2024 IBRD survey of 206 multinational enterprise affiliates
operating across sectors in Mexico found a strong commitment among firms to green their value chains as part of their
corporate environmental and sustainability strategies. Consultations with several anchor industrial companies carried out
as part of the project’s demand analysis further confirmed strong interest in improving sustainability of supply chains.
More than 50 large companies in Mexico have set Science-Based Target Initiative (SBTi) targets, including commitments
to reduce Scope-3 GHG emissions from their purchased goods and services. Firms that do not comply risk forwardexclusion from supply chains, with implications for sales and employment, while those that do comply can secure longer-

10.
SMEs form the backbone of Mexico’s economy and a significant part of the employment force, but
environmental performance is a barrier to their integration into GVCs.  Defined as companies with 11 to 250 employees,
SMEs account for almost 30 percent of formal employment and a similar share of value added. Their participation in GVCs
is limited, accounting for only 13 percent of value added and 17 percent of employment among GVC-linked manufacturing
firms. Meanwhile, anchor companies increasingly expect local suppliers to adopt sustainability practices, including
renewable energy use, circular economy models, and emissions monitoring, and many SMEs are not prepared for such
requirements. The National Survey on Business Financing (ENAFIN 2024) reports that while 64 percent of firms
implemented at least one sustainability measure in the last three years, most of these actions were incremental (e.g.,
energy or water savings), and only 10 percent reported using renewable energy. Yet SMEs account for 30 to 40 percent of
industrial GHG emissions, driven by energy consumption, manufacturing processes, and transportation.
11.
Mid-cap companies play a crucial bridging role in advancing sustainable value chains.2 These companies often
serve as suppliers to multinational firms and, in many cases, may act as anchor companies themselves by sourcing inputs
from SME suppliers. Owing to their larger size, mid-caps face greater scrutiny of their sustainability practices than do
SMEs, creating stronger incentives in mid-caps to invest in clean technology and energy efficiency. The scale of potential
improvement in mid-cap performance is large, especially once Scope-3 emissions from their suppliers are factored in.
12.
Investing in energy efficiency and sustainable practices is a growth opportunity for Mexican mid-caps and SMEs.
If Mexico were to match the European Union’s emission intensity, emissions could drop from 22.3 to 14.3 kg CO₂ per USD
of GDP (2022 PPP terms), a reduction of 36 percent. Such savings could drive broader economic gains. Emissions in
Mexican manufacturing are concentrated in a small number of firms: targeted improvements could deliver substantial
aggregate emissions reductions. Through energy audits, replacing outdated machinery, deploying energy-management
systems, battery storage, waste heat recovery, and distributed renewable energy solutions, firms could reduce both costs
and emissions. Water-saving technologies and circular economy practices could also make value chains more sustainable.
Similarly, fleet electrification, alternative fuel use, and digital monitoring tools can lower economy-wide logistics costs.
13.
Lack of access to long-term financing constrains SME capacity to invest in sustainability. Global evidence
indicates that the absence of affordable, long-tenor financing (along with technical verification capacity) represents a key
bottleneck for green technology adoption by firms, particularly SMEs. This is particularly relevant given Mexico’s shallow
financial system, with the bank private-sector credit to GDP ratio of 34.7 percent (2024) well below regional (52.4 percent)
and upper-middle-income countries (142.3 percent) averages. As per WBG Enterprise survey (2023), Bank financing
represents a small fraction of funding sources used by Mexican firms, with only 6.3 percent of working capital and 4.7
percent of investment needs financed by banks, much lower than regional and upper-middle-income averages. Most of
firms’ investment needs are financed internally (79 percent), followed by from suppliers (14 percent). According to the
latest IBRD/IMF Financial Sector Assessment Program (FSAP), a concentrated banking system with conservative risk
appetite, weak competition, short-term funding structures that discourage maturity extension, information asymmetries
linked to informality and weak financial statements, and weak collateral recovery all constrain financial access. Meanwhile,
enterprise surveys indicate that Mexican SMEs are willing to adopt cleaner technologies if financing is available.3 However,
SMEs account for only 18.7 percent of total bank credit in Mexico. SME loans are mostly provided for short-term, working-

14.
Mid-cap firms face financing constraints of a different nature and intensity than SMEs. They have better access
to short-term, working-capital credit than SMEs – owing to their size, capacity, and audited financial statements – yet still
experience financing gaps relative to large corporations in longer-tenor investment finance, including sustainable finance
instruments. According to the WBG Enterprise Survey (2023), 33.9 percent of midcap firms in Mexico report being credit
constrained versus 11.8 percent for LAC region. Moreover, mid-caps often fall into a “missing middle”, being too large for
SME-focused credit programs offered by banks but too small to tap into equity or bond markets. Mexico’s large sustainable
bond market (cumulative issuance above US$86 billion) is dominated by state entities and a small set of large corporates
(mostly banks), with negligible presence of SMEs and midcaps. Similarly, the equity market is shallow (capitalization about
20 percent of GDP in 2024) and highly concentrated among a few firms.
15.
The scarcity of long-term financing for sustainability investments reflects both supply and demand constraints.
On the supply side, Mexican financial institutions are limited in their capacity to design, originate, appraise and monitor
green loans and sustainable finance products. The limited availability of credible MRV systems constrains lenders’ capacity
to evaluate the environmental performance and credit risk of borrowers. Less than 15 percent of Mexican banks offer
dedicated green or sustainable loan products; green lending represents less than 1 percent of their loan books. On the
demand side, the barriers faced by SMEs and mid-caps include lack of awareness about energy/resource efficiency
measures, high perceived payback uncertainty, certification costs, insufficient access to standardized data on emissions
and resource use, and lack of internal knowledge for preparing bankable sustainable-investment projects. Most Mexican
firms lack the capacity to measure emissions, implement energy-use, generation or management systems, and comply
with reporting frameworks, and lack resources to access this expertise from the market.
16.
The government has introduced policies and instruments to promote sustainable finance. The Mexico
Sustainable Taxonomy and the Sustainable Finance Mobilization Strategy provide a classification and implementation
roadmap to orient sustainable financial flows. The government has also released the National Development Financing
Program (PRONAFIDE) 2025–2030, which reinforces the role of public development banks in longer-tenor financing,
crowding in private capital, and progressively aligning their operations with taxonomy-based and climate-consistent
criteria. Mexico’s financial sector regulators have elevated disclosure and risk management requirements, enhancing
transparency for issuers (including anchor companies) and investors, supporting the pipeline of bankable green assets.
17.
NAFIN can play a critical role, bridging financing gaps and greening value chains. NAFIN is well positioned to
address systemic barriers to sustainable finance market as it can mobilize long-term financing from donors and capital
markets, absorb higher risks, design and pilot innovative products, and offer capacity building and technical assistance to
private lenders and firms. As the country’s largest development bank, with a mandate to support SMEs (mostly through
second-tier operations) and larger firms (through first-tier lending), 4 NAFIN has been a pioneer of sustainable finance in
the region, the first issuer of a green bond in Latin America (2015). However, while NAFIN has piloted donor-funded EE

its credit portfolio. Most of NAFIN’s sustainable finance programs are donor-funded and offered to SMEs through PFIs,
with a mixed record of take-up. NAFIN’s first-tier operations are limited to a few dozen large borrowers, leaving the midcap
segment unattended by both first and second tiers. NAFIN’s current strategy therefore focuses on scaling up financial and

Mexico faces significant climate vulnerability due to its exposure to extreme weather events such as heatwaves,
droughts, hurricanes, and floods, which disproportionately impact its southern states and marginalized populations.
For SMEs and mid-caps, these climate risks translate into increased operational costs, reduced productivity, and
heightened financial distress, particularly in sectors like agriculture, retail, and services that rely heavily on local demand
and are less equipped to adapt. Extreme temperatures have been shown to raise loan delinquency rates among Mexican
firms, as liquidity shortages and limited access to credit make it difficult for these firms to weather shocks and invest in
resilience measures.
20.
According to the WBG report Advancing Women's Financial Inclusion in Mexico (2024), Mexico has a 14 percent
gender gap in financial access, one of the largest in Latin America and more than double the regional average of 6.8
percent. According to the National Survey of Enterprise Financing (ENAFIN) 2024, women-led businesses face persistent
barriers to financing: only 46 percent have ever sought credit compared to 50 percent of firms nationally, and just 42
percent have successfully accessed it, against a 46 percent national average. Many women entrepreneurs underestimate
their financing needs or avoid formal credit because of distrust and limited information. These challenges are intensified
by low financial literacy, restricted networks, and gender norms that position men as financial decision-makers. Greater
economic participation by women can accelerate Mexico’s development, given their significant role in small, and medium
enterprises. Furthermore, research indicates that women entrepreneurs are more focused on social and environmental
goals than men, often prioritizing social objectives and engaging in sustainable business practices.5 Therefore,
empowering women-led businesses would increase earnings, sustainability and boost creation of more jobs, thus
improving social and economic outcomes in the country.

Subcomponent 1a: First-tier line of credit to improve the sustainability of BEs (US$79.80 million IBRD,

NAFIN will require that every BE applying for an SLL prepares and adopts a credible Sustainability Plan (SP) on a
company-wide basis with material and measurable Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and ambitious, relevant and
timebound sustainability performance targets (SPTs).  The plans would seek to capture both the company-level
Scope 1 and 2 emissions, as well as, where applicable and measurable, Scope 3 emissions originating from a midcap’s value chain. For companies which currently do not have sustainability plans with KPIs and SPTs, the
preparation of required documentation will be supported under the TA component. To mitigate the risk of
greenwashing, targets and results in the SP will be subject to, respectively, ex-ante validation and ex-post
verification by a qualified, independent validation entity.
NAFIN will develop an incentive framework linking SLLs’ financing terms to improvements in borrower’s
sustainability performance. Considering that this will be a first systematic deployment of SLLs in the Mexican
financial system, NAFIN will offer a step-down pricing incentive to SLL borrowers subject to their compliance with
commitments recorded in Sustainability Plans. In line with the established global practice, the SLLs will be
considered general purpose corporate loan financing corporate expenses (all or a portion of the expenditures
incurred by a BE for investments, goods, works, non-consulting services, consulting services, and/or working
capital), with eligible BEs retaining flexibility in the use of proceeds, while being held accountable for achieving
verified sustainability outcomes.7 The pricing incentives offered under the Project will be limited in scale and timebound. Given that the SLLs will be a new product for NAFIN, particular focus will be given to developing the
detailed framework for operationalizing this instrument in line with industry standards. Support for BEs would
include resources– particularly in hard-to-abate sectors – to lower the cost of capital by creating incentives to
increase the sustainability of planned subprojects. Activities would also seek to support – amongst others – the
preparation of credible SP, with KPIs and SPTs, compliant with sustainable finance principles and Mexico’s
Sustainable Taxonomy. At the initial piloting stage during the Project implementation, the ex-ante validation and
ex-post verification costs of SLLs will be covered by NAFIN with ESMAP-DSNZ funds and funded by resources
provided through the World Bank; after the piloting stage, this cost is expected to be shifted to the borrowers.
NAFIN will use blended IBRD-CCEFCF resources to extend SLLs to eligible BEs. For all SLLs, it will be required that
(i) a GHG reduction target is included as one of KPIs, with a corresponding SPT in the BEs’ Sustainability Plans; and
(ii) at least 50% of the GHG verified reduction at the portfolio level comes from energy-related measures that
reduce emissions. Compliance will be monitored through reports prepared by independent verification parties
that evaluate achievement of SPTs. NAFIN would report results to the World Bank at the aggregate level (overall
SLL portfolio). This will also apply to second-tier lending.

A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis
43.
The Project is aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement on both mitigation and adaptation. The Project is
expected to bring significant economic, financial, environmental, technical, and social benefits. By supporting new
financial instruments to adopt commercially proven energy efficient technologies, renewable and low-carbon energy
sources, and other sustainability measures aligned with Mexico’s Sustainable Taxonomy, mid-caps and SMEs supported
under the Project will become more resource-efficient, reducing waste and optimizing the use of materials, energy, and
water, and therefore reducing their costs and improving competitiveness. Improvements will result in more reliable and
efficient operations, increasing productivity and creating jobs. Improvements in firms’ sustainability performance will
facilitate their access to larger global markets through green value chains. BEs will also benefit from increased access to
finance to help lower the typically high upfront capital costs associated with green investments, and from robust technical
assistance and capacity building.
44.
The Project supports Mexico’s NDC 3.0 and the National Climate Change Strategy in both their adaptation and
mitigation goals, as well as Mexico’s net-zero goals announced at COP22. Mexico’s NDC includes an unconditional
mitigation target of 35 percent by 2030, to reach gross emissions of 644 MtCO2e by that year, as well as an unconditional
net emissions target of 364 to 404 MtCO2e by 2035 and a conditional target of 332 to 363 MtCO2e by that same year. As
the Project will seek to support the integration of mid-caps and SMEs into green value chains, it will support sustainable
investments that reduce emissions and increase renewable energy generation. In addition, the operation supports the
inclusion of resilience considerations into SMEs and mid-caps investments and supports the financing of technologies or
interventions to meet resilience and sustainability standards, in alignment with the NDC’s adaptation goals of increasing
circular economy efforts, resource efficiency and promoting ecotechnologies.
(a) Assessment and reduction of mitigation risks. The Project has a low risk of having a negative impact on the
country’s low-GHG-emissions pathway. The Project will finance SMEs’ and mid-caps’ efforts to reduce
emissions, become more sustainable, transition to resilient, reliable and affordable energy and other
sustainable practices. Given this focus, it is expected to decrease GHG emissions and be universally aligned.
Risk management is embedded by requiring that every BE applying for an SLL adopt a credible sustainability
plan that focuses on GHG emissions reduction and efficient use of energy and other resources; and ex-ante
validation and ex-post verification of results will be conducted by qualified, independent validation entities.
In the case of green loans, the Project will finance firms’ specific investments defined in a positive list in the
POM and aim at lowering BEs’ carbon footprint and improving sustainability performance.
(b) Assessment and reduction of adaptation risks. As Mexico faces hazards such as floods, hurricanes, and
droughts and chronic stresses from rising temperatures and shifting precipitation and seasonal patterns, BEs
could be slightly impacted by heat‑related productivity losses, water scarcity or energy‑supply disruptions.
However, investments to be made – as well as the existing portfolio – shows low exposure to such climate
hazards and carbon lock-in risks and are not expected to face physical asset risks. Furthermore, NAFIN has
already implemented measures to manage climate-related risks, including green finance tools and climate risk
