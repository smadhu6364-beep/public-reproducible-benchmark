I.
STRATEGIC CONTEXT
A. Project Strategic Context
1.
While Jordan has demonstrated resilience to various external shocks, economic growth continues to fall short of
its potential, and job creation remains a pressing challenge. Over the past two decades, the private sector has not
expanded its job creation at a pace sufficient to match the rapid population growth, resulting in a shortage of highproductivity employment opportunities. Female labor force participation rates remain among the lowest in the world.
Additionally, Jordan’s small, open economy continues to face vulnerabilities tied to its position in a geopolitically volatile
region.
2.
Breaking free from its low-growth trajectory and fostering job creation requires Jordan to attract high-value
private investment that enhances productivity, drives innovation and export-led growth. The country's export portfolio
remains vulnerable, constrained by limited diversification and insufficient integration into global value chains. Jordan
boasts a highly educated and skilled talent pool, particularly in fields like technology and engineering (8,000 Information
and Communications Technology (ICT) graduates per year; 1 in 5 university graduates hold ICT and engineering degrees).
However, the country faces a significant challenge with high unemployment, especially among youth and recent
graduates. In this context, the rapid growth of the digital economy globally and in Jordan has presented a remarkable
opportunity to create jobs and attract foreign direct investments. For example, between 2013 and 2023, the ICT sector in
27 OECD countries expanded by an average of 6.3 percent annually, approximately three times faster than overall
economic growth. The Jordan Country Private Sector Diagnostic 2.0 (forthcoming) estimated more than tripling of annual
exports (from US$0.5 billion to US$1.8 billion) and doubling of jobs (from 100,000 to 200,000) in IT and Engineering
services sector in the next ten years subject to addressing relevant policy and regulatory constraints.
3.
According to the Global Entrepreneurship Monitor Jordan National Report 2024/2025, approximately 21 percent
of adults in Jordan are engaged in starting or running a new business, up from 16 percent in 2023. While this upward
trend is encouraging, challenges persist. Factors such as social norms, legislative barriers, and limited access to finance
continue to influence participation in entrepreneurship in Jordan. Addressing these issues is crucial for fostering a more
inclusive entrepreneurial ecosystem. The Global Entrepreneurship Monitor Jordan National Report 2024/2025 indicates
that a gender gap exists, for example, measured by the ratio of female to male participation in entrepreneurship or the
established business ownership rate, but this gap has improved over recent years.1
4.
Fostering entrepreneurship and easing access to finance are expected to create opportunities for innovation and
job creation, productively absorbing a talented workforce, particularly youth and women. Jordan made strides in
promoting entrepreneurship and innovation ecosystems in recent years, with major initiatives to support startups, digital
transformation, and integrating technology into key sectors—scaling these efforts will be crucial. Access to finance
remains a hurdle – the 2023 Financial Sector Assessment Program by the International Monetary Fund and World Bank
underscored challenges such as underdeveloped financial instruments and reliance on traditional banking system with a
subdued credit growth. A key focus of the proposed Innovative Startups and SMEs Fund (ISSF) 2.0 Project is to unlock
equity funding for high-potential sectors and startups; this is vital for diversifying the economy, increasing exports, and
creating jobs.

B. Sectoral and Institutional Context
State of the early-stage financing ecosystem
5.
MENA has been among the fastest growing venture capital (VC) ecosystems globally but has yet to attain the
reach and economic impacts of mature ecosystems in the US and other regions. Jordan compares favorably to the
regional average, with 0.5 percent GDP in VC funding during 2018–2022, making it the fourth most funded market among
Middle East and North Africa emerging countries reported in MagniTT (US$246 million) 2; this also reflects government
support in facilitating an entrepreneurial ecosystem. Jordan was the first ignition of regional startup ecosystem with first
tech startup exits to global IT companies; and being a regional hub for tech multinationals. The regional VC market is
expected to continue a growth trend over the next decade and beyond.
Sectoral challenges tackled under the proposed Project
6.
Jordan’s venture capital market has significant potential for deepening if the already substantial impact of the
ISSF 1.0 Project (ISSF 1.0) is leveraged. Jordan has witnessed a significant evolution in its entrepreneurship support
ecosystem, driven by a collaborative effort from the government, international donors, and private sector initiatives. This
has catalyzed the establishment of accelerators and incubators, providing mentorship, workspace, and equity and grant
funding opportunities. With a now well-established business development support ecosystem that serves startups and
supports deal flow creation, the key sectoral challenge that this proposed Project seeks to address is the limited availability
of early-stage financing for innovative startups and small and medium enterprises in Jordan.
7.
Availability of long-term funding and market size in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, US and other
markets prompt high-growth Jordanian startups to expand elsewhere, but core operations often remain in Jordan due
to domestic advantages.  While the US continues to play a major role in the VC industry, there has been remarkable
expansion in growth capital and market opportunities in GCC VC ecosystems thanks to government support. This attracted
many high-growth startups to seek expansion there, and particularly from Jordan and neighboring countries in view of the
proximity and cultural affinities.  At the same time, Jordan continues to have advantages when it comes to ICT talent and
software development which create an incentive to retain footprint in the country. Jordan’s startup ecosystem will benefit
from ISSF serving as a strategic partner to GCC-based VC funds to attract their capital to Jordan – even though some
Jordanian startups relocate their headquarters to GCC countries in later growth stages, their core operations often remain
in Jordan.
The rationale for public sector investment
8.
While ISSF 1.0 has made significant strides, the Jordanian startup ecosystem is still in a developmental stage.
There is a clear economic imperative for a continued public support to sustain and accelerate the growth of this ecosystem.
Despite the progress made, many promising Jordanian startups still struggle to secure adequate funding, particularly at
early stages. The proposed ISSF 2.0 Project can help address these persistent funding gaps by providing additional capital
and attracting more international investors to the Jordanian market. Support is crucial for building local capacity in
Jordan's VC industry and maintaining the country's competitive position in the region. As noted, the long-term support
from government underpinned recent growth of VC market in the GCC region, and middle-income MENA countries aiming
to scale-up their innovative industries have followed a similar strategy (such as fund of fund projects for early-stage
funding are implemented in Egypt, Morocco, and Tunisia).
9.
The proposed ISSF 2.0 Project will focus on channeling funding to enable the startup industry to thrive, while
creating the conditions for sustainability in the longer term. The results of the initial ISSF 1.0 in attracting top-tier venture
capital funds that can crowd in resources to invest in Jordan provide a strong rationale for expanding these efforts. The

Project approach to resolve sectoral challenges, within the Government policy framework
10.
The proposed ISSF 2.0 Project is a follow-on project that will scale up early-stage equity funding for
entrepreneurs in Jordan, in line with the objectives and strategies of the GoJ. The proposed ISSF 2.0 Project will support
the replenishment of ISSF investable capital, which will be deployed through commitments to high-impact VC funds that
can catalyze investments into Jordanian startups. Through a co-investment strategy, ISSF, as a strategic partner in the
Jordanian equity market, will make commitments to regional and international VC funds, providing matching capital and
de-risking investments (on commercial non-subsidized terms) which will lead to the deployment of capital into Jordan
startups. ISSF prioritizes sectors that align with national development goals, such as ICT, health-tech, fintech, Greentech,
and that present significant job opportunities for youth and women.
11.
By bridging the financing gap for early-stage startups and strengthening the venture capital ecosystem, ISSF
activities are aligned with government policies to drive innovation, digital transformation, economic growth, and job
creation. This includes the Economic Modernization Vision, the Entrepreneurship Policy 2021-25, Jordan's Artificial
Intelligence Strategy and Implementation Plan 2023-27. Importantly, a National Council for Future Technology has been
established with the main objective to accelerate technological innovation and digital transformation, positioning Jordan
as a regional leader in the future economy. ISSF is in a position to serve as one of the key enablers and success factors for
the Council’s goals, by leveraging ISSF’s financial capacity and hands-on experience in supporting the tech-based startup
ecosystem and capitalizing on ISSF’s knowledge in promoting Jordan as a destination for VC investments. There are also
strong synergies between the proposed ISSF 2.0 Project and other World Bank-funded projects, in particular the Youth,
Technology, and Jobs Project, which, among other impacts, contributes to an expansion of investable startups in the ICT
sector (Figure 1).

Climate vulnerability context of the project
12.
Climate change modelling indicates Jordan’s vulnerability to various climate-related effects and geophysical
hazards. The Jordan 2022 Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR) stresses an expected decrease in total
precipitation, with increased variability in the location, timing, and quantity of rainfall; increased drought occurrence, and
more frequent extreme events. Since project beneficiaries are mostly startups operating in the technology sector, the risk
of climate effects and natural hazards is not expected to have a significant impact on the achievement of the project
development objective (PDO). ISSF investments will support greentech startups that can be a steppingstone for
decarbonizing industrial and service sectors, as well as to enhance resilience against climate-related effects. Greentech is
a broad term that can encompass any technology that is environmentally friendly, including climate tech solutions that
are focused on technologies directly tackling climate change mitigation and adaptation. For example, smart solutions to
enhance climate resilience of agriculture sector (data-driven solutions such as precision farming, AI-powered crop
monitoring, technologies supporting closed-loop farming and smart irrigation systems to optimize water use); renewable
energy (e.g., solar panel efficiency optimization); energy and water efficiency (e.g., Artificial Intelligence (AI)-powered
energy management systems, leak detection). Jordan National Green Taxonomy (currently under development) will be
leveraged in classifying green investments by the financial sector including ISSF.
Funding to innovative startups in Jordan: state of play and results achieved by ISSF 1.0
13.
Jordan’s early-stage funding ecosystem has advanced with ISSF playing a catalytic role (Figure 2). ISSF 1.0 served
as a catalyst in attracting VC funds which might otherwise have not established a local presence to invest in Jordanian
startups. To date, ISSF has deployed around US$44 million of investments, which further mobilized about additional US$65
million directly, and about US$194 million additional capital mobilized in the respective deal total round sizes by third
party investors. This attracted and scaled up 21 recognized VC funds across seed, early, and growth stages. Within ISSF
1.0’s fund of funds portfolio, minority of the funds are purely Jordan funds – the rest now have Jordan as one of their
focus markets, indicating ISSF’s ability to attract new funds to Jordan. Regional fund managers/ VC ecosystem players
consulted during project preparation indicated a high potential impact for the proposed ISSF 2.0 Project given the central
role of ISSF in financing the ecosystem.
14.
ISSF has been the only sizable catalytic provider of venture capital financing to startups in Jordan. Estimates
based on the implementation suggest that 1 Jordanian Dinar (JOD) invested by ISSF attracts at least 2 – 3 JOD of private
capital to Jordan. In addition to its Fund of Funds functions, the ISSF co-invested along with VC funds in 25 Jordanian
startups (US$5.83 million), two of which have generated exits, with an increasing number of startup exits expected in the
coming years from both the Fund of Funds and co-investment components of ISSF 1.0. ISSF has also played a role in
supporting startups through investment readiness and business development services, stimulating the pipeline of
investible startups through incubation and acceleration services, with more than 700 startups benefitting from such
support (see Figure 3 for a summary of results).

16.
ISSF is uniquely positioned in Jordan’s micro- small- and medium enterprises financing ecosystem, addressing
the funding gap in a still underdeveloped venture capital market. Commercial banks primarily focus on lending to
established SMEs with collateral, microfinance institutions cater to micro-businesses with small-ticket loans, and grant
programs support micro- small- and medium enterprises through non-dilutive funding. None of these financial institutions
are able to provide the risk capital that early-stage, high-growth startups require. Additionally, capital markets tend to
serve large corporates, leaving a void in financing for young, innovative businesses. ISSF is the only sizable actor in Jordan
that operates as a VC Funds of Funds with a dedicated focus on catalyzing early-stage venture capital investments in
Jordan.

B.
Theory of Change and PDO Indicators
18.
The project tackles Jordan’s limited early-stage financing, funding needs of innovative startups and SMEs, and
high unemployment among tertiary-educated graduates by deploying capital to mobilize regional and international VC
funds, and co-investing in startups. Economic literature highlights the role of VC in fostering innovation, scaling startups,
and job creation.3 By mobilizing private capital and improving access to funding—especially for greentech, women, and
youth-led startups—the intervention aims to increase firm survival, job creation, and economic growth. Long-term, this
will build a sustainable entrepreneurial ecosystem, enhancing technology diffusion, productivity, and investment cycles
(Lerner, 2010; Bernstein et al., 2016). 4

D.
Project Components
23.
The proposed ISSF 2.0 Project is a follow-on Investment Project Financing (IPF) project aiming to scale up earlystage equity funding. The proposed Project will retain key elements of the design of the ISSF 1.0, while institutionalizing
good practices and lessons that emerged during the implementation period. The proposed Project integrates an updated
design when it comes to key components as well as governance and implementation arrangements. The proposed ISSF
2.0 Project comprises of three components: (i) Fund of Funds investments, including pre-seed and seed investments to
catalyze the pipeline; (ii) Co-investing in startups with lead private investors; and (iii) Project management and

Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design
41.
Lessons learned from ISSF 1.0 that were considered in designing the proposed ISSF 2.0 Project include:
• Sustaining the strengths in ISSF management and technical teams’ capacity. In the initial years of operation,
ISSF faced challenges linked to, for example, high turnover of the team. The issues have been addressed and
ISSF now has a stable team with qualified professionals. This contributed to an increased pace of deployment in
ISSF’s investable capital in recent years, and the GoJ and the ISSF Board have underscored that it is critical to
retain the team of ISSF to ensure strong implementation from day 1 of ISSF 2.0.
• Governance arrangements. ISSF continues to adjust its governance arrangements based on private-sector
practices. This includes institutionalizing good practices in how the ISSF Board and Investment Committee
operate and removing unnecessary layers to ensure a more efficient and focused operation. Specifically, ISSF
institutional capacity had matured enough that the supervisory role played by JLGC as implementing entity in
ISSF 1.0 is deemed unnecessary.
• Project components. The Fund of Funds component, combined with a smaller allocation for co-investment in
startups along with VC funds, proved to be an efficient way to generate impact. The deal flow support is
redesigned to better align with the ISSF mission and needs of the ecosystem, providing resources for pre-seed
and seed investments and phasing out grant-based technical assistance activities which are well covered by
other development partners and the GOJ.
• Operational Due Diligence (ODD). ISSF is integrating the ODD into its ongoing monitoring of portfolio funds to
assess the compliance, governance, and operational efficiency of underlying fund managers, ensuring
adherence to fiduciary responsibilities and alignment with best practices in fund management. It ensures that
underlying funds follow robust operational practices and have the tools and capacity to monitor risks such as
fraud, mismanagement, or regulatory non-compliance. This protects capital and enhances confidence in longterm returns, and as such will remain an integral part of the proposed ISSF 2.0 Project and can be funded from
either of the components.

Fund selection and investment process
66.
ISSF investment selection under the Fund of Funds component will be guided by an updated ISSF Investment
Strategy approved by its Board, and take account of the following considerations:
(a) Sourcing and Selection of VC Funds: (i) Market Scouting: identify potential VC funds aligned with the ISSF Fund of
Fund's investment strategy and objectives; (ii) Due Diligence: assess the VC funds' track record, team expertise,
background checks, reputational checks, investment thesis, and governance structures. It involves Legal and
Corporate due diligence, and Operational Due Diligence of the funds; (iii) Approval Process: present findings to the
ISSF Investment Committee for approval.
(b) Signing Investment Agreements: (i) Negotiation of Terms: ISSF commitments, governance, etc.; (ii) Legal
Documentation: LPAs, Side Letter, and other binding legal contracts.
(c) Capital Commitment: (i) Commitment Allocation: allocate a specified portion of the ISSF's capital to selected VC
fund; (ii) Funding Schedule: to ensure adherence to Jordan mandate and other terms in between capital calls.
(d) Payment of Management Fees: (i) Fee Structure: ISSF pay annual management fees to the VC fund, typically
calculated as a percentage of committed capital, to cover operational expenses.
(e) VC Funds' Investment Process: (i) Deal Sourcing; (ii) Due Diligence: assess startups' market potential, financial
health, and sustainability of the GP; (iii) Investment Decisions.
(f) Monitoring and Support by a VC fund: (i) Portfolio Oversight: VC funds monitor portfolio companies' performance
and provide mentorship, operational guidance, and strategic support; (ii) Follow-On Investments: allocate
additional capital to successful startups in later funding rounds.
(g) Exits and Realization of Returns: (i) Exit Strategy: VC funds execute exits through Initial Public Offerings, mergers
and acquisitions, or secondary sales; (ii) Profit Realization: generate returns from divestments based on startups'
growth and valuation increases.
(h) Distribution of Profits: (i) Waterfall Structure: VC funds distribute profits according to the LPs, prioritizing the
return of capital to investors, management fee recovery, and subsequent profit-sharing; (ii) Carried Interest:
allocate a portion of the profits to VC fund managers as performance incentives.
(i) Reporting and Evaluation: Regular Reporting: VC funds provide periodic updates to the ISSF on portfolio
performance, financial metrics, and market conditions.
