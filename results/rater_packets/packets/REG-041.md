I.
STRATEGIC CONTEXT

A. Project Strategic Context

1.
Bangladesh experienced rapid economic and social progress in recent decades, reaching lower-middle-income
status in 2015. Stable macroeconomic conditions underpinned an average annual real gross domestic product (GDP)
growth of 6.4 percent between 2010 and 2023, and poverty rates declined. However, the pace of GDP growth and poverty
reduction has moderated in the last few years. An interim government was sworn in on August 8, 2024, after the former
Prime Minister resigned amid a mass uprising. Economic activities have been gradually normalizing since the transition,
but significant downside risks and uncertainty remain. Real GDP grew by 4.2 percent in FY24, declining from 5.8 percent
in FY23. On the supply side, slowdown in GDP growth was primarily driven by the industry sector. Growth in the industry
sector declined to 3.5 percent from 8.4 percent, remaining below the decade’s historical average of 9.5 percent due to
energy shortages, import restrictions, and monetary tightening. Services grew by 5.0 percent. Inflation remained
elevated, reaching 9.9 percent in January 2025. Monetary policy has remained tight to bring down inflation. The current
account deficit narrowed in FY24 and in FY25 Q1 as remittance inflows grew following the depreciation of the currency.
The decline in the foreign exchange (FX) reserves stabilized following budget support by development partners in
December 2024. As of January, FX reserves stood at US$19.9 billion (approximately 3.3 months of prospective imports).
The fiscal deficit is estimated to have narrowed to 3.7 percent of GDP in FY24, from 4.6 percent in FY23, primarily due to
a reduction in expenditure despite weak revenue collection. Public debt stood at 36.8 percent of GDP in FY24.
2.
Real GDP growth is projected to decline in FY25. GDP growth would be affected by significant uncertainties due
to the lingering economic impact of the recent political transition. These uncertainties are expected to keep investment
and industrial growth subdued in the short term. Growth is expected to rise gradually, benefiting from critical reforms.
3.
To achieve faster growth and transition to an upper-middle-income country, Bangladesh needs to implement
structural reforms focused on protecting and increasing human capital. There are persistent weaknesses in human
capital investment, protection, and use. The country’s human capital index score is 0.46, which means young people are
not achieving 54 percent of their productivity potential. Eight out of ten workers are in the informal economy with limited
access to services to help boost their livelihoods. As the economy undergoes structural transformation, human capital
across the life cycle will need to improve to help future workers become more productive and employable. Global
evidence highlights how targeted and tailored interventions providing an effective safety net with access to economic
inclusion services can protect and augment the productivity of the future workforce. These reforms, along with building
a competitive business environment, diversifying exports, and ensuring critical financial sector reforms, would strengthen
international competitiveness as Bangladesh prepares for graduation from Least Developed Country status in 2026,
which will reduce concessional financing and preferential market access for its exports.
4.
Bangladesh faces a high level of vulnerability to the effects of climate change. The 2021 Global Climate Risk Index
ranks it as the seventh most affected country between 2000 and 2019,  with high susceptibility to extreme weather events
such as cyclones/storm surges, flash floods, and heavy rainfall. More than 90 million Bangladeshis (56 percent of the
population) are likely to live in ‘high climate exposure areas’ affected by natural hazards caused by climate change.
According to the Bangladesh Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR), recurring flooding affects a greater share
of the population than any other natural hazard, affecting more than 1 million people annually. Once every three to five
years, up to two-thirds of the country is inundated by floods. A major flood in August 2024 affected over 3 million people,
highlighting the urgent need to address these climate risks to support sustainable economic development and prevent
climate-vulnerable populations from being left behind. Evidence from the last decade shows that the parts of Bangladesh
exposed to multiple overlapping shocks have witnessed relatively slower poverty reduction. In the years ahead, climate

B. Sectoral and Institutional Context

5.
Bangladesh’s social protection system follows a life cycle approach to address vulnerabilities and risks across
different demographic groups. Excluding civil service pensions, the Government of Bangladesh (GoB) allocated 1.8
percent of GDP to 139 social protection programs in FY25.1 Analysis of the budget highlights that the majority of programs
remain small with limited coverage, except for the key allowance programs, student stipends, and food programs. Only
11 programs receive one-third of the total social protection financing by the GoB in FY25. Over the past five years,
spending on social assistance programs for poor households accounts for nearly 30 percent of the budget. The largest
social assistance programs include Old Age Allowance (6.2 million beneficiaries), Widow Allowance (2.9 million
beneficiaries), and Disability Allowance (2.9 million beneficiaries), which are implemented by the Department of Social
Services (DSS), Ministry of Social Welfare (MoSW). Social protection spending also remains inadequate across the life
cycle for those without public sector jobs, with program support for the elderly accounting for 11 percent of the budget,
while interventions for the early years receive the smallest share (2 percent) and allocations for programs serving the
youth and/or working-age populations are at 12 percent. Social insurance spending remains the lowest at 0.2 percent,
while financing for labor market programs—which include livelihoods and entrepreneurship support programs, economic
inclusion programs and vocational training —has been increasing (6 percent in FY25).
6.
Social assistance programs—that aim to protect poor households from falling further into poverty through cash
or in-kind transfers—have expanded in the past decade. Overall, as per Household Income and Expenditure Survey
(HIES) data, households benefiting from social assistance programs increased by nearly 13 percentage points between
2010 (24.6 percent) and 2022 (37.6 percent), reaching 14.4 million households. Half of all poor households received social
assistance in 2022, an increase from 23.7 percent coverage rate in 2010 (Atlas of Social Protection Indicators of Resilience
and Equity 2023). The significant increase in beneficiary households in 2022 corresponds to rising social assistance
spending, particularly on cash transfer and food programs, in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. In urban areas, the
share of households receiving benefits from social assistance programs more than doubled from 9.4 percent in 2010 to
23.9 percent in 2022. The share also increased in rural areas from 30.5 percent in 2010 to 44.0 percent in 2022. The
contribution of public transfers to the household budgets of the poor has also expanded in the last decade. The relative
contribution of cash transfers, stipends, and food programs to the per capita household income of those in the bottom
income quintile increased by 10.7 percentage points, from 4.9 percent in 2010 to 15.5 percent in 2022.
7.
The impact of an expansion in social assistance coverage on poverty remains limited due to inadequate benefits
and inefficient targeting. While government reforms to modernize the delivery systems and expand coverage have been
successful, there is immense scope for further scale and innovation. Exclusion and inclusion errors remain a challenge.
Digitized tools for targeting have been tested and are yet to be scaled across all programs. Nearly half (47 percent) of the
poor households are not covered by any social assistance  program (HIES 2022). Further, social protection benefits
received by the bottom 40 percent of the income distribution reduced from 51 percent in 2016 to about 44 percent in

8.
Bangladesh’s future prosperity will require poor households to graduate from social assistance programs
toward self-reliance through tailored livelihoods support. While the country invests in several innovative economic
inclusion and livelihoods programs, these are largely led by nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) or semiautonomous
organizations. Government-led initiatives to address underserved poor communities remain ad hoc with no welldeveloped linkages to safety nets programs that can enable effective graduation for the poor. A graduation strategy that
allows poor households—with a focus on youth and women—to ‘move up the ladder’ is vital for Bangladesh’s labor
market and a fiscally sustainable social protection net. Data from the Bangladesh Labor Force Survey for 2023 find youth
unemployment for ages 15–24 remains high at 16 percent, as well as 37 percent for those with advanced education
degrees. The latest Bangladesh Sample Vital Statistics Report (2023) found 39.8 percent of youth (15–24 years) were not
in education, employment, or training. Female labor force participation remains nearly half of that of men. Given that
the majority of workers are in the informal economy and engaged in microenterprises, the need for augmenting support
for micro-entrepreneurs will be essential to the recovery plan in Bangladesh. Extending credit plus support could allow
affected micro-entrepreneurs to maintain the operational liquidity needed to cover day-to-day expenses, manage
inventory, and seize growth opportunities. Women workers, especially those from female-headed households, face

9.
Recognizing the above challenges, the GoB has initiated several reforms to strengthen coverage, targeting, and
graduation in the national social protection system. These efforts have been guided by the National Social Security
Strategy (NSSS 2015–2026), which lays out a vision for an inclusive social security system. Multiple building blocks have
been created for a stronger delivery chain for programs. At the Finance Division (FD), Ministry of Finance (MoF), the
government has established a Social Protection Budget Management Unit (SPBMU) to coordinate across different line
ministries and implement a digital government-to-person (G2P) payment system that now covers over 35 million
beneficiaries. The system is also designed to verify each beneficiary through their national identity (NID) numbers, which
reduces the scope for leakage and fraud. Past efforts to create a social registry through the National Household Database
(NHD) did not materialize due to incomplete and outdated data, lag in management information system (MIS)
development efforts, early cuts in government funding, and lack of institutional mandate to drive integration across
ministries. However, drawing on these past lessons, the GoB has codified a policy to create a robust interministerial
platform to build a Dynamic Social Registry (DSR) that harnesses recent capacities and policy mandate (through the NSSS)
to coordinate beneficiary data at the FD and DSS. Further, the DSS has implemented successful pilots to use proxy means
data as a building block for more transparent targeting systems. As the country makes its journey toward upper-middleincome status, while contending with various challenges—including demographic shifts, informality, climate-induced or
other shocks, and new technology—deliberations on the new social security strategy for 2026+ have also begun with
MoSW as the nodal agency for implementing the country’s vision for social protection. This momentum offers an
opportunity to reform and rethink the role and design of the national social protection system in a manner that promotes
equity, builds resilience, and increases opportunity for all while strengthening service delivery and financing. The
proposed project is consistent with the World Bank Country Partnership Framework FY23–27 (Report No. 181003-BD),
discussed by the World Bank Board of Executive Directors on April 27, 2023.

To augment the impact of cash transfers, Subcomponent 2.1 will invest in systems to assess benefit adequacy
of programs. The proposed project will provide the DSS with necessary technical assistance and financial resources to
develop and adopt a standardized benefits adequacy framework for periodic review of benefit levels across its cash
transfer programs. Global evidence suggests that social protection benefits generally lose their value over time unless
prevented by index-linking (periodic review mechanism). PBCs under this component will incentivize the establishment
of a rigorous and evidence-based review of benefit levels commensurate with the poverty line at the DSS. The benefit
adequacy framework will be designed to inform future program budgeting of allowance amounts. Targets will incentivize
the DSS to adopt and utilize the framework consistently and submit this analysis to the FD. The following key building
blocks will be financed while developing the framework:
•
Construction of a representative index. This will require economic modeling to assess the fiscal costs and
welfare impact of using options such as Consumer Price Index inflation or HIES data for benefits indexation.
The index also needs to reflect the consumption patterns of the target beneficiaries (that is, calculating a
special inflation rate for low-income households or constructing a customized consumption basket based on
HIES data). Additional consideration of climate vulnerabilities shall be factored for determining adequacy
frameworks for households living in frequently flood-affected Upazilas/locations.
•
Establishing frequency/triggers for benefit levels revision. The framework will outline both cyclical
frequencies (that is, annual, biannual, and so on) for periodic revision of benefit levels and countercyclical
triggers (that is, inflation levels exceeding prespecified thresholds) for benefit levels’ revision that respond
to economic and climate shocks.

interventions combine cash assistance with complementary services or interventions such as behavior change
communication, psychosocial support, visits from social workers, and establishment of peer/community/social clubs. The

to build resilience and have a greater impact on poor and vulnerable populations. The National Adaptation Plan (NAP) of

floods, cyclones, or storm surges) according to the Multi-Hazard Risk and Vulnerability Assessment Modeling
and Mapping. The target beneficiaries for these social clubs will be selected from the Old Age Allowance
recipients, based on their interest in joining the clubs and poverty levels/needs assessment. The specific
procedures for selection of locations and beneficiaries for the social clubs will be specified in the POM. These
social clubs will promote the welfare of selected beneficiaries of the Old Age Allowance by linking individuals
to intergenerational self-help groups, which would engage participants in cultural activities and emergency
counselling, provide self-reliance training (including protocols and recommendations on how to access local
climate risk information, identify and respond to climate hazards), and connect them with physical and
psychosocial care services. A contingency plan for climate shock and natural hazards period will be developed

Component 3: Expanding Livelihoods Programs for the Poor (US$47.18 million, of which IDA US$47 million equivalent)
22.
Component 3 will support the economic empowerment of poor people—including youth, women, injured
persons, and PWDs—by strengthening the DSS livelihoods programs and providing comprehensive and responsive
livelihoods support. Potential beneficiaries from climate-vulnerable locations will be prioritized in eligibility
determination and outreach plans. A key objective of the GoB’s NSSS is to expand the coverage and effectiveness of
economic inclusion initiatives to improve the self-reliance of poor communities by gradually moving them out of poverty
and social assistance programs. Additionally, the government recognizes the need for income generation and
diversification for the vulnerable, especially youth, PWDs, and other disadvantaged groups, as part of Bangladesh’s
national climate change adaptation plan.8 To meet these goals, Component 3 will draw on lessons from successful
economic inclusion programs, including adaptive measures globally and within Bangladesh, to strengthen the DSS
livelihoods program that target poor and vulnerable groups. The DSS currently implements four poverty-targeted
livelihoods programs to serve unique target groups. These include: (a) Rural Social Services for rural poor: (b) Urban
Community Development for urban poor; (c) Rehabilitation Program for Burn Victims and PWDs; and (d) Rural Mothers
Program for reaching rural mothers. As of FY24, these programs cover approximately 2 million beneficiaries with an
outstanding credit amount of BDT 20.1 billion (US$168 million). These programs are part of a single ecosystem managed
under the leadership of the Livelihoods Program Director at the DSS. However, there is limited integration within these
programs in terms of administrative systems, data/information, and resource management.
23.
Beneficiary coverage in the DSS livelihoods programs is low, and the provision of training and complementary
services are ad hoc and limited. Despite targeting by poverty level, inclusion and exclusion errors persist in the DSS
programs, leading to low coverage of poor households. In addition, the socioeconomic impact of the programs remains
limited, stressing the importance of strengthening program design and delivery. According to a 2018 evaluation of the
DSS livelihoods program in rural areas by the Bangladesh Institute of Development Studies, beneficiary households had
higher income (12.5 percent) and consumption (14.3 percent) than non-beneficiary households, 8.4 percent of the
beneficiaries graduated from ultra-poor to non-poor status, and 30.4 percent beneficiaries graduated from poor to nonpoor status. However, the evaluation reports no long-term impact on household savings and asset creation and limited
participation (7 percent of beneficiaries) in skill enhancement trainings. While the livelihoods programs have training
components, they are often ad hoc and limited in coverage and depth. Moreover, they do not currently provide training
on financial literacy, life skills, and socio-emotional skills.

F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design

28.
Past projects supported by the World Bank to strengthen cash transfers and safety nets support in Bangladesh
and other lower-middle-income countries highlight the following key lessons that have been integrated into the design.
First, experience and evidence highlight that cash transfers need complementary services to effectively tackle poverty
and boost human capital. Therefore, by layering multiple interventions with cash transfers/safety nets, economic
inclusion programs can leverage existing delivery systems and support the poorest who are engaged in self-employment
opportunities to diversify incomes and unlock their productive potential (State of Economic Inclusion Report, 2024).
Second, evidence from allowance programs supported by IDA financing highlights that targeting errors and exclusions
continue to constrain the impact of social assistance programs. The CTM project tested the use of PMT to improve
targeting, which has been successful. Lessons on PMT design learned through these pilots will be scaled through the
proposed project activities. Countries that have invested in establishing a DSR have improved efficiency in the intake and
registration process of social protection which led to reduced application time, decreased administrative cost, and more
effective use of resources (Social Protection Delivery Systems Handbook, 2020).
29.
During the first year of implementation, where DSR design is a key result, the World Bank will leverage
international and national lessons on building DSRs across 20+ countries and enhancing economic inclusion services
across 25+ projects. The RAISE project currently offers economic inclusion interventions to low-educated youth in urban
households, and the CTM project is supporting the DSS to implement an economic inclusion pilot targeted toward young
widows and PWDs to build their resilience by improving income-earning opportunities and addressing the
multidimensional barriers they face. Implementation lessons from these projects will be integrated into the design
features of the livelihoods component of the proposed project.

A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis

34.
The project is expected to bring economic benefits to poor populations by (a) improving the capacity of
government agencies and other stakeholders to effectively design and manage a DSR, which will improve targeting
efficiency as well as cost savings for the GoB; (b) improving accountability and access to social assistance programs; and
(c) strengthening livelihood support services that will significantly boost consumption and earnings among vulnerable
populations. The project caters to poor workers and households with limited opportunities to invest in human capital.
Public financing is required as there are insufficient incentives for private provision of these services for
households/workers facing low-income poverty traps. The World Bank can leverage international and national lessons
on building DSRs and implementing economic inclusion programs as highlighted in Section II, F above. The goal is also to
inform national social protection policies by providing an exit pathway out of cash transfer programs. This positions the
World Bank uniquely to extend support to Bangladesh by providing new knowledge; sharing best practices; and providing
technical guidance based on a wealth of country-level and international experience.
35.
Under Component 1, an analysis was conducted to evaluate the potential of the DSR in enhancing pro-poor
targeting in social protection programs in Bangladesh. According to the World Bank Social Registry Global Database, 62

countries have social registries that cover an average of 41 percent of their populations. A sample of 18 registries with

The benefits of DSR-based targeting were further assessed through data from the NHD pilot conducted during
2014–2017 and the DSS-MIS. Based on data from the Old Age Allowance, 10 percent of beneficiaries were below 62 years
and thus ineligible for the cash transfer; 40 percent of beneficiaries were from the lowest two quintiles; and among the
poor elderly living in the lowest two quintiles, 41 percent were in the program. This is in line with a 2013 study by the
International Food Policy Research Institute that reports the share of Old Age Allowance beneficiaries in the bottom two
quintiles as 51 percent. The inclusion and exclusion errors are approximately 60 percent, highlighting the significant
benefit of establishing the DSR to improve the targeting for safety net programs. This recurring saving in the budget
would create fiscal space to further increase the level of coverage. Moreover, improved targeting would contribute to
transparency and trust in program administration.
37.
Given the high levels of fragmentation in social protection programs and the absence of a unified targeting
mechanism in Bangladesh, improving the beneficiary selection process through the DSR can inform targeting in other
safety nets that support poor and vulnerable elderly people, women, and PWDs. As the number of elderly people
increases in the country and fertility rates decline, United Nations estimates show that those ages 65 years and above
are expected to make up 7.1 percent of the population in 2029, increasing significantly from 5 percent in 2015. In
addition, the growing need to focus on women’s economic empowerment, climate-vulnerable populations, and
supporting PWDs is likely to add pressure to expand coverage and increase the benefit levels of safety net programs. By
improving pro-poor targeting across programs, the DSR can play an important role in balancing the coverage and benefit
levels under fiscal constraints.
38.
Cost-benefit analysis. A cost-benefit analysis of the cash-plus program under Component 2 was conducted to
assess the feasibility of the interventions. The cash-plus program targets Old Age Allowance and Disability Allowance
beneficiaries and will provide interventions such as enrollment in social clubs and psychosocial care services through the
DSS and external service providers. While the broader component aims to improve cash transfer adequacy and
institutional capacity, the analysis focuses only on the 300,000 beneficiaries of the cash-plus (social care) interventions.
Evidence from cash transfer programs that target the elderly and PWDs in India and Brazil estimates an increase in the
consumption per capita by 5–10 percent. Using the lower-bound estimate, the net present value of cash transfer benefits
is estimated at US$503 (BDT 60,360) per beneficiary over a five-year period. Under Component 3, a cost-benefit analysis
was conducted for the segment of vulnerable women, injured persons, and PWDs who will receive productive grants in
addition to training on technical skills, financial literacy and life skills, training stipends, and complementary services
including linkages to mentorship, markets, and others, based on their needs. The cost per beneficiary is estimated at
US$671 (BDT 80,520). Existing evidence from economic inclusion programs in Bangladesh, India, Ethiopia, and Niger
demonstrate an increase in consumption per capita between 15 to 20 percent a year after the program ends. Using the
lower-bound estimate, the consumption per capita is expected to increase in year 3 from its baseline value (consumption
per capita of households in the bottom 40 percent with widows and PWDs from HIES) and stabilize between years 4 and
10 with a social discount rate of 5 percent. The benefits-cost ratio is 1.71, which is in line with the economic inclusion
programs in Niger and Afghanistan, where the ratio ranged from less than 1 to 2.5, depending on the assumptions. The
benefits are expected to be higher as the analysis does not adequately capture the noneconomic benefits of economic
inclusion programs such as psychosocial gains and women’s empowerment.

Gender. In Bangladesh, women are less likely than men to participate in the labor force (42.5 percent versus 81.3

also support female-headed households with ‘cash-plus’ programs that combine cash transfers with a tailored package
of livelihoods support. The project targets 60 percent of cash-based interventions to women. Furthermore, women report

transfers supported through the proposed project will disburse benefits to eligible female beneficiaries through digital
bank transfers, thereby deepening financial access for women outside the net of formal banking channels.  The project
also enhances women’s access to DSS cash transfer programs and DSR rollout by (a) developing sex-disaggregated
systems that improve policy making, M&E for the DSR, and program monitoring and rollout (b)testing targeted outreach
activities and incentives through Upazila offices and the DSS frontline staff (c) prioritizing women in the application and
grievance redressal processes for cash transfer access and livelihoods programs. One of the first cash transfer programs
being onboarded on the DSR delivers cash benefits to widows and female-headed households, addressing gender gaps
in access to social assistance and ensuring women’s feedback is taken on board effectively and early. The project also
supports the capacities of select Upazilas to reduce time lags in access to information and services experienced by women

The social care interventions supported by this PBC will focus on connecting allowance beneficiaries to elderly / PWD
support services for physical and psychosocial care with a view to enhancing welfare outcomes to have a greater impact
on the welfare of vulnerable elderly and PWDs – a group most affected by the negative impact of climate change – and
contribute to their climate resilience building.  By providing climate-resilience capacity building and comprehensive
support, including mental health services and emergency counseling, these interventions will help build adaptive capacity
and resilience to climate shocks. The establishment of social clubs will further promote community-based support
systems, enabling beneficiaries to engage in activities that enhance their understanding of climate risks and adaptive
strategies
The livelihoods support interventions will focus on providing livelihoods support services (skills training and
complementary services) to DSS allowance beneficiaries with a special focus on vulnerable youth, female headed
households and PWDs. This will enhance their economic resilience and thereby, the capacity to deal with climate induced
shocks without resorting to negative coping strategies. The focus on building skills and providing resources for sustainable
livelihoods will ensure that beneficiaries are better equipped to adapt to and mitigate the impacts of climate change,
thereby strengthening their overall resilience.

PBC 3 will incentivize periodic evidence-based review of benefit levels of DSS cash transfer programs commensurate to the

PBC 4.1 (US$5.2 million) will focus on the approval and adoption of guidelines for implementation of the DSR. The
guideline will incorporate adaptive measures, including responding to extreme climate events (flood, cyclone, storms
surge) by facilitating easier enrolment for those affected by such events through multiple channels for self-enrollment in
climate vulnerable areas.

PBC 4.3 (US$5.2 million) will support DSS to issue and adopt updated cash transfer program guidelines which will enable
the roll-out of cash plus interventions and use of DSR for beneficiary selection. This activity will (i) ensure that DSS has the
ability to efficiently and quickly target and deliver emergency assistance to vulnerable households in climate shock /
disaster affected areas (ii) support establishment of linkages between DSS livelihoods programs and allowance/cash
transfer programs will enable DSS to provide vulnerable households with social care (physical and psychosocial care
services) and livelihoods support (skills training and complementary services), which will help them cope with the impact
of climate change by strengthening their  awareness and economic and climate resilience.

PBC 4.4 (US$5.2 million) will finance training for DSS staff/personnel and frontline cadres on using the new DSR for intake
and proactive outreach. PBC 4.5 (US$5.2 million) will incentivize the presence of access points in each Upazila (sub-district
local unit) for vulnerable communities, particularly those affected by climate change, to share, record and update their
relevant asset data for the DSR. Both PBCs will build the human and digital resources for the DSR to be citizen-centric and
credibly implemented. Together, they will serve as a vital information gateway to tackle disasters and climate shocks, as
impacted communities can update their information and avail benefits from the disaster management machinery and
safety nets. PBC 4.2 (US$5.2 million) will finance process evaluations of the first phase of implementing the DSR, thereby
providing the government an important evidentiary base to scale-up registry efforts and modify required business
processes. The evaluation will focus on lessons from disaster prone districts and areas with severe flooding and climate
risks, thereby providing more data to inform climate-responsive SP delivery systems.
