Economic Opportunities for Resilience Project (P511652)
I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT
A. Project Strategic Context
1.
Burkina Faso households’ economic resilience is constrained by low human capital, limited resources, and
access to economic opportunities, especially for women. The country experiences some of the highest rates of poverty
in the region, with 43.2 percent of the population living in poverty (2021).1 Labor market participation rates are low among
men (53.5 percent) and women (41.0 percent), and 93.5 percent of jobs are classified as informal (95.8 percent among
women),2 with a low human development and capital index.3 Underemployment is particularly high among young people
and women, with large segments neither in employment, education, or training.4 Only 13 percent of men and 7.3 percent
of women have some secondary education among low-income households. Women’s limited access to resources is a key
driver of their inequality, food insecurity, and vulnerability, 5 and gender-based violence (GBV) is widespread and
exacerbated by poverty and climate change.6 Youth also face significant challenges regarding employment opportunities.
2.
A series of compounding shocks affect the resilience of the poor and vulnerable population. The deteriorating
security situation has led to internal displacement, with about 2 million (about 9 percent of the population) internally
displaced persons (IDPs) in 2023.7 While efforts to reclaim national territory facilitated the gradual return of 1.2 million
people as of March 2026, about 200,000 IDPs were newly registered during 2025.8 Recent cuts in humanitarian funding
have reduced support for IDPs. Climate change also severely undermines food security, with 5 out of 10 Burkinabe in rural
areas affected at least once by droughts or irregular rainfall patterns in the previous three years (floods are also
increasingly frequent). 9 Shocks undermine food and income security, forcing households to adopt negative coping
strategies such as skipping meals, selling productive assets, pulling children out of school, or migrating for work. During
the 2024 lean season, about 3 million Burkinabe were food insecure,10 and increased competition over natural resources
generates significant conflict risks.11 Women and girls experience heightened vulnerability to climate change and other
shocks because gender inequalities increase their risk of poverty, resource scarcity, extreme weather, and displacement.
Displacement disproportionately affects vulnerable populations such as women, children, and those living with disability.
3.
The proposed operation supports the implementation of the national social protection strategy and new
flagship program: the Economic Empowerment Support Program for Poor and Vulnerable Households (Programme
d’Appui à l’Autonomisation des Ménages Pauvres et Vulnérables, PAMPV). In particular, this project aims to strengthen

B. Sectoral and Institutional Context
4.
Burkina Faso has launched major reforms to improve the coverage, coordination, and shock-responsiveness of
its social protection system. The country spent 2.6 percent of its gross domestic product on social assistance spending in
2020, exceeding the regional and global average. However, the data are outdated without updated figures, and the impact
on reducing poverty was limited due to fragmented coordination, with more than half of all social assistance beneficiaries
not belonging to the bottom 40 percent of the income distribution.12 To address this situation, the current National Social
Protection Strategy (2024–2028) emphasizes adaptive social protection tools such as the Unique Social Registry (Registre
Social Unique, RSU), the combination of regular and shock-responsive programs, better integration of the needs of IDPs
and returnees, and stronger coordination between humanitarian and long-term assistance. At the heart of the strategy is
the creation of the national flagship program (PAMPV), which focuses on promoting economic inclusion, building human
capital, strengthening social cohesion, and improving targeting. The PAMPV has a goal of reaching 200,000 of the poorest
households by 2029 (about 14 percent of the population).
5.
These reforms build on the achievements made during 2014–2024 with the World Bank-supported Social Safety
Net Project ‘Burkin Naong Sa Ya’ (P124015). Financed by the International Development Association (IDA) and the Sahel
Adaptive Social Protection Program (SASPP) Multi-donor Trust Fund (MDTF), the project delivered cash transfers to over
1 million beneficiaries and helped establish the foundations of an adaptive system. Yet significant challenges remain,
particularly the need to expand the RSU nationwide, better integrate the needs of IDPs, and deepen coordination between
humanitarian and long-term assistance. For example, the RSU currently covers approximately one-quarter of the poor and
vulnerable households in the country, out of the target to register all poor and vulnerable households. Also, the RSU and
IDP data are managed separately, while coordination efforts are under way. The Government’s decision in 2023
to suspend cash transfers and food vouchers led the PAMPV to focus on in-kind support, economic inclusion, human
capital, social cohesion, and strengthened targeting.
6.
In parallel, the Government has been accelerating efforts to strengthen resilience of IDPs, while enhancing its
overall ability to respond to shocks and emergencies. This evolution comes against a backdrop of a significant decline in
humanitarian assistance and funding since early 2025, prompting increased national ownership to address critical gaps. It
further underscores the Government’s commitment to improving the coherence, complementarity, and sustainability of
interventions through the operationalization of the Humanitarian-Development-Peace (HDP) triple nexus, linking
emergency response with recovery, resilience, and peace consolidation efforts, as illustrated by the Council of Ministers’
approval of the National Council for Emergency Relief and Rehabilitation (Conseil National de Secours d’Urgence et de
Réhabilitation, CONASUR) project in November 2025. The objective of the CONASUR project in the amount of CFAF 51
billion (about US$90 million) is to reinforce institutional and logistical capacity in the CONASUR to prevent and manage
disasters and humanitarian crises through purchasing vehicles and constructing warehouses.
7.

multiple government actors beyond the ministry in charge of social protection. Core governmental actors include (a) the
Permanent Secretariat of the National Council for Social Protection (Secrétariat Permanent du Conseil National pour la
Protection Sociale, SP-CNPS), which reports to the Prime Minister’s Office and is responsible for coordinating social

facilitate the issuance of Burkinabe National Identity Cards (Carte Nationale d’Identité Burkinabè) and support the care of
survivors of GBV and child victims of violence; and (f) the ministry in charge of health, which will provide
training/awareness raising related to health and nutrition for beneficiaries.
9.
The proposed design and activities fully leverage synergies and collaboration with other sectors, including
infrastructure, agriculture, health, private sector, and digital development. Reflecting the intersectoral nature of social
protection and labor and recognizing that poverty reduction and economic empowerment require a multisectoral
approach, the project has been and will be closely coordinated with other sectors. For example, economic inclusion, which
refers to a package of programs and services that aim to increase the resilience of people and communities, and agriculture
activities and extension services constitute an important pillar to improve beneficiaries’ livelihoods in rural areas. Also,
supporting livelihoods (including nonfarming) entails close collaborations with private sectors. Accompanying measures
to promote human capital and social cohesion will be implemented with partners working in health and local
development. Furthermore, a unique ID supported under the West Africa Unique Identification for Regional Integration
and Inclusion (WURI, P161329) project is a prerequisite to realize an integrated system envisioned under this project.
10.
The project fully aligns with the World Bank strategy for Burkina Faso, which aims to promote sustained
development, with a strong focus on creating more and better jobs. As a main component of the project, economic
empowerment will directly contribute to Burkina Faso’s new Country Partnership Framework13 (CPF, FY2026–2031), as
proposed activities focus on enhancing productive opportunities for poor and vulnerable populations, including expanding
their skills and IGAs in the agricultural sector. The operation will support improved household well-being and productivity
through behavior change interventions, thereby contributing to better education and health. Importantly, the adaptative
social protection system—a foundational infrastructure—will improve target efficiency of public resources beyond social
protection programs, including other services such as health, education, agriculture, and energy, as well as shock
responses. Furthermore, the project will support the MFS’s capacity to operate emergency food assistance, which is
instrumental in preserving food and nutrition security in fragile and insecure areas. The project also contributes to the
World Bank Gender Strategy to accelerate gender equality to end poverty and to use social protection to achieve its goals
of ending GBV, elevating human capital, and expanding opportunities for women.

11.
The following components are verified as Maximizing Private Finance for Development -enabling (MFD-e).
Component 1 supports strengthening linkages between beneficiaries and microfinance institutions (MFIs) through access
to savings, credit, and financial literacy, thereby addressing key constraints to financial access and inclusion and enabling
the expansion of private financial services among poor households—particularly women—in underserved areas. It will

This subcomponent will support the national rollout, management, and use of the RSU, which will be used to
target the poor for key social protection programs, including and beyond the PAMPV. It will finance household
assessment for about 800,000 households and the maintenance, updating, and management of the RSU database. Using
the data collection methodology developed in 2022, the RSU will expand its geographic coverage by reaching more
provinces and regions, prioritizing areas based on poverty levels, security, and climate risks. While a census sweep is
adapted to urban areas, in rural areas, poor and vulnerable households are preidentified through community processes.

another validation is conducted on the final list of poor households by the community. With the support of the proposed
operation, the goal is for the RSU to cover all poor households in Burkina Faso—about 1.4 million households or 40 percent
of the total population in the country—including female-headed households, displaced persons, persons with disability,
and the elderly. These groups are among those who experience heightened climate vulnerability in the country.

IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY
A. Technical, Economic, and Financial Analysis

Technical Analysis
71.
The proposed project builds on strong global and national evidence demonstrating that integrated social
protection systems combined with economic inclusion interventions can significantly improve livelihoods, resilience,
and economic opportunities for poor and vulnerable populations. Economic inclusion programs typically combine
productive grants, coaching, training, and access to savings mechanisms to help poor households increase their productive
capacity and diversify their livelihoods. Evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa shows that such programs can lead to sustained
improvements in IGAs, productive assets, and women’s economic empowerment, particularly when implemented through
coordinated national social protection programs.
72.
Burkina Faso’s experience over the past decade provides robust evidence38 supporting this approach. Impact
evaluations of the Government’s social safety net program show that support to poor households significantly increased
their ability to accumulate productive assets and savings, thereby strengthening their capacity to invest in livelihoods and
cope with shocks. Evaluations indicate that beneficiary households increased their total assets by 32 percent, while
participation in savings groups increased by 134 percent and informal household savings increased by 65 percent.
Ownership of livestock increased by 11 percent, further strengthening households’ productive capacity. These
improvements in assets and savings help households mitigate negative coping strategies during shocks—such as reducing
food consumption or withdrawing children from school—and enable them to diversify livelihoods beyond traditional
agriculture.
73.
Evidence also shows that social protection programs contribute to improved human capital outcomes for
children in poor households. The previous safety net project significantly increased school enrollment, school attendance,
and grade progression among young children. Nutrition outcomes for children under five improved, particularly during
poor harvest years, suggesting that social protection interventions helped protect children’s health and development
during periods of stress. At the national scale, the project increased school enrollment among children ages 6 to 15 years
by 14.3 percent, years of schooling by 14.9 percent, and the probability of completing any schooling by 14.8 percent, while
progression to the next grade increased by 16.4 percent. Improvements were also observed in children’s language, motor,
and social development outcomes.
74.
Economic inclusion interventions layered on the safety net have also demonstrated strong impacts on women’s
livelihoods. Despite being implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic and a period of rising insecurity, productive
inclusion measures significantly improved women’s economic outcomes. Evaluations show that these interventions
increased nonagricultural annual income among women beneficiaries by 37 percent and the number of days spent on off-

Economic Analysis
78.
The economic rationale for the proposed project rests on three main channels: (a) increased productivity and
income-generating opportunities for beneficiary households through economic inclusion interventions; (b) positive
spillover effects on local economies; and (c) efficiency gains resulting from strengthened social protection delivery
systems. Economic inclusion interventions are expected to generate income gains by helping poor households strengthen
their productive capacity and diversify their livelihoods. By providing productive support, training, coaching, and access to
savings mechanisms, such programs help beneficiaries engage in more productive economic activities, increase the
resilience of their livelihoods, and reduce vulnerability to shocks. These improvements translate into higher household
productivity and sustained improvements in welfare over time, particularly among poor women and vulnerable
populations who face structural barriers to accessing economic opportunities.

79.
Beyond direct impacts on beneficiary households, social protection programs can also stimulate local economic
activity through increased demand for goods and services. To better understand these broader economic impacts, recent
research supported by the World Bank applied Local Economy-Wide Impact Evaluation (LEWIE) simulations to analyze the
local economy effects of safety net programs. These models capture both the direct impacts on beneficiary households
and the indirect spillovers generated through local markets. The analysis found strong multiplier effects in both Senegal
and Burkina Faso. In Senegal, every CFAF 100 transferred to beneficiary households generated approximately CFAF 200 in
total nominal income gains in the local economy, corresponding to a multiplier of 2. Similarly, in Burkina Faso, the analysis
estimated a multiplier of 2.03, consisting of the initial transfer and an additional CFAF 103 (US 18 cents) generated through
increased local economic activity. The LEWIE analysis also shows that these multiplier effects extend beyond program
beneficiaries. While beneficiary households experience the largest gains, a substantial share of the additional income
generated accrues to nonbeneficiary households engaged in local economic activities such as wage labor, agriculture, and
small enterprises. In the Senegal case study, for example, nonbeneficiary households received significant income spillovers
as increased consumption by beneficiary households stimulated demand for locally produced goods and services. These
findings highlight the role of social protection programs in supporting broader local economic development and
strengthening rural economies.
80.
In addition to these economy-wide benefits, investments in social protection delivery systems are expected to
generate important efficiency gains in public spending. Social protection systems built around comprehensive social
registries have been shown to be significantly more efficient than systems relying on fragmented or ad hoc targeting
mechanisms. In Burkina Faso, the social registry is expected to improve efficiency through several channels, including
reducing duplication across programs, improving targeting accuracy, and lowering administrative costs associated with
beneficiary identification and program management.
81.
Improving system efficiency is particularly important in Burkina Faso, where social protection spending is
relatively high compared with regional averages but remains fragmented across numerous programs implemented by
different actors. Strengthening core delivery systems and supporting the implementation of the Government-led PAMPV
will help consolidate interventions, improve coordination among actors, and increase the overall cost-effectiveness of
social protection spending. Over time, these investments will enable Burkina Faso to transition toward a more integrated
and efficient social protection system capable of supporting both long-term poverty reduction and shock response.

the project will prepare an SEA/SH Action Plan that will include a series of project-specific mitigation measures (community
consultations and sensitizations, on-going SEA/SH risk assessment, accountability and response framework to manage
incidents, recruitment procedures, code of conduct), and the Grievance Mechanism (GM) will include procedures to
specifically handle SEA/SH incidents and referral pathways to locally available medical, psychosocial and/or legal aid
services for GBV survivors.
