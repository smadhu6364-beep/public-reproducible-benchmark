I.
STRATEGIC CONTEXT

1. This Project Appraisal Document (PAD) describes the Phase III of the Health Security Program (HeSP) in Western
and Central Africa (AFW), using the Multiphase Programmatic Approach (MPA). On December 19, 2023, the World
Bank’s Board approved the HeSP MPA, with a financing envelope of up to US$500 million equivalent. Concurrently,
Phase I of the MPA (HeSP-1) was also approved with a total IDA financing of US$158 million equivalent covering Cabo
Verde, Guinea, Liberia, and the Economic Community of West African States along with a Pandemic Prevention,
Preparedness and Response grant of US$4 million for Cabo Verde and a Global Financing Facility (GFF)1 grant of US$16
million for Guinea (P179078 – Report No: PAD00117). On September 30, 2025 the Phase II (HeSP-2) covering Nigeria
was approved with a total IDA financing of US$250 million. This proposed Phase III (HeSP-3) expands the Program to
Cameroon, Central African Republic (CAR), Chad, Republic of Congo (RoC), and the Economic and Monetary
Community of Central Africa (CEMAC) (P508837) with a total IDA financing of US$280 million equivalent and a Global
Financing Facility grant of US$10 million for Cameroon. Therefore, this PAD also seeks approval to increase the MPA
financing envelope from US$500 million to US$688 million equivalent. The Program Development Objective (PrDO)
will remain unchanged. Similarly, the components, PrDO indicators, menu of activities, and results indicators, will
remain unchanged across all phases.

A. Regional Context

2. With a population of approximately half a billion and comprised of 22 countries, AFW remains among the most
fragile regions. The region’s population is projected to grow rapidly, adding 800 million working-aged people in the
next thirty years, increasing demand for health and economic resources.2 A challenging macro-fiscal outlook and
high poverty levels make the region particularly vulnerable to shocks, as seen during the COVID-19 pandemic. While
HeSP-3 countries have different income levels (CAR and Chad are low-income; Cameroon and RoC are lower-middle
income), they face similar health security challenges driven by porous borders, displacement and frequent crossborder movement, and systemic weaknesses in disease surveillance and response systems. Despite its resource
wealth, the sub-region continues to lag socioeconomically behind West Africa, with persistently high poverty levels.
In 2023, poverty affected 65.8 percent of the population in CAR, 46.8 percent in RoC, 34.5 percent in Chad, and 23.4
percent in Cameroon.3 A large share of the population (80.6 percent in CAR and 68.7 percent in RoC) falls within the
world’s poorest 20 percent.4

3. Fragility in AFW is exacerbated by conflict and violence, leading to large population displacements. In 2022, there
were 1.1 million new Internally Displaced People (IDPs) in the region, with most people living in areas affected by
Fragility, Conflict, and Violence (FCV).5,6 Porous borders, migration driven by FCV, humanitarian crises, and climate
risks heighten cross-border health threats. Regional conflicts, such as Boko Haram attacks in Cameroon and ongoing
unrest in CAR, can spread instability to neighboring countries via refugee and arms flows.7,8 Chad currently hosts
over 1.5 million IDPs and refugees due to conflict in Lake Chad Basin and instability in Sudan, CAR, and Libya .9

Vulnerable groups (especially rural women and girls10, the poor, and mobile populations) face disproportionate
exposure to health threats, including infectious diseases and gender-based violence. Women are at heightened risk
of internal displacement, sexual violence (including exploitation and trafficking) and forced pregnancies and
marriages during natural disasters, conflicts, and other health emergencies, as seen during the Ebola epidemic and
COVID-19 pandemic. 11

4. Central African countries face high vulnerability to infectious disease outbreaks, a risk that continues to rise due
to factors like zoonotic pathogens, migration, urbanization, inadequate health services, and extreme poverty.
Health emergencies often stem from endemic and often vaccine-preventable diseases (e.g., yellow fever, measles,
cholera), but emerging threats and high-impact pathogens such as EVD and Lassa fever are becoming more
prevalent. In recent years, Cameroon has experienced multiple epidemics: over 21,000 cholera cases (2.4 percent
fatality rate), 125,000 COVID-19 cases (1.6 percent fatality rate), and cases of mpox, rabies, and avian influenza.12
Central Africa is also significantly burdened by antimicrobial resistance (AMR). For instance, in CAR, the mortality
rate associated with AMR is among the highest in the region, with 60.2 deaths per 100,000 people directly
attributable to AMR and 251.3 deaths per 100,000 people indirectly linked to AMR.13 In Chad, fragility, displacement,
and cross-border risks have led to repeated cholera outbreaks since 2015—including a 2019 episode with over 12
percent fatality—and hepatitis E outbreaks in displaced populations, such as the 2024 epidemic among Sudanese
refugees in Ouaddaï.14 Seasonal floods have also raised the risk of arboviral diseases like dengue, chikungunya, and
Rift Valley fever.15

5. This rising threat of health emergencies extends beyond human health. Zoonotic16 outbreaks across Africa have
risen by over 60 percent over the last two decades. Coastal West Africa has been identified as a region where the
risk of zoonotic diseases may increase due to factors such as deforestation and growth in human and livestock
populations.17 This challenge highlights the critical need for implementing multisectoral One Health (OH) strategies
that focus on the intersection of health, agriculture and environment sectors.18,19 Several zoonotic diseases are
endemic to Central Africa, and the recent mpox epidemic in 2024 underscores the need for continuous surveillance,
public awareness, and robust healthcare. In Cameroon, health risk mapping conducted in 2023 identified 13 major
risks affecting humans and animals. Four of these were classified as very high risk (COVID-19, cholera, yellow fever,
and meningitis), while six were deemed high risk (measles, rabies, mpox, Lassa Fever, Ebola, and Marburg).

services and detect/respond to emergencies, protecting people’s health, and preventing setbacks in human capital
gains and development. These efforts also advance the jobs agenda through strategic investments in resilience and
regional integration in one of the world’s most fragile settings, helping to preserve access to education, ensuring
market functionality, and safeguarding people’s jobs. Strengthening health security protects livelihoods today by

7. The health and economic impacts of health emergencies are significant and lasting. Over the last 20 years, at least
13 EVD outbreaks have been reported in equatorial Africa. The largest, a devastating multi-country epidemic in
Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone from 2013 to 2016, resulted in over 28,616 cases and caused 11,310 deaths.20 This
EVD outbreak was estimated to have cost directly affected countries at least US$2.8 billion combined, with
downstream and regional impacts due to interruptions in travel, trade, and population movements,21 diminishing
agricultural productivity, further exacerbating food insecurity and poverty. This extended epidemic had long-term
effects on health systems, including over 500 documented healthcare worker deaths22 and significant supply chain
disruptions in systems already facing chronic insufficiencies in human resources and infrastructure. Reductions in
the provision of care and care-seeking behaviors were not only drivers of EVD transmission in affected communities
but also decreased the effective delivery of essential health services for other health conditions, including vaccine
preventable diseases, malaria, acute respiratory infections, diarrhea and Non-Communicable Diseases (NCDs). In
Central Africa, repeated EVD outbreaks underscore the region's vulnerability to health crises due to weak health
systems, limited surveillance capacity, and difficulties in deploying timely responses, particularly in conflict-affected
areas.

8. A regional approach is critical for successful health security operations. HeSP-3 is well placed to address crossborder infectious disease outbreak risks, while contributing to global and regional public goods. The Program
supports economies of scale and institutional efficiency by leveraging shared assets, enabling South-South learning,
and unlocking regional IDA and development partners financing often inaccessible to countries individually.
Implementation experience grounded in evidence shows that regional investments in health security strengthen
health systems resilience. The Independent Evaluation Group (IEG) concluded that the implementation of the
Regional Disease Surveillance Systems Enhancement (REDISSE)23 series of projects allowed West African countries
to be better prepared to respond to COVID-19, aided by regional leadership and coordination supported through
World Bank investments. Importantly, REDISSE galvanized multiple sectors to address human-animal-environment
related diseases. AFW’s regional approach to health security has been replicated in other regions across the World
Bank and continues to be a model for our partners. Overall, health security remains a cornerstone of the World
Bank’s development agenda in the region, as a “protector” of the people’s health and wellbeing, the investments in
resilient health systems, the economic development and job creation in AFW countries.

program laid the foundation by strengthening institutional capacities at both national and regional levels, with an

B. Project Components

26. The proposed HeSP-3 has five components in line with the Program components. HeSP-3 participating countries
will have the flexibility to select relevant activities under the components, based on their country's context and
priorities, provided these are well-aligned with the PrDO, PDO and theory of change.

Component 1. Prevention of Health Emergencies (US$21.77 million equivalent).
27. Subcomponent 1.1 Health Security Governance, Planning, and Stewardship (US$10.20 million). At the regional
level, this subcomponent will support (i) mapping of regional and national resources for prevention, detection, and
response to health emergencies; (ii) alignment of development partners and financing to country needs and
priorities for health security; (iii) standardized regulations supporting the handling and transfer of biological
materials (e.g., specimen sample transportation); and (iv) regional, cross-border and national information sharing
of health and climate data, as well as coordination of core prevention, detection, and response capacities. At the
country level, the subcomponent will support countries’ prioritization, coordination, regulation, management, and
monitoring of national health security agendas that are aligned with regional and global targets. Regional level
support will also review/support the required governance, legal and regulatory authorities in place for National
Public Health Institutes (NPHIs) (or government institution/departments with equivalent functions) including
institutional capacity support. This encompasses extensive technical support to the development and management
of National Action Plans for Health Security (NAPHS); and supports the review of public health laws, policies, and
regulations (e.g., updating legal frameworks to operationalize health emergency management and response efforts
outlined in multisectoral, multi-hazard national plans). This subcomponent will also support monitoring of IHR core
capacities using tools such as the JEE and PVS, particularly at borders and Points of Entry (PoE), to inform planning
and priority setting. The Project will also support TA in key health security-related cross thematic areas such as
studies/assessments related to medical countermeasures and strengthening of regional standard-setting for disease
surveillance, among others.

28. Subcomponent 1.2 Scaling-up One Health Agenda and combatting AMR (US$11.57 million). This subcomponent is
dedicated to the multisectoral collaboration embedded in the OH approach (the intersection of animal,

29. Subcomponent 2.1. Collaborative Surveillance (US$26.79 million).60 This subcomponent will focus on strengthening
multisectoral and integrated surveillance capacities (including indicators and event-based surveillance), particularly
for epidemic-prone diseases, climate-sensitive diseases, and unusual health events. Activities at cross border and
national levels relate to real-time monitoring and quality improvement for early detection and response and will
operationalize early warning surveillance systems in OH sectors (animal, environment, and human health) with an
emphasis on PoE. Early warning surveillance systems will focus on engagement of community-level actors (with
attention to vulnerable populations – including women). In addition to data collection and management, the
subcomponent will support training and tools for data analysis, and platforms for information sharing at all levels
(community, national, and regional) and across OH sectors. This capacity building will aim to strengthen linkages
between community, national, and regional surveillance systems, to improve the rapidity with which data on
potential health threats are shared within and across countries. Program supported activities will include training
and logistics support (i.e., transport) to strengthen capacity for event verification, investigation, and risk assessment
to inform the level of threat and response - as well as support to the Field Epidemiology Training Program (FETP) at
basic, intermediate, and advanced61 levels. To support closing the gender gap in women’s access to formal trained
technical roles in the health sector, outreach efforts will be done to ensure purposeful targeting of women in
administrative or informal positions to be included in these professional training programs. The increased rate of
women graduating and institutionalized within the health workforce will be critical to ensure FETP and health
security related community-based approaches and activities are designed and implemented with a gender lens
which in turn will improve access and quality of community health approaches. Finally, activities will also build on
gains in national and cross-border regional surveillance activities across OH sectors including PoE, with an emphasis
on data sharing and feedback loops to ensure effective data usage for decision-making.

pathogens. Importance will be placed, at both regional and national levels, on ensuring that laboratories implicated
in human, animal, or environmental health function in an integrated fashion to ensure the OH approach is
operationalized and maximize sharing of materials/supplies and transportation capacities across sectors and
countries. Expanded laboratory capacity would also focus on the ability to carry out surge testing and build capacity
for genomic sequencing with sharing of information across countries to inform surveillance and risk assessment
related activities. Regional level activities aim to strengthen regional interconnected laboratory networks and the

37. Paris Alignment Assessment. On adaptation, Project activities are focused on managing health emergencies and, as
such, measures to reduce the risks of climate change will be integrated into each country’s Project activities. For
example, climate risks to laboratories will be reduced through laboratory and sample transport emergency
preparedness and shock planning. HeSP-3 aims to build broader systems resilience and as such, resilience measures
are embedded in all Project activities. For example, it will enable the integration of health surveillance systems with
climate shocks surveillance systems (e.g. alerts for extreme heat, floods, or droughts) for laboratories and public
health departments. Therefore, the adaptation risk has been reduced to an acceptable level. On mitigation, the
Project will finance EDGE level 1 energy efficiency post rehabilitation or construction certification for all building
investments.  All other Project activities are universally aligned with the Paris Agreement on climate change.

C. Project Beneficiaries

38. About 627 million people in the AFW region are expected to benefit from the Program given its significant
contribution to protecting global public goods. Investments in strengthening health security are considered public
goods, that is, benefiting the entire population of a country (or that of sub-region in this case), as the benefits of
these investments are both non-exclusionary (no one is excluded from them) and non-rivalry (one’s benefit does
not reduce the benefit of others). For example, early detection of a dangerous virus benefits the entire country’s
population, as well as neighboring countries, by allowing faster containment. In particular, the HeSP-3 will especially
benefit infants and children, adolescents, mothers (given the high burden on maternal and perinatal conditions in
the region), and the elderly (who are the most vulnerable to illness and risk factors). In addition, due to the OH
focus, beneficiaries also include livestock farmers, as well as the general population, who will benefit from less
exposure to zoonoses. The Project also benefits disadvantaged populations, including IDPs and refugees. Regional
institutions, such as CEMAC, will benefit from investments in institutional capacities and efforts to fortify their
respective regional agenda and country network.

E. Lessons Learned and Progress on Learning Agenda

41. World Bank support to health security have yielded important lessons from recent health emergencies. In 2016,
the World Bank launched the REDISSE series of projects (Phases 1 to 4) with support to regional (WAHO and the
Economic Community of Central African States – ECCAS)64 and country level activities (including CAR, Chad, and
RoC). Implementation lessons from REDISSE have informed the overall HeSP design by emphasizing the need to
enhance health emergency preparedness with collaborative surveillance under an OH approach, integrating gender
considerations, and establishing a health security coordination platform linked with national health system
strengthening projects and related operations. Moreover, the World Bank’s support to the COVID-19 response in
AFW also provided valuable lessons for the design of the HeSP, namely the importance of building up countries’
health systems’ resilience to ensure continuity in the provision of essential health services during emergencies, and
the value of pre-positioned financing (e.g., via REDISSE) to quickly implement response efforts, avoiding critical
delays in mobilizing necessary resources and the use of framework agreements to expedite procurement. HeSP-3
will leverage the technical and operational lessons from REDISSE and the COVID-19 pandemic by supporting
investments to enhance the resilience of health systems to safeguard the continuity of essential health services
during emergencies, including by focusing on community level actors, such as CHWs and agriculture extension
workers for PHC and pandemic preparedness and response services.

47. Program sustainability will be enhanced by the flexible yet standardized menu of activities and the regional
approach of the Project. The Program will support: (i) green and resilient refurbishments to prioritized health
facilities (including laboratories); (ii) technical and institutional capacity; (iii) establishment of regional standards,
policies, and protocols with buy-in at country and regional level; (iv) training on the use of equipment and their

health. Knowledge sharing will also be facilitated through the RCC as well as other technical working groups
convened by CEMAC and its specialized agencies and will include countries across the region. Finally, development
of standard protocols at the regional level, initiatives for cross-country collaboration, such as established protocols
and training for cross border efforts to prevent, detect and respond to health emergencies including improved data
and information sharing, are also expected to contribute to the sustainability of Program investments.

IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY

A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis

49. The economic case for investing in the capacities to cope with health emergencies in the region focuses on three
dimensions: prevention, detection, and response. Communicable diseases (including vaccine preventable and
endemic diseases in the region) place major constraints on the health, education, and potential earnings of people
living in the region. In fact, infectious disease outbreaks can cause higher indirect than direct costs, due to significant
human capital and productivity losses. Weak preparedness capacities to prevent and detect disease outbreaks are
exacerbated by the imminent threat of climate change, which is expected to impact already weak regional and
national systems, displace populations, and create environmental conditions more favorable for extreme weather
events and disease emergence and transmission. Hence, the economic rationale for investing in strengthening the
region’s ability to mount a robust response to these crises is key to mitigating the economic burden not only on
individuals, but for all countries in the region. Such investments also have significant positive spillover effects across
sectors, from education to labor, and across borders.

50. At the global level, the return on country system investments and operations to reduce pandemic risk are more
than tenfold. On a per annum basis, the estimated return at the global level ranges from 50 to 123 percent per
annum. The International Working Group report on financing pandemic preparedness (From Panic and Neglect to
Investing in Health Security: Financing Pandemic Preparedness at a National Level) estimates the expected annual
loss due to an influenza pandemic at 2.07 percent of Gross National Income (GNI) in Liberia. This does not capture
the impact of pathogens or emerging pandemic threats such as Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) and
Ebola (which have been linked to larger losses), the effect of smaller scale outbreaks, or of emerging diseases that
become endemic. Investments in pandemic preparedness yield substantial economic benefits, especially in regions
like Central Africa. A joint UNICEF/WFP study found that humanitarian preparedness interventions in high-risk
contexts have an average financial return on investment (ROI) exceeding 200 percent, meaning every dollar invested
saves more than two dollars in future emergency response costs. In CAR, the COVID-19 pandemic significantly
impacted the economy, with projections indicating a reduction in economic growth from an anticipated 4.5 percent
to between 0 percent and -1.2 percent in 2020.65 Similarly, RoC experienced a sharp economic contraction in 2020
due to the pandemic, with output declining and a slow recovery observed in subsequent years.66 These examples
underscore the critical importance of investing in pandemic preparedness to mitigate severe economic downturns.
Strengthening health systems and ensuring operational readiness can prevent substantial economic losses and save
lives. Given the high vulnerability of Central African countries to outbreaks of diseases with epidemic and pandemic
potential, such investments are not only cost-effective but essential for sustainable development.

51. Most outbreaks have significant short- and long-term impacts. For example, while in 2016, the World Bank
estimated that the EVD epidemic in 2013-2016 resulted in short-term economic costs of US$2.8 billion across the

54. In general terms, the use of public funds to invest in the overall health of a population is a public good. In the case
of health emergencies such as disease outbreaks, the rationale for public sector investment is based on the
overwhelming economic burden that such events, individually and collectively, place on communities and countries
in the region and beyond. The multisectoral impacts of outbreaks threaten economic growth and poverty reduction,
by imposing long-lasting negative impacts on human capital and labor productivity, giving rise to greater recognition
that strong health emergency preparedness and response capacities also constitute global public good. The systems
required to prevent, detect, and respond to health emergencies, such as surveillance and laboratory systems, are
both non-rival and non-exclusive. The benefits of these systems are shared regionally and globally; when functioning
well, they prevent outbreaks from spreading across borders and thereby protect the health and economic wellbeing of countries beyond the ones in which investments are made. Additionally, sharing costly resources across
countries can further increase the efficiency of investments. For example, high-level assets such as level three
reference laboratories or well-staffed, highly capacitated research institutions may be leveraged to serve the needs
of multiple countries with similar disease risk profiles.

75. Analysis. Despite critical roles as frontline caregivers and CHWs, women remain underrepresented in formal
epidemiological training and decision-making.76 Past health emergencies, including COVID-19 and the 2014-2016
Ebola epidemic, highlighted how this disparity contributed to gaps in health security capacity, surveillance, RCCE –
hampering the effectiveness of emergency response. Only 28 percent of physicians in Africa are women,77 and fewer
than 30 percent of STEM78 graduates in sub-Saharan Africa are female. In AFW, women dominate low-paid frontline
roles but are largely absent from technical positions (such as, field epidemiologists, laboratory specialists).
Anecdotal evidence and lessons from prior World Bank projects such as the West Africa Regional Disease
Surveillance Capacity Strengthening (WARDS, P125018) confirm that women’s underrepresentation in key technical
roles79 is partly due to limited access to training programs like FETP/FELTP training and In Service Applied Veterinary
Epidemiology training (ISAVET) programs. Women face multiple barriers to training access, including time and
mobility constraints due to domestic obligations, societal norms where many women face pressure to prioritize
family over career, lack of formal opportunities, few female role models, and gender bias in career advancement.80

76. Actions. From supply side perspectives, the HeSP-3 aims to strengthen health security in Central Africa, inter alia,
by closing the gender gap in technical roles such as epidemiologists and veterinarians. Under Subcomponent 2.3,
the project targets frontline women in human and animal health through proactive outreach (e.g., collaboration
with ministries of health and agriculture to identify and encourage women candidates to apply), inclusive (e.g.
gender targets), flexible training formats, including shorter options that minimize time away from home. The project
supports FETP/FELTP and ISAVET training at three levels: basic (three months), intermediate (nine months), and
advanced levels (two years, equivalent to a master's degree). Graduates are deployed to support outbreak response,
surveillance, and public health programs. This approach will expand women’s access to formal training, increase
employment opportunities; and enhance service uptake, as female patients often prefer female providers. This can
generate triple dividends: advancing gender equality, building a stronger health security capacity, and promoting
women’s economic empowerment as a driver for development. HeSP-3 will also integrate gender considerations
into OH, AMR, and risk communication plans, and ensure continuity of essential services during crises. Moreover,
the project will address women’s vulnerabilities during health emergencies by supporting continuity of essential
services during crises, including for sexual and reproductive health and maternal care, and mitigating risks like sexual
violence and disease exposure.

ANNEX 1: Economic and Monetary Community of Central Africa (CEMAC)

I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT

A. Sectoral and Institutional Context
1. CEMAC was established in 1994, by the Treaty of N'Djamena, with the objective of promoting cooperation, and
sub-regional integration among its six member states,81 promotion of trade, the institution of a genuine common
market, and greater solidarity among peoples and towards underprivileged countries and regions. From 2008,
CEMAC expanded its mandate to promote economic integration and human development among its member states.
The CEMAC Commission has specialized agencies in charge of human, animal, and environmental health, including:
(i) Organization for the Coordination of Endemic Disease Control in Central Africa - OCEAC; (ii) Interstate Centre for
Higher Public Health Education in Central Africa – CIESPAC; (iii) Economic Community of Livestock, Meat and
Fisheries Resources - CEBEVHIRA; and (iv) Regional Center for Applied Research on the Development of Agricultural
Systems in Central Africa – PRASAC.

2. At the regional level, CEMAC will leverage its comparative advantages to implement regional level activities,
including: (i) its political mandate and direct line of communication with the political decision-makers of its member
states; (ii) capacity to propose the adoption of regional conventions, agreements, and regulations for the regional
health sector; (iii) advocacy at the highest level for the adoption and implementation of international health
resolutions; (iv) ability to leverage and facilitate the exchange and mobilization of resources between countries and
to harmonize health policies; and (v) capacity to collect, manage, and disseminate health information to guide
interventions in the region.

B. Relevance to Higher-Level Objectives

3. Program objectives are aligned with the 2025-2028 Strategic Health Security Plan for the CEMAC zone, which
emphasizes: (i) strengthening health surveillance systems for early detection and rapid response to emerging and
re-emerging health threats; (ii) improving health emergency management capacity, by developing coordinated
preparedness and response mechanisms, including the rapid mobilization of human and material resources; (iii)
promoting sub-regional and international cooperation, to ensure concerted management of cross-border health
risks and facilitate the exchange of information and resources between member states, as well as with neighboring
countries; (iv) strengthening the resilience of healthcare infrastructures, by improving access to quality healthcare,
particularly in rural and remote areas, to reduce health inequalities; and (v) strengthening the capacities of
healthcare professionals in charge of prevention and management of health events at national and sub-regional
level, to ensure a collective and informed response in times of crisis. Objectives are also aligned with the Animal
Health Strategy for Africa 2019-2035, which provides a framework to African Union institutions and Regional
Economic Community members for a sustainable animal health system that meets relevant international standards.

4. Project Development Objective (PDO): Increase regional collaboration and health system capacities to prevent,
detect and respond to health emergencies in Central Africa.

6. Subcomponent 1.2. Scaling-up OH Agenda and combatting AMR (US$2.00 million). The subcomponent will finance

opportunities for interoperability; (xiii) development of protocols, tools, and platforms for collaborative

ANNEX 2: Republic of Cameroon

I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT

1. In 2023, Cameroon’s estimated population stood at 28,372,687.82 Cameroon has a young population with 56
percent of the population is of working age (15-64), 41 percent aged 0-14, and 3 percent 65 and older. By 2050, the
population is expected to grow by 84 percent to 51.2 million, with the working-age group (15-65) increasing to 62
percent of the total population.83 As the largest economy in the CEMAC, Cameroon plays a crucial role in the regional
economy. However, it faces persistent economic and political challenges that hinder sustainable development.
Poverty remains widespread, with an estimated 37.7 percent of the population living below the national poverty
line of FCFA 813 (US$1.34) per day. Further, poverty is prevalent in the conflict-afflicted areas, namely the Extreme
North (69.2 percent), the Northwest (66.8 percent), the North (61.1 percent) and the Adamaoua (45.1 percent)
regions (National Statistics Institute, 2024). Almost half of the population (46.6 percent) lives below the Low- and
Middle-Income Countries (LMIC) poverty line of US$3.65 per day, and the economy is predominantly informal.
Ongoing socio-political tensions in these areas have led to significant internal displacement, with over 720,000 IDPs
and approximately 420,000 refugees, primarily from CAR and Nigeria. These demographic shifts contribute to
heightened health vulnerabilities and service delivery challenges, particularly among marginalized populations.

2. The country’s macroeconomic stability has been impacted by declining oil revenues and structural economic
challenges. Despite efforts to restore fiscal balance, public finances remain under strain. The COVID-19 pandemic
exacerbated existing vulnerabilities, reducing GDP growth from a pre-crisis projection of 4.2 percent to -2.5 percent
in 2020. In 2024, growth estimates showed signs of recovery after their slowdown in 2023,84 but World Bank
projections expect General Government Expenditure (GGE) per capita to stagnate over the next five years.85 As a
result, in 2022, Cameroon’s domestic funding for health per capita was just US$10.79, far below required levels for
achieving universal coverage of the essential Disease Control Priority (DCP-3) package of reproductive, maternal,
newborn, child and adolescent health and high-priority interventions. In addition, low government funding (driven
by weak revenue generation and low prioritization of health in the government’s budget) has led to a high reliance
on household’s out-of-pocket payments (61 percent of current health spending). These economic constraints have
affected essential health services, hindering the expansion of service coverage and improvement of quality of care.

3. Cameroon's health system is organized into three tiers: central, intermediate, and peripheral. The Ministry of
Public Health (MPH) at the central level formulates and implements health policy, coordinating and regulating the
sector. Decentralization, initiated by the 1996 Constitution, transfers authority and resources to local governments,
enhancing local governance and public service delivery. Decentralized territorial communities manage service
delivery in regions and communes. The intermediate level, with 10 regional delegations, supports health districts.
The peripheral level includes 205 health districts responsible for implementing health programs. Health services are
provided by 2,811 public health facilities, including seven general hospitals, 13 central hospitals, 25 regional
hospitals, 175 district hospitals, 291 sub-division medical centers, and 2,300 integrated health centers. At the
community level, CHWs deliver health promotion and prevention services.

ANNEX 3: Central African Republic

I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT

1. The Central African Republic (CAR), a landlocked country in Central Africa, has an estimated population of
5,459,07587 as of March 2025. Despite its vast natural resources, including gold, diamonds, and timber, CAR remains
one of the world’s least developed countries, ranking among the lowest on the Human Development Index. The
country has experienced decades of political instability, armed conflict, and governance challenges, which have
severely hindered economic growth and social development. More than 70 percent of the population lives below
the poverty line, and the ongoing security crisis continues to exacerbate humanitarian needs.

2. The protracted crisis has led to significant population displacement, with over 700,000 IDPs and nearly 750,000
refugees in neighboring countries. The fragile security situation has also disrupted access to essential services,
including healthcare, education, and infrastructure. In addition to conflict-related challenges, CAR faces climate
change vulnerabilities, including increased droughts, floods, and food insecurity, which further threaten public
health outcomes and facilitate disease outbreaks.

3. CAR’s health system is severely under-resourced and fragile, with limited infrastructure, medical personnel, and
financing along with frequent drug stock out. The public health sector is managed by the MoHP, but due to the
ongoing crisis, much of the service delivery relies on international humanitarian organizations and NonGovernmental Organizations (NGOs). Approximately 40 percent of health facilities are either non-functional or only
partially operational, particularly in conflict-affected areas where access remains extremely difficult. The country
faces a high burden of communicable diseases, including malaria, HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and neglected tropical
diseases. Malaria is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality, responsible for over 40 percent of all reported
illnesses. The prevalence of HIV/AIDS remains high, with an estimated 3.7 percent adult prevalence rate, among the
highest in Central Africa. Waterborne diseases such as cholera and diarrhea are also widespread due to poor
sanitation and lack of access to clean water. In addition to communicable diseases, NCDs, including cardiovascular
diseases and diabetes, are emerging concerns, exacerbated by limited healthcare services and diagnostic

ANNEX 4: Republic of Chad

I.
STRATEGIC CONTEXT

1. Chad is a country located in Central Africa, covering an area of 1,284,000 km² with a population of 20.3 million
inhabitants as of 2024. It is divided into three climatic zones--desert in the north, Sahelian in the center and tropical
in the south. This climatic difference determines the epidemiological profile of Chad's 23 provinces. The country has
a Human Capital Index of 0.30 in 2024 (World Bank 2024), signifying that a child born today will achieve only 30
percent of its potential. Chad’s economy is primarily dependent on agriculture, livestock, fishing, and oil resources.
Livestock farming accounts for 30 percent of the country's non-oil exports, contributes 53 percent to rural GDP, and
supports 40 percent of the rural population.

2. Chad is confronted with numerous internal and external shocks that affect the resilience of its healthcare system.
These include the economic crisis associated with the decline in oil prices, security challenges both domestically
(intercommunity conflicts) and internationally (terrorism), humanitarian crises such as the war in Sudan, health
crises including COVID-19, dengue fever, diphtheria, measles, and viral hepatitis E, as well as climate change-related
events like the floods in 2022 and 2024.

3. Chad’s poor health indicators are due to low public spending in the sector. In 2014, the maternal mortality rate
was 860 per 100,000 live births. The PNDS aims to reduce this to 751 by 2021, and the National Health Policy targets
500 by 2030, still higher than the SDG3 target of 140. Despite efforts like the "Every Newborn" action plan, neonatal
mortality has only slightly decreased from 34 per 1,000 live births in 2014 to 33 in 2019, far from the 2030 target of
12. Neonatal mortality accounts for 33 percent of infant and child mortality, which is currently 122 per 1,000 live
births, compared to the 2030 target of 25. In 2018, household expenditure on health constituted 64.4 percent of
current healthcare spending (NHA2018). During the same period, overseas development assistance rose from
FCFA18 billion in 2018 to FCFA26 billion in 2020, before declining to FCFA21.29 billion in 2023. This assistance
accounted for 29 percent of the healthcare budget in 2018, 16 percent in 2019, 28 percent in 2020, 24 percent in
2021, 27 percent in 2022, and 21 percent in 2023. Over this entire period, the health budget averaged 6 percent of
the national budget annually.

4. Recurring Disease outbreaks pose a significant health threat to the country. In February 2023, a risk mapping
exercise identified the main health risks across the country, whereas 18 threats and 25 risks were found including:
Hemorrhagic fever, Trauma, Yellow fever, Cholera, Malnutrition, Meningitis, Chikungunya, COVID-19, Measles,
Envenomation, Poliomyelitis, Malaria, Chemical poisoning, Viral hepatitis E, Rabies, and Mpox. The influx of refugees
including “zero dose” child refugees, fleeing the conflict in neighboring Sudan, alongside increasing frequency and
intensity of floods, heatwaves and other climate shocks significantly exacerbate the risk of vector-borne, waterborne, and respiratory disease outbreaks.89

5. The Project is aligned with Chad 2024-2029 healthcare priorities focusing on six actions: (i) enhancing epidemic
response; (ii) reducing communicable and NCDs; (iii) prioritizing prevention; (iv) expanding UHC; (v) reforming the
healthcare system (UAS); and (vi) strengthening the overall healthcare system. The proposed interventions are
designed to enhance the country’s capacity to prevent and respond to disease outbreaks and pandemics. Given the

ANNEX 5: Republic of Congo

I.
STRATEGIC CONTEXT

1. RoC has an estimated population of 6,440,00591 as of March 2025. Approximately 70 percent of RoC’s population
lives in urban areas, with Brazzaville and Pointe-Noire being the largest cities and economic hubs. Human capital
development has long lagged that of peer countries: the World Bank’s Human Capital Index value for Congo is 0.42
in 2020, leaving the country well below the average of 0.48 for lower-middle income countries. The country has
significant forest coverage, representing over 65 percent of its territory, contributing to its high biodiversity and
environmental richness. Despite being an oil-producing economy, RoC has faced persistent economic challenges
due to fluctuations in global oil prices, leading to high debt levels and limited fiscal space for social sector
investments. Poverty remains a critical issue, particularly in rural areas, where access to basic services, including
healthcare, is limited. From 2015 to 2021, real GDP contracted by an average of 2.9 percent, leading to a significant
decline in per capita income and a rise in extreme poverty from 33.5 percent in 2015 to 46.6 percent in 2022. The
country is also highly vulnerable to climate change, with increased risks of flooding, droughts, and vector-borne
diseases due to shifting environmental conditions. Congo has been through several periods of social unrest and
conflict and remains classified as a country affected by FCV.

2. The RoC healthcare system consists of public, private, and faith-based providers, with the Ministry of Health and
Population (MoHP) overseeing service delivery. However, the system is underfunded and faces significant
disparities between urban and rural areas. Brazzaville and Pointe-Noire host most of the country’s specialized
healthcare facilities, while many rural regions suffer from a lack of healthcare professionals, poor infrastructure, and
limited medical supplies. The country92 faces a double burden of disease, with a high prevalence of communicable
diseases such as malaria, HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and neglected tropical diseases, alongside a rising burden of NCDs
like cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cancer. Malaria remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality,
particularly among children under five. Meanwhile, the increasing prevalence of hypertension and respiratory
illnesses is linked to rapid urbanization, pollution, and lifestyle changes.

3. RoC’s pandemic preparedness and response capacity remain weak, with limited epidemiological surveillance,
laboratory capacity, and emergency response mechanisms. The 2021 GHSI and the WHO-led JEE 2023 identified
gaps in disease surveillance, human resources for health emergencies, and intersectoral coordination. The COVID19 pandemic further exposed these vulnerabilities, highlighting the need for stronger early warning systems, betterequipped laboratories, and improved health workforce capacity to respond to emerging threats.

4. The HeSP-3 Program aligns with RoC’s national health priorities and international commitments, including the
PNDS 2023–2026, which emphasizes strengthening primary healthcare, improving disease surveillance, and
enhancing health security. Additionally, RoC has committed to UHC and is implementing reforms to expand
healthcare access and reduce out-of-pocket expenditures. The country has also integrated climate resilience
strategies into its health planning, recognizing the growing health risks posed by climate change. HeSP-3 aligns with
the SDGs, particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 13 (Climate Action), ensuring that health sector
investments contribute to climate adaptation and epidemic preparedness.

emergency, and national guide for detection of priority and multi-resistant pathogens.

develop multi-sectoral national plans for preparedness and response to public health events; (ii) strengthen the
monitoring of activities under the OH approach; (iii) develop and implement the national action plan to combat
AMR; and (iv) contribute to reducing the impact of disease risks and public health events under the OH approach
through the development of risk communication SOPs based on the OH approach and training health personnel on
RCCE.

surveillance, including sentinel surveillance; (iii) strengthen community-based surveillance and event-based
surveillance; (iv) strengthen food surveillance; and (v) strengthen the digitization of surveillance via DHIS2 (e-SMIR).

laboratories; and (vi) dissemination of laboratory system strategy documents.

trials, and sample collection, storage and transport; and (ii) strengthening of surveillance including FELTP (basic,
intermediate, and advanced levels), e-SIMR, targeted training for environmental and animal health, biomedical
waste management, and training of community and civil society players involved in health emergency interventions.
