I. MPA PROGRAM STRATEGIC CONTEXT
1.
The rapidly growing youth population in Eastern and Southern Africa represents a major opportunity to
strengthen human capital, productivity, and long-term prosperity, but realizing this opportunity depends on faster
progress in basic education. The region continues to face a severe learning crisis, with high learning poverty, large
numbers of children out of school, weak completion at primary and lower secondary levels, and persistent disparities by
gender, poverty, location, disability, and displacement status. Recent shocks, including conflict, climate change, food
insecurity, debt pressures, and tighter fiscal conditions, have further constrained education systems and increased the
urgency of cost-effective reforms that improve learning and expand equitable access at scale. The Advancing Innovative
Methods to Promote Learning in Eastern and Southern Africa (AIM4Learning, P507220) Multiphase Programmatic
Approach (MPA) supports this agenda by providing a long-term regional framework for countries to improve access,
learning, teaching quality, learning environments, system capacity, and cross-country collaboration in basic education.1
Phase 1 of the MPA was approved by the World Bank’s Executive Directors on February 20, 2025, and three subsequent
phases have been approved so far. The strategic context remains the same as described in the Phase 1 Program Appraisal
Document (PAD).
II.
MPA PROGRAM DESCRIPTION
A.
Rationale for using the MPA
2.
The MPA is designed to support a sustained regional response to the learning crisis, a large, long-term, and
shared challenge across participating countries. AIM4Learning provides a regional framework that signals long-term
commitment, supports engagement with a critical mass of countries, and generates political and operational momentum
around reducing learning poverty. It gives participating countries flexibility to join a common development initiative while
selecting and sequencing interventions according to their own starting points, priorities, and implementation capacity.
The MPA creates opportunities for cross-country learning, co-creation of adaptable solutions, access to regional resources
and expertise, and alignment of partner financing around a common framework. It also supports economies of scale by
helping countries develop and use shared standards, tools, evidence, and approaches that can reduce duplication, lower
costs, and strengthen regional integration. All approved AIM4Learning phases are now effective, and implementation
progress across the portfolio is satisfactory, although the MPA remains at an early stage, with the longest running project
under implementation for approximately 10 months. All active phases are rated Satisfactory for progress toward
achievement of the PDO and implementation progress, with countries advancing core implementation arrangements,
including PIU staffing, operations manuals, work plans, budgets, fiduciary systems, and early delivery of first-year results.
B.
Program Development Objective and Indicators
3.
The MPA Program Development Objective (PrDO) is to increase equitable access and improve learning
outcomes in basic education in Eastern and Southern Africa. Basic education refers to pre-primary, primary, and lower
secondary education. The MPA aims to reach 72 million children with better and more equitable education, reduce
learning poverty by one quarter, reduce the out-of-school population by half, and increase lower secondary school access
for adolescent girls by one third.

C.
Program Theory of Change
4.
The MPA’s Theory of Change identifies learning poverty and unequal access, progression, and learning across
population groups as central constraints to human capital formation in the region. At the country level, the MPA
addresses these constraints through three connected dimensions. First, it supports equitable access to basic education,
including by improving learning environments and reducing barriers to lower secondary progression, especially for girls.
Second, it supports efforts to reduce learning poverty by strengthening teacher effectiveness, teaching and learning
materials, assessment, and instructional support. Third, it strengthens service delivery, financing, and implementation
capacity so that reforms are planned, financed, monitored, and sustained through country education systems. At the
regional level, Pillar 4 supports a learning agenda, cross-country collaboration, regional public goods, and evidence
generation so countries can learn from one another, reduce duplication, and adapt effective approaches to their own
contexts.
D.
Program Beneficiaries
5.
The main beneficiaries of the AIM4Learning MPA are children of basic education age in participating countries,
who are expected to benefit from expanded access, improved learning environments, stronger teaching, and better
implemented education reforms. Over the MPA period, the Program is expected to reach 72 million children with better
education. Teachers, school leaders, education ministries, assessment agencies, implementation units, and other
institutions responsible for basic education reform will also benefit from strengthened systems, training, tools, and
implementation support. The Program places particular emphasis on marginalized and vulnerable groups, including girls,
children from poor and rural households, children with disabilities, refugees, internally displaced children, and other
children who face barriers to access, progression, and learning.
E.
Program Description
6.
The MPA provides a long-term regional framework for improving access and learning in basic education while
allowing each participating country to tailor reforms to its own context, priorities, and implementation capacity. The
Program is organized around four pillars. Pillar 1, Transforming Learning Spaces, supports safe, inclusive, climate-resilient,
and technology-enabled learning environments. Pillar 2, Empowering a Skilled and Motivated Workforce of Educators,
supports teacher preparation, teacher professional development, instructional support, school leadership, and teacher
workforce reforms. Pillar 3, Strengthening Systems, Finance, and Learning, supports curriculum, assessment, learning
materials, teacher management, financing, governance, and implementation systems. Pillar 4, Building Cross-Country

Page 3
Collaboration, supports regional learning, shared tools and standards, regional public goods, and evidence generation on
what works to reduce learning poverty and expand access at scale.
F.
Program Framework
7.
The AIM4Learning MPA supports participating countries and regional entities over a 10-year implementation
period. Phase 1 included Comoros and Ethiopia, followed by subsequent phases for Mozambique, Madagascar and
Malawi. Each country’s operation selects from the MPA menu of activities while aligning with the PrDO and results
framework. All existing phases of the AIM4Learning MPA are effective, and implementation progress across participating
countries is satisfactory, although the portfolio remains at an early stage. The MPA allows countries and regional bodies
to join the Program based on readiness, relevance to the MPA approach, availability of financing, and alignment with the
PrDO. Table 2 presents the MPA Program Framework.

G.
Learning Agenda
8.
The MPA includes a practical learning agenda to help countries address evidence gaps and improve
implementation during the life of the Program. The learning agenda focuses on five core questions. First, how can
countries strengthen initial teacher education and continuous professional development to improve teaching quality and
student learning? Second, how can foundational systems, including curriculum, assessment, teacher support, learning
materials, infrastructure, regulation, and financing, be aligned so reforms work together? Third, how can digital tools
improve learning, teaching, administration, and learning continuity during disruptions? Fourth, how can genderresponsive interventions improve girls’ access, retention, progression, and learning? Fifth, how can cross-country
collaboration and knowledge sharing accelerate the adoption and adaptation of effective practices? The regional grant
will help advance this agenda by financing selected joint studies, implementation research, diagnostics, synthesis
products, and learning mechanisms linked to country reform priorities.
H.
Program Implementation
9.
Implementation arrangements for the AIM4Learning MPA are anchored in country-level implementation
systems and regional coordination. Country operations are implemented through national ministries, agencies, and
project implementation units, with arrangements tailored to each country context. The regional grant will be implemented
by Association of African Universities (AAU), which will serve as the regional coordinating and facilitating entity for Pillar
4 activities. AAU will support cross-country learning, partner engagement, evidence sharing, and the connection of
participating countries to regional public goods and specialized expertise. Participating country ministries and
implementation units will remain responsible for country-level implementation and any national rollout financed through
country operations, government programs, or partner resources.
I.
Overall Program Risk
10.
The overall residual risk for the AIM4Learning MPA is Substantial. The MPA will be implemented across countries
with different institutional capacities, macroeconomic conditions, political and governance contexts, environmental and
social risks, and fiduciary systems. Key risks relate to fiscal pressures, fragility and conflict, climate shocks, uneven
implementation capacity, fiduciary weaknesses, and the challenge of sustaining reforms across complex education
systems. These risks are mitigated through the MPA’s phased design, use of country-specific implementation
arrangements, investment in system capacity, implementation support, and Pillar 4 mechanisms for regional learning,
peer exchange, evidence generation, and adaptation across phases. The regional operation financed under this phase has
a Moderate risk rating because it will finance technical assistance, evidence generation, knowledge exchange, regional
public goods, and coordination activities rather than physical investments or direct service delivery.

I.
STRATEGIC CONTEXT
1.
This Project Appraisal Document covers Phase 5 of the Advancing Innovative Methods to Promote Learning in
Eastern and Southern Africa (AIM4Learning) Multiphase Programmatic Approach (MPA), approved by the World
Bank’s Executive Directors on February 20, 2025 (P507220, PADHI01046).2 Phases 1 through 4 of the MPA are active.
Phase 5 responds to shared education constraints across Eastern and Southern Africa (AFE) that are difficult, costly, or
inefficient for countries to address individually. It will operationalize the MPA’s regional learning and collaboration
pillar by helping participating countries identify, adapt, and use evidence and regional public goods to inform basic
education reforms. By generating and sharing evidence, facilitating cross-country learning, and supporting the uptake
of shared regional resources, the operation will strengthen countries’ capacity to improve basic education outcomes
at scale and contribute to the wider objectives of the AIM4Learning MPA. The operation will be financed through an
IDA GROW grant to AAU as the eligible regional organization responsible for supporting the regional coordination and
learning agenda under Pillar 4.
2.
Eastern and Southern Africa’s rapidly growing youth population creates a major opportunity for faster growth,
poverty reduction, and job creation, but only if countries can strengthen education and skills outcomes at scale. Over
the next 30 years, the number of young people in the region is projected to rise from 155 million to 260 million,
significantly increasing the region’s share of the global workforce. Realizing this demographic opportunity will depend
on whether countries can equip many more children and young people with the foundational skills needed to learn,
adapt, and participate productively in labor markets.
3.
Learning poverty is the central education challenge in the region and a major driver of human capital deficits
that constrain productivity, earnings, and jobs. Approximately 89 percent of children in AFE are learning poor, and
education accounts for the largest share of the human capital deficit in many countries, often explaining around one
half to two thirds of the total gap. This matters not only for education outcomes, but also for the jobs agenda, because
weak foundational learning limits the later acquisition of technical, digital, and work relevant skills and reduces the
productivity and adaptability of the future labor force.
4.
Shortfalls in access, progression, and completion continue to weaken the skills pipeline, with lower secondary
now the most important access bottleneck for the MPA’s broader development objectives. Across the region, 35
million children are denied basic education, 28 percent of children who start school do not complete primary, and 55
percent do not complete lower secondary.3 One out of three school age girls remain out of school in many settings,
and without substantial action, 66 million children, adolescents, and youth in the region could be out of school by 2030.
These shortfalls matter for the region’s longer-term skills and jobs agenda because lower secondary is increasingly the
stage at which foundational learning must be consolidated and connected to broader skills pathways. The urgency is
reinforced by labor market conditions. Around 8 million young people enter the labor force each year in AFE, but fewer
than 1 million secure formal jobs. Strengthening foundational learning and expanding progression beyond primary are
central to building the skills base needed for higher productivity, better jobs, and longer-term economic
transformation.
5.
Gender gaps in the education workforce constrain girls’ retention, progression, and learning, particularly at
lower secondary level. Girls face barriers linked to distance, safety concerns, household responsibilities, early marriage,
adolescent pregnancy, and direct and opportunity costs. These barriers are compounded in many settings by the

6.
Countries are seeking to improve learning and expand access in a more difficult financing and delivery
environment. Conflict, climate pressures, food insecurity, debt stress, and recent fuel and energy price pressures are
raising costs for households and education systems while further constraining public financing. Higher fuel prices can
make schooling less affordable by increasing household spending on transport, food, and other essentials. They can
also raise school operating costs and put pressure on school feeding, materials, supervision, and other non-salary
spending. Recent spending trends reinforce these concerns. Seven of the nine countries in the region with available
data show a decline in government education spending as a share of gross domestic product since 2020, while external
financing conditions are also weakening. United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO)
education finance monitoring indicates that total aid to education is expected to fall by more than one quarter by 2027,
with important implications for low income countries where aid accounts for about 17 percent of public education
spending. These pressures can affect the regularity and adequacy of school grants and other non-salary spending,
increasing the risk that costs are shifted to communities or that essential inputs for learning are reduced. They also
make it more important for countries to understand the costs, financing requirements, and sustainability of
foundational learning reforms, particularly where reforms depend on protecting core school-level inputs during periods
of fiscal stress.
7.
Climate change is an intensifying risk across AFE, adding pressure to education access, quality, and system
resilience. Based on the 2022 Notre Dame Global Adaptation Initiative Vulnerability Index, nearly all countries in the
region rank among the world’s most climate-vulnerable countries, reflecting overlapping climate hazards and limited
adaptive capacity. Cyclones, droughts, floods, heat stress, and climate-related food insecurity have disrupted schooling,
damaged school infrastructure, strained household and government education budgets, increased displacement, and
reduced students’ readiness to learn. Country Climate and Development Reports (CCDRs) for countries in the region
identify education as a sector exposed to climate risks, underscoring the need for stronger evidence, practical tools,
and institutional capacity to help education systems adapt. Addressing climate vulnerability as part of the learning crisis
reinforces the case for regional collaboration, shared tools, and evidence-informed implementation support under the
operation.
8.
Evidence on how to improve learning is growing, but it remains too narrow to guide large-scale reform across
the different country contexts and government school systems in AFE. Structured pedagogy, foundational literacy
and numeracy reforms, targeted remediation, classroom assessment linked to instruction, stronger instructional
content and learning materials, teacher professional development, and school-based support systems have shown
measurable gains in some countries. These experiences show that countries can improve learning, not only expand
access. However, much of the evidence remains concentrated in a limited number of countries, interventions, and
delivery settings, often at small scale or in highly supported environments that differ from the government school
systems most children attend. A priority now is to build stronger evidence on how countries can adapt, finance,
implement, and sustain these approaches through government-led education systems at scale.
9.
The core implementation challenge is to help countries translate promising evidence into reforms that can be
adapted, implemented, and sustained within country education systems. Countries across the region are looking for
more effective and scalable ways to improve learning and expand access, but many lack the systems needed to test,

Page 15
adapt, and apply evidence under local conditions. Policymakers often face practical uncertainty about which
approaches are most relevant, how they should be sequenced, and what implementation support is needed to sustain
them at scale.
10. This implementation challenge is central to the World Bank’s jobs agenda because foundational learning
underpins later acquisition of technical, digital, and work-relevant skills. Weak foundational learning narrows the
pipeline into later education and training, weakens the supply of adaptable workers, and reduces the returns to
subsequent skills investments. Addressing learning poverty and progression constraints in basic education is central to
the foundations on which higher productivity and better jobs depend.
11. A regional response is warranted because many constraints to reducing learning poverty are shared across
countries, while the costs of generating, adapting, and applying relevant knowledge are often too high for countries
to bear efficiently on their own. Regional platforms, public goods, common standards, and cross-country learning can
reduce duplication, lower costs, and help countries move more quickly from promising evidence to practical
implementation. Experience from regional initiatives, including the Africa Centers of Excellence (ACE), shows the value
of collaboration in supporting peer exchange, practical learning, and adaptation across diverse country contexts. This
is particularly important in basic education, where countries face similar reform challenges but often work in isolation.
12. The operation will finance a lean regional knowledge and collaboration mechanism, coordinated by AAU,
together with peer learning and technical support to help countries use evidence and regional public goods more
effectively. The mechanism will build on existing regional initiatives, platforms, and partner resources wherever
feasible, and will be supported by a simple digital repository or online workspace only to the extent needed to make
selected tools, standards, evidence, and practical resources easier to find and use. AAU will serve as the regional
coordinator and facilitator for this mechanism, rather than as a stand-alone technical platform or content production
entity. Where important gaps remain, the operation will support targeted development of additional shared resources
that can reduce duplication, lower costs, and strengthen implementation across participating countries. This will help
countries move from evidence generation and regional exchange to practical action in basic education reforms.

Component 1: Connecting Countries to Regional Public Goods for Basic Education Reform (US$1.5 million)
20. This component will help participating countries identify, access, adapt, and use selected regional public goods
and standards to strengthen basic education reforms. Given the limited size of the grant and the number of
participating countries, the component will not finance a comprehensive set of new regional public goods or largescale national implementation. It will instead operate as a connector to existing regional resources and specialized
institutions, and finance the preparatory work needed to adapt those resources for country use. Targeted development
of additional shared resources will be financed only where country demand is clear and existing tools are insufficient.
In doing so, the component will help turn regional initiatives and frameworks into practical country-facing resources,
while generating feedback from country implementation that can inform future regional dialogue and institutional
support.
21. The component will begin each year with a demand and public goods mapping exercise. AAU will work with
participating countries and specialized regional partners to identify priority reform areas where regional public goods
can support country implementation. This process will draw on a regularly updated map of existing regional public
goods and initiatives (see Annex 1 for examples of current regional public goods and initiatives) and will be reviewed
during annual work planning to reflect country demand, partner capacity, and emerging regional initiatives. Annual
work planning will apply a prioritization filter. Candidate activities will be assessed against: (i) relevance to major drivers
of learning poverty, access, progression, or system resilience; (ii) demand from more than one participating country;
(iii) availability and quality of existing regional public goods or partner expertise; (iv) feasibility within the regional grant
envelope; and (v) a clear pathway to country uptake, implementation, or follow-on financing. The exercise will review
existing tools, standards, guidance, evidence products, assessment resources, teacher support materials, and
implementation models from African-led and regional initiatives, including resources relevant to climate resilient

countries interested in adapting them, the technical partners to be engaged, and the follow-on activities that could be
financed through AIM4Learning country projects or partner resources.
22. Based on this mapping, the component will finance a small number of uptake and adaptation packages in
priority areas identified by participating countries. These packages may cover learning assessment and data use,
structured pedagogy and teacher support, lower secondary expansion, gender-responsive and inclusive education

cost implications, and contextual adaptability of the selected regional public goods.

programs, or partner resources.

E. Lessons Learned Reflected in the Project Design
37. The design of the regional grant is informed by the analytical frameworks and evaluation findings that underpin
the AIM4Learning MPA, including the 2018 World Development Report on Realizing Education’s Promise, the World
Bank’s Literacy Policy Package, work on cost-effective learning spaces, the World Bank Education Technology
Approach, and the 2024 Independent Evaluation Group review of World Bank support for basic education. These
sources emphasize the need to focus education systems on learning, strengthen assessment and data use, support
teachers more effectively, align reforms across system functions, and monitor whether interventions are being
implemented as intended. For this regional grant, the main lesson is that evidence and regional learning only add value

Page 22
when they are translated into practical tools, implementation support, and decisions that help country programs
improve reform design and delivery.
38. Experience from regional operations also shows that regional learning agendas are most useful when they are
selective, demand driven, and grounded in common implementation challenges. The project will use annual work
planning to agree on priority questions with participating countries and align support with the approved AIM4Learning
learning agenda. This approach will help ensure that research, diagnostics, and learning exchanges focus on practical
reform issues, including learning assessment, teacher support, lower secondary progression, gender-responsive
teacher workforce and school leadership reforms, climate-related disruptions, and the use of evidence in country
education systems.
39. The project design also reflects lessons on the importance of linking evidence generation to uptake.
Implementation research can help countries identify bottlenecks during rollout and adjust programs while reforms are
still being implemented. Communities of practice and regional problem-solving forums can support peer learning when
they bring together policymakers, implementers, assessment agencies, researchers, and technical partners around
common problems. Targeted diagnostics can support the uptake of regional public goods by identifying readiness gaps
and adaptation needs. These lessons are reflected in the project’s focus on uptake and adaptation packages, practical
implementation notes, documented follow-up actions, and the use of evidence and regional public goods in policy,
program design, and implementation decisions.
40. Experience from the ACE and other regional operations suggests that capacity building must be treated as a
core objective of regional operations and be tracked to facilitate achievement of target results. Furthermore, there
should be strong ownership of the capacity building undertaken. The project reflects this lesson by including targeted
capacity-building activities to strengthen AAU’s coordination, management, monitoring, and reporting functions under
Component 3. The capacity building activities under Component 3 will be monitored through the results framework.

will build on AAU’s existing project management systems and add only the targeted capacity needed to manage this
operation effectively. Core functions will include project coordination, annual work planning, procurement, financial
management, environmental and social risk management, monitoring and evaluation, stakeholder engagement,
partner coordination, and reporting. AAU is not expected to undertake most technical work directly. Rather, it will
commission, facilitate and manage inputs from specialized regional institutions, research networks, technical partners,
and service providers in line with the activities outlined in Components 1 and 2.

43. A Regional Steering Committee, comprising representatives of participating AIM4Learning MPA countries, will

IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY
A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis
Technical
48. The project design is technically sound and aligned with the AIM4Learning MPA’s regional learning and
collaboration pillar. It supports country implementation of reforms and interventions with a strong evidence base for
improving learning, expanding access, and strengthening progression in basic education. These include teacher
support, structured pedagogy, learning assessment and data use, digital tools, gender-responsive reforms, and
approaches to strengthening lower secondary progression where these support foundational learning and the wider
skills pathway. The regional operation addresses a key implementation challenge under the MPA by helping countries
adapt and apply evidence-informed approaches in their own education systems.
49. The design is appropriate for a small regional grant because it focuses on catalytic and gap-filling activities that
add value across countries. The operation will not seek to develop a comprehensive set of new regional public goods.
Instead, it will help countries identify, assess, adapt, and use existing regional public goods, standards, evidence, and
implementation resources, while financing targeted gap filling where clear demand exists and existing resources are
insufficient. This approach is consistent with the PDO because it strengthens regional collaboration and the use of
evidence and regional public goods to inform basic education reforms.
50. The implementation approach is realistic because activities will be selective, demand driven, and linked to
country-level implementation opportunities. Annual work planning will identify the priority reform areas, countries,
regional public goods, technical partners, and follow-on activities to be supported. The regional grant will finance a
limited number of joint research studies, targeted diagnostics, technical preparation, implementation learning,
evidence uptake, and selective gap filling where these activities can inform decisions across more than one country.
Larger implementation activities, including national rollout, full assessment implementation, or large data collection
exercises, are expected to be financed through country operations, government programs, or partner resources. This
design allows the regional grant to strengthen evidence use and practical learning while drawing on existing regional
initiatives and specialized institutions.

51. The economic rationale for the regional operation rests on its contribution to the effectiveness, efficiency, and
scalability of the AIM4Learning MPA. The MPA’s economic analysis remains relevant for this project and finds that the
expected returns to improved learning, higher completion, and expanded access to lower secondary education are
substantial. Reductions in learning poverty and increases in basic education completion are expected to raise
employment and earnings over time, while improved access to lower secondary education, especially for girls, is
expected to generate additional benefits through better health, lower rates of child marriage and adolescent
pregnancy, and higher labor force participation. The regional operation will contribute to these benefits by helping
countries adopt, adapt, and implement evidence-informed reforms more effectively.
52. The project is expected to generate economic benefits through three main channels. First, it will reduce
duplication by helping countries identify and use existing regional public goods, standards, tools, and evidence rather
than developing separate resources independently. Second, it will support better policy and implementation decisions
by financing joint research studies, implementation learning, targeted diagnostics, and evidence uptake linked to
country reform priorities. Third, it will strengthen regional collaboration and peer learning, allowing countries to share
implementation experience, avoid repeated mistakes, and adapt effective approaches more quickly. These channels
are especially important in a context of tightening fiscal space, where countries need to prioritize approaches with
stronger evidence, lower transaction costs, and clearer pathways to scale.
53. Public financing is justified because the project supports regional public goods and coordination functions that
are unlikely to be financed adequately by individual countries. The benefits of shared evidence, common tools,
regional learning, and knowledge platforms extend beyond any single country, creating positive spillovers across the
MPA and the wider region. The operation also addresses coordination failures by connecting country demand with
regional initiatives, specialized institutions, and partner resources. A regional grant is appropriate because it can help
countries connect to existing regional expertise, adapt shared tools to their own systems, and avoid duplicating work
across separate country operations. The project’s regional value added is not limited to cost sharing; it will also create
a structured mechanism through which regional institutions, technical partners, and participating countries can
connect regional public goods to country implementation.
Gender Responsiveness
54. The project will be gender responsive by addressing a specific evidence and implementation gap related to girls’
education and the female education workforce. Girls in the region face persistent barriers linked to distance, safety
concerns, school-related gender-based violence, household responsibilities, early marriage, adolescent pregnancy, and
direct and opportunity costs. These barriers are reinforced by the limited availability of female teachers and school
leaders, who can serve as role models, mentors, and sources of support for adolescent girls. The project will not directly
finance teacher recruitment, school leadership appointments, school-level service delivery, or gender-based violence
prevention and response services. Instead, it will help countries generate, adapt, and use evidence and regional public
goods that can inform gender-responsive teacher workforce and school leadership reforms. Under Component 1, the
project will finance at least one uptake and adaptation package on gender-responsive teacher workforce and
leadership reforms, covering female teacher recruitment, deployment, retention, professional development, and
women’s progression into school leadership. Under Component 2, the regional learning agenda will include
implementation research, diagnostics, evidence synthesis, or peer learning on gender-responsive approaches that can
improve girls’ retention, progression, and learning, with attention to the role of female teachers and school leaders.
These outputs will be discussed through project-supported learning mechanisms and used to inform country policy,
program design, implementation planning, or follow-on financing through country projects, government programs, or
partner resources. For the purposes of the Results Framework indicator, implementation may include early
implementation steps, including adoption or dissemination of national guidelines, rollout of training or orientation for
relevant staff, integration of female teacher recruitment, retention, or leadership measures into teacher workforce

Page 26
plans or programs, or inclusion of related actions in country project workplans or government implementation plans.
Progress will be monitored through the Results Framework indicator on the number of countries that develop and
implement national policies for improving recruitment and retention practices for female teachers and school leaders.
Citizen Engagement
55. Citizen engagement under the regional operation will focus on ensuring that project-supported regional public
goods, learning activities, and technical support respond to the needs of participating countries and intended users.
Because the project will not directly finance school-level service delivery, feedback will be gathered primarily from
participating ministries, implementation units, assessment agencies, teacher support institutions, researchers,
technical partners, and users of the regional knowledge and collaboration platform. Feedback mechanisms will be
embedded in communities of practice, regional problem-solving forums, technical workshops, platform user feedback
channels, and periodic stakeholder consultations led by AAU. Feedback will be reviewed during annual work planning,
Regional Steering Committee meetings, and the midterm review to adjust priorities, improve the usefulness of projectsupported tools and evidence products, and strengthen the uptake of regional public goods in country reform
processes. Progress will be tracked through a citizen engagement indicator measuring the number of annual work plans
that document stakeholder feedback and show how this feedback informed project priorities, platform improvements,
or learning activities.
Disability and Inclusion
56. The regional operation will support disability inclusion by helping participating countries use evidence, regional
public goods, and technical exchange to strengthen inclusive basic education reforms. While country projects will
finance direct interventions, including accessible infrastructure, inclusive teacher training, curriculum adaptations, and
support to learners with disabilities, the regional grant will focus on the shared tools and learning needed to improve
implementation across countries. Under Component 1, the project will help identify, adapt, and disseminate relevant
regional resources on inclusive education, including guidance on accessible learning materials, disability-inclusive
teacher support, data use, and reasonable accommodation in schools. Under Component 2, joint studies, diagnostics,
communities of practice, and implementation notes will consider disability inclusion where relevant and promote the
use of disability-disaggregated data where available. The regional knowledge and collaboration platform and projectsupported learning activities will also be designed to be accessible to users with disabilities, consistent with the
project’s focus on inclusive participation and use.
Climate and Disaster Risks
57. The project will strengthen participating countries’ capacity to adapt education systems to climate risks. Its
design responds to priorities identified in CCDRs across the region, including climate-resilient education management,
learning continuity during climate shocks, and integration of climate adaptation content into curricula and teacher
training. These priorities are addressed through project activities that support regional public goods, practical tools,
evidence generation, and cross-country learning.
58. Paris Alignment. The project activities are considered universally aligned with the Paris Agreement on both
adaptation and mitigation because they consist of technical assistance, knowledge sharing, research, coordination, and
capacity building activities, and do not finance physical investments.
59. Assessment and reduction of adaptation risks. On adaptation, the project will build climate resilience through
technical assistance that strengthens the adaptive capacity of education systems across the region. Although it will not
finance infrastructure, the project will support access to regional public goods, including tools for climate risk
monitoring, emergency response planning, and learning continuity during climate disruptions. It will also support crosscountry knowledge exchange on climate-responsive education and the integration of climate adaptation content into

Page 27
curricula and teacher professional development. Through research, policy analysis, and regional collaboration, the
project will contribute to the adaptation priorities reflected in participating countries’ Nationally Determined
Contributions and National Adaptation Plans.
60. Assessment and reduction of mitigation risks. On mitigation, the project’s activities consist of technical
assistance, knowledge exchange, and research, with a negligible greenhouse gas footprint. The project will contribute
to mitigation goals by supporting education reforms that build the human capital foundation for sustainable, resilient,
and inclusive development. CCDRs in the AFE region note that occupations in low carbon and climate-resilient sectors
generally require stronger cognitive skills than other jobs, and that skills for climate-resilient livelihoods are a
development priority. By helping countries strengthen foundational learning and access climate-responsive curricula
through regional public goods, the project will support the transition to a low carbon economy by helping equip the
future workforce with analytical and problem-solving skills.

Directive for Investment Project Financing, the Bank Guidance on Financial Management in World Bank Investment

Bank funded projects. The AAU will open and maintain (i) one US Dollar account to receive funds from the World Bank
and (ii) one Ghanaian cedi denominated operational account for day-to-day transactions to minimize the impact of
