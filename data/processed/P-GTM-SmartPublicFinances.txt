I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT
1.
This Program Appraisal Document (PAD) outlines a proposal for a Multiphase Programmatic Approach (MPA)
to support the Smart Public Finances: Data-driven Public Expenditure Management for Greater Efficiency Program. The
Program aims to enhance the effectiveness, efficiency, and accountability of public spending in participating Latin America
and the Caribbean (LAC) countries. The total financing amount of the MPA is US$140 million. Phase 1 will allocate US$50
million out of this for an investment operation in Guatemala (as set forth under Annex 1 of this PAD). The remainder of
the MPA financing will be allocated to the other participating countries in subsequent phases. There will be separate legal
agreements with each country.
2.
Governments are under increasing pressure to deliver improved public services across infrastructure, health,
education, and citizen security, while operating within tightening fiscal constraints. Rising debt levels, rigid expenditure
commitments, and heightened exposure to external shocks have reduced fiscal space in many countries in the LAC region,
limiting the scope for additional spending. In this context, enhancing the productivity of public expenditure—ensuring that
each unit of spending yields greater and more outputs and equitable results—has become essential. When resources are
strategically allocated and managed effectively, they can act as a catalyst for improved public service delivery and foster
inclusive economic growth and job creation.
3.
Improving Public Financial Management (PFM) systems is key for better governance and a predictable economic
environment. Weak PFM systems make it hard for governments to translate budgets into effective action, monitor results,
and maintain public trust. Robust PFM systems and practices enable the credible formulation and execution of budgets,
human resource and procurement management, and reinforce transparency and fiscal discipline. This enhances resource
flows to key sectors such as health, education, and security, allows access to reliable data for better decisions, providing
a foundation for better governance. They also help foster a transparent and predictable economic environment needed
to attract private investment and expand employment opportunities.
4.
The MPA is designed to address these challenges through a dual approach. It offers a menu of reform options
from foundational compliance and fiscal control measures to advanced strategies that improve spending efficiency and
sustainability, tailored to each country’s institutional context. It also facilitates cross-country learning and collaboration,
leveraging the World Bank Group’s (WBG) longstanding experience in supporting PFM reforms.
A. Regional Context
5.
The 2025 WBG LAC Economic Review1 highlights a need for LAC countries to create more fiscal space by
improving efficiency, reducing spending, and increasing revenues. Despite the recent recovery, economic growth is low,
with a forecast of 2.1 percent of GDP growth for 2025, making LAC the lowest growth region globally. Inflation is remaining
high longer than expected, limiting governments’ ability to cut interest rates and loosen monetary policy. Simultaneously,
government spending, deficits, and debt levels are often elevated – often due to high interest rates on the expanded stock
of post-COVID debt. Progress on debt reduction remains limited, with the debt-to-GDP ratio rising to 63.3 percent in 2024.
Poverty and inequality reduction have stalled, with monetary poverty estimated at 24.4 percent2 and most countries
above the WBG high inequality threshold3 at the end of 2024. This situation may deteriorate as increased return migration
flows may strain labor markets and resources for reintegration, overseas development assistance are decreasing, and
important remittance flows may lower. While not all challenges affect all countries equally, every country faces multiple
challenges, pointing to a universal need to generate fiscal space through increased public spending productivity.

6.
Guatemala and Honduras face significant socioeconomic challenges. Poverty remains high—57.3 percent in
Guatemala and 49.3 percent in Honduras. Access to education is limited, especially in rural areas, perpetuating poverty
and constraining economic opportunities. Underfunded health systems drive persistent disparities. Formal job creation
lags labor force growth, fueling large informal sectors4, high unemployment and underemployment, particularly among
youth and women. These pressures expose institutional weaknesses and erode public trust, hindering effective
governance and economic development as citizens question governments’ ability to manage resources responsibly.
7.
Countries of the subregion face fiscal constraints and risks. They have limited fiscal space due to historically low
tax revenues and post-pandemic fiscal consolidation. Remittances are among the main sources of household income and
drive much of the countries’ spending – remittances amount to 19.4 percent of GDP in Guatemala and 26 percent in
Honduras in 2023. A decline in these inflows could considerably reduce Value-Added Tax (VAT) collections, which account
for 49 percent of the government’s net income in Guatemala and 33.7 percent in Honduras, in 2023. Extreme weather
events pose an additional risk that can cause significant infrastructure damage and economic losses. In this context,
effective, efficient and accountable public spending is vital to ensure resources are directed to areas of greatest need and
impact. By investing in high-impact projects, efficient spending can drive growth, enhance productivity, competitiveness,
and climate resilience, and reduce poverty and inequality through social programs for vulnerable populations.
B. Sectoral and Institutional Context
8.
PEFA scores in Central America underscore persistent weaknesses in key areas and a need for tailored reform
strategies that align with each country’s reform maturity. Robust PFM systems address three main priorities: establishing
financial compliance and fiscal control, ensuring fiscal stability and sustainability, and promoting efficiency and
effectiveness in service delivery.5 These priorities form a hierarchy where compliance and stability are prerequisites for
effective service delivery. Before advancing beyond core functions, it is crucial for governments to establish solid
information ecosystems, accounting systems, and regulatory frameworks. Despite past reform efforts and good progress
in some areas, recent PEFA assessments6 across Central America point to several persistent weaknesses. Low scores in
budget reliability, particularly concerning expenditure composition outturn, suggest a notable variance between budgeted
and actual expenditures, potentially disrupting service delivery and hindering policy objectives. Scores for predictability
and control of budget execution, along with accounting and reporting—particularly the availability and timeliness of inyear budget reports—are at basic or below levels. Similarly, the availability, coverage, and timeliness of performance
information on public service delivery are at or below basic levels. These findings highlight the urgent need for tailored
reforms to strengthen PFM practices and improve overall governance.
9.
Core PFM information systems suffer from fragmentation, obsolescence, and weak institutional support. While
the adoption of information systems for budgeting and treasury functions has advanced in much of LAC7, WBG
assessments in Guatemala, Honduras and other countries highlight persistent challenges.8 Reforms have often been
sporadic, lacking the continuity and integration needed for systemic impact. Limited interoperability between systems has
led to manual processes, duplicate records, and inefficiencies in data flows. In many cases, the monitoring and evaluation
(M&E) phase of the budget process is underdeveloped, limiting the availability of performance data for decision-making
and weakening transparency. Additionally, the development and use of performance indicators remain inconsistent,
undermining the assessment of whether public expenditures achieve their intended results. Poor system maintenance,

10.
The limited use of data and data analytics for policymaking and government operations leads to shortcomings.
Although many LAC governments have collected extensive administrative data for years and invested in management
information systems (MIS), they rarely harness this information to improve public sector performance. A recent World
Bank report10 found that 96 percent of countries in the region rely primarily on descriptive analytics—used mainly for
compliance—while the use of diagnostic and predictive analytics remains minimal.11 Even where real-time data systems
exist, challenges related to data quality, accessibility, and limited analytical capacity constrain their effectiveness. Crosscutting areas such as public investment, procurement, PFM, and human resources remain particularly underutilized.
Another missed opportunity is the limited use of data analytics in incorporating gender, social inclusion, and climate
considerations into budgeting and other PFM functions. Governments also struggle to build and retain analytical capacity
due to the lack of structured career paths for data professionals, insufficient training opportunities, and inadequate
resourcing. Strengthening data systems and analytical capabilities represents a major opportunity to improve decisionmaking, increase efficiency, and enhance service delivery outcomes.
11.
A suboptimal allocation and utilization of resources in critical sectors, such as education, health, and citizen
security, results in shortcomings in service delivery. Weaknesses in PFM systems cause inefficiencies in budgetary
control, cash flow management, and resource allocation resulting in budget overruns and unsustainable government debt.
They also hamper domestic revenue mobilization, execution of spending plans, and accountability, ultimately affecting
the quality and accessibility of essential public services. For instance, in the education sector, fragmented responsibilities
and a large wage bill limit fiscal space for quality improvements, particularly in underperforming schools and lagging
regions. Similarly, in the health sector, inadequate allocation of resources creates inefficiencies, leading to insufficient
supplies and stockouts.
12.
Strengthening PFM systems offers a significant opportunity to address many of the weaknesses outlined above.
A World Bank review of 160 operations in 84 countries over four decades12 found that 88 percent of completed projects
resulted in functional systems, even in complex institutional environments. These experiences highlight critical success
factors: clearly defined problems, alignment with institutional contexts, strong reform ownership, practical system design,
phased implementation, comprehensive training, and robust monitoring. Guatemala’s Sistema Integrado de
Administración Financiera Pública (SIAF), for example, stands out as a durable and adaptable model that has informed
reforms in other countries in the region and remained operational for more than two decades. These global and regional
lessons have informed the design of this MPA, which aims to strengthen the integrity, interoperability, and analyticsreadiness of PFM systems to support a more transparent, efficient, and accountable use of public resources.
C. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives
13.
The MPA aligns with the WBG's strategy to drive development and reduce poverty by creating jobs. This
objective is achieved by improving the productivity of public spending and encouraging private sector growth, particularly
through advancements in PIM, HRM, and public procurement, as well as through contributing to establishing a robust
fiscal enabling environment for growth and job creation. Strategic and well managed public investment in infrastructure—
such as transportation networks, reliable energy grids, and high-speed internet — helps enhance private business
efficiency. Well managed public procurement can allow public spending to support job-intensive activities in the

14.
The MPA aligns with the World Bank Group’s goals of poverty reduction and shared prosperity on a livable

improving service delivery in participating countries, in alignment with their national development plans. In Guatemala,
it supports the 2024-2028 General Government Policy (Política General de Gobierno), particularly its first strategic pillar,
“toward a legitimate and effective public service”, which emphasizes a transparent, efficient, and accountable public
administration by strengthening planning and budgeting processes. The MPA’s focus on modernizing public financial

D. Multiphase Programmatic Approach
(i)
Rationale for using MPA
16.
The rationale for selecting the MPA for the implementation of the Smart Public Finances Program centers on
enhancing efficiency and impact. Specifically, it aims to establish a long-term, streamlined, scalable, and replicable
framework to lower transaction costs in the preparation and implementation of interventions, and promote a learning
agenda that amplifies results. While national PFM ecosystems differ, countries face common challenges within similar
economic and institutional contexts, allowing best practices to be replicated and adapted as needed. The MPA fosters
collaboration by creating a shared development initiative where countries can align efforts while maintaining flexibility to
address their specific needs. Key benefits of the MPA include better project design by avoiding siloed operations, greater
adaptability to evolving priorities through a phased approach, and economies of scale by aggregating resources and
replicating successful reforms, solutions, and practices. The MPA also strengthens cooperation by leveraging Technical
Assistance (TA) and fostering cross-country learning, ensuring that solutions are both innovative and contextually relevant.
17.
The MPA’s design promotes agility, cross-country alignment, and continuous learning to maximize the
effectiveness and sustainability of reforms. Participating countries are aligned around common objectives and, where
feasible, sharing of knowledge and design principles to harness synergies and avoid inefficiencies of fragmented
implementation. An adaptive approach allows the MPA to integrate lessons across phases and tailor financing, and
implementation to country-specific needs and absorption capacity. This flexibility strengthens procurement, execution,
and the overall effectiveness of interventions over time. By embedding continuous learning and feedback mechanisms,
the MPA remains agile, responsive, and capable of delivering sustained impact at scale, aligning with the Better Bank
agenda’s commitment to improving development outcomes.

Program Development Objective
19.
The Program Development Objective (PrDO) is to enhance effectiveness, efficiency, and accountability of public
expenditures. It will be monitored by measuring progress on four PrDO indicators across the MPA’s outcomes and learning
agenda (Table 1). Each participating country contributes to the PrDO indicators in a customized way by choosing its Project
Development Objective (PDO) Indicators from a list of discreet PEFA indicators selected for their achievability and
potential for impact based on prior PEFA assessments and recent World Bank country PFM ecosystem assessments.
Countries may select one or more PDO indicators that correspond to the different PrDO Indicators.
20.
Public expenditure effectiveness is defined as the extent to which government spending successfully produces
intended results. This outcome is measured via a PrDO indicator: “Improved elements of public expenditure effectiveness”
encompassing three possible PDO indicators: (i) Aggregate Expenditure Outturn (PEFA PI-1); (ii) Performance information
for service delivery (PEFA PI-8); and (iii) Medium term perspective in expenditure budget (PEFA PI-16).
21.
Public expenditure efficiency is defined as the degree to which government spending achieves maximum
possible output from a given level of public funds. This outcome is measured via a PrDO indicator: “Improved elements
of public expenditure efficiency” encompassing five possible PDO indicators: (i) Expenditure Composition Outturn (PEFA
PI-2); (ii) Public Investment Management (PEFA PI-11); (iii) Payroll Controls (PEFA PI-23); (iv) Procurement (PEFA PI-24);
and (v) Internal controls on non-salary expenditure (PEFA PI-25).

(iii)
Program Framework
23.
The MPA aims to achieve the PrDO by developing the foundations for and deploying smart, data-driven PFM
solutions. It addresses core challenges related to the effectiveness, efficiency, and accountability of public resource use.
These include the suboptimal allocation and utilization of public spending in strategic sectors, the limited adoption of
evidence and data analytics in policymaking and operations, and the technical vulnerabilities that constrain existing PFM
ecosystems. To respond to these challenges, the MPA supports interventions across four pillars: (i) strengthening the

26.
World Bank assessments of PFM ecosystems provide critical inputs to the Learning Agenda by highlighting the
foundational elements required for successful reform: inclusive governance structures, robust institutional capacities,
and sustainable information systems. These assessments emphasize that establishing an effective governance framework
and achieving consensus on an integrated conceptual model are essential steps toward a unified vision of the PFM system.
Strengthening institutional capacity—through skilled personnel and adaptable structures—is vital for translating policy
into practice and responding to shifting priorities. Sustainable information ecosystems serve as the backbone of
government financial operations, enabling transparency, accountability, and operational efficiency. For these systems to
be effective, they must be adaptable, user-centered, and built on strong digital infrastructure. Finally, safeguarding data
security and privacy is indispensable for protecting sensitive information and maintaining public trust. These insights form
the analytical foundation of the Learning Agenda, guiding the design of TA, peer learning, and knowledge generation
activities under the MPA.

insights from a diverse range of participants. By tailoring operational arrangements to each cluster, the CoP will

E.  Lessons Learned
39.
The MPA draws on the Bank’s vast experience and accumulated lessons from supporting PFM reform across
diverse country contexts. The World Bank Independent Evaluation Group’s (IEG) working paper “Lessons from Reforming
Financial Management Information Systems: A Review of the Evidence” (World Bank, 2018), which evaluates the WBG’s
experience with 134 IFMIS projects across 74 countries since 1985 offers important findings that directly inform the design
of this operation. These include: (i) to clearly determine the rationale for implementing new systems and identifying
specific problems to address; (ii) to look beyond technical robustness to policy and institutional environments and key
preconditions, such as a Treasury Single Account (TSA) and consistent budget classification structures across levels of
government; (iii) that reform commitment is an essential success factor for implementation; (iv) for system designs to
derive from functional requirements of governments rather than technical considerations; (v) for phased approaches to
implementation, prioritizing fundamental modules necessary for budget execution and reporting; (vi) for sequenced
approaches for costly and logistically complex system deployments; (vii) for essential technical training and training for
end users and managers throughout system lifecycles; (viii) that implementing advanced budgeting methodologies
without operational budget execution systems is unlikely to achieve significant results; (ix) that IFMIS reform projects tend
to be costly and lengthy and should be realistically appraised and adequate lending instruments be chosen; and (x) that
the attribution of PFM outcomes can be facilitated through adequate monitoring and evaluation frameworks.
40.
Building on these lessons, a new wave of reform under the MPA emphasizes several key interventions that
enhance sustainability. These include integrated PFM conceptual models covering core processes—such as budget
formulation, execution, and reporting—and their interaction with support processes across the budget cycle;
establishment of data governance models and standards that enable effective data utilization for decision-making;

adoption of enterprise architecture to align IT practices with business goals and processes; implementation of information
security policies that embed security measures from the design phase; development of inclusive PFM governance models
that accommodate all stakeholders and provide mechanisms for dialogue and resolution; and permanent capacity building
programs designed to mitigate the impact of talent turnover.
41.
Holistic PFM assessments serve as analytical underpinning and inform the design of the country-specific and
regional MPA interventions. Based on prior reform experiences, the Bank conducted comprehensive PFM ecosystem

comprehensive modernization of PFM ecosystems is therefore required, incorporating best practices such as results-based
approaches, multi-year planning, and differentiated financial operations recording to ensure effective functioning and
continuity of critical government operations. Dialogue with government counterparts also revealed a strong demand for
cross-country learning and knowledge exchange across multiple dimensions of PFM reform.

The operation draws on lessons from other recent MPAs. The Brazil Progestão Program (P181608) surfaces

implementation support; (vi) coordination among implementing agencies; and (vii) alignment of project M&E with

operational synergies by standardizing technical documentation—such as implementation agreements, operations
manuals, terms of reference, and bidding documents—while allowing flexibility for country-specific customization.

STRATEGIC CONTEXT
A. Country Context
1. Guatemala is the largest economy in Central America, with a total output of US$113 billion in 2024. However, its
Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita significantly trails behind that of Costa Rica and Panama.1 The country has
experienced steady growth, averaging 3.2 percent annually between 2014 and 2023, outpacing the regional average,
thanks to prudent fiscal and monetary management. However, this economic expansion has not led to a significant
reduction in poverty. As of 2023, around 55 percent of the population lived in poverty, and the informal economy
accounted for 23.3 percent of GDP, with 71.1 percent of the workforce employed in informal sectors.2 Structural
challenges, such as limited access to basic services and productive job opportunities, along with frequent natural disasters,
are major causes of the country’s high poverty rates. Remittances, which totaled US$19.8 billion in 2023 (19.4 percent of
GDP), play a crucial role in sustaining household consumption. Since remittance income drives much of the country's
spending, any decline in these inflows could reduce Value-Added Tax (VAT) collections, which currently generate 47.8
percent of the government’s net income, creating fiscal vulnerabilities.
2. Guatemala’s main challenge is to increase infrastructure investment and social spending to boost growth and
reduce poverty without jeopardizing fiscal responsibility. Guatemala’s revenue and expenditures are insufficient for the
country to address its significant infrastructure deficit and accelerate poverty reduction. In fiscal year 2024, central
government revenue and expenditures averaged 11.8 percent and 14.1 percent of GDP, respectively. Public investment
was low, averaging 2.7 percent of GDP in 20233, which is below the levels of comparable countries. Between 2018 and
2022, social spending averaged 6.5 percent of GDP and accounted for 45 percent of total expenditure. In comparison,
social spending in other Central American countries and the Dominican Republic averaged 8.6 percent of GDP during the
same period. Both the government and private sector recognize the need to invest in infrastructure. There is a consensus
around improving transportation infrastructure, including rural roads, ports, railways and airports.
B. Sectoral and Institutional Context
3. Financial management in Guatemala faces ongoing challenges in resource mobilization and public expenditure
effectiveness. While there have been modest gains in tax collection, significant issues remain in ensuring efficiency,
sustainability, and growth. PFM functions, such as budgeting, accounting, treasury, human resources, public investment,
asset management, and procurement, require strengthening to enhance performance. Inefficiencies in key sectors like
education, health, security, and infrastructure hinder service quality, compounded by outdated information systems and
limited use of data for decision-making. Moreover, weak accountability and transparency have further undermined public
trust, highlighting the urgent need for comprehensive reforms to ensure efficient, transparent, and equitable use of public
resources. These systemic challenges underscore the urgent need for comprehensive reforms in public expenditure
management to enhance efficiency, transparency, and accountability, ensuring that public resources are used effectively
to promote sustainable development and reduce poverty.

public investment resources, improving efficiency in spending is critical. Guatemala performs relatively well in
institutional design, particularly in areas such as fiscal stability, project formulation standards, and rules protecting

outputs has not evolved into performance-based planning, as resource distribution remains incremental, and performance
monitoring is minimal. Entities comply with reporting requirements but do not apply a results-oriented approach due to
the complexity of input-output design. The reliance on an annual budget framework limits medium-term planning and

C. Rationale for Bank Involvement and Role of Partners
30. The World Bank began supporting PFM reforms in Guatemala in 1995 through several investment projects, with
the first two earning positive ratings. The first project, SIAF I, aimed to modernize financial administration. Subsequent
projects, SIAF II and SIAF III, added new modules and features. These efforts were intended to enhance the budget cycle's
efficiency, transparency, and accountability by automatically aggregating key governmental information and were
successful at the time of implementation. More recently, the Bank brings significant value through its ongoing technical
assistance and close engagement with MINFIN. This includes support in the development of a comprehensive diagnostic
and reform strategy of the PFM ecosystem. This reform strategy serves as a critical foundation for aligning the proposed
project’s interventions with priorities identified in the reform strategy, ensuring relevance, ownership, and impact.
31. The project also takes into consideration the ongoing support provided by development partners in related areas.
There is strong synergy between this project and parallel initiatives by the IMF and IDB to enhance PFM in Guatemala. The

D. Lessons Learned
32. A common reason for the failure of PFM reforms in previous projects in Guatemala is the lack of adequate
preparation and institutional stability within the implementing agency. To mitigate this risk, it is crucial for the project
team to secure strong, sustained commitment from senior government leadership demonstrated through consistency
and, where necessary, a willingness to make trade-offs. To address these challenges, it is essential not only to ensure that
reform champions remain in place throughout the project lifecycle, but also to establish incentives that sustain reforms
beyond individual tenures. In this regard, the Bank has maintained continuous engagement with a dedicated team within
MINFIN, which operates under clear directive from the Minister of Finance, who has identified this project as a key priority.
In addition, project implementation will be anchored in the Technical Budget Directorate (Dirección Técnica del
Presupuesto, DTP), ensuring strong client ownership and active participation in defining reform needs.
33. FMIS projects often falter when the foundational preconditions for PFM reforms are not thoroughly assessed.
Evidence from global experience shows that projects which neglect these critical preparatory steps frequently face delays,
scope reductions, or outright failure15. While there is no universal formula for sequencing or prioritizing FMIS-related
reforms, the importance of conducting an early diagnostic is widely recognized (World Bank, 2010). In the case of
Guatemala, this principle has been put into practice. Prior to project preparation, a comprehensive diagnostic and reform
strategy was completed. This has been instrumental in identifying priority areas and determining the appropriate
sequencing of interventions, grounded in the country’s institutional realities and reform maturity. The reform strategy
serves not only as a technical guide but also as a tool to align stakeholder expectations and mobilize political support to
ensure reform sustainability.

39. Gender. Gender considerations are integrated across components to ensure that PFM reforms contribute to inclusive
and equitable outcomes. The project will promote gender-responsive planning by integrating criteria that address

management systems will consider the different ways assets are used and benefit individuals based on gender. Efforts will
also include incorporating gender-disaggregated data into fiscal transparency tools, promoting gender-responsive
budgeting practices, and supporting women's participation in training, governance, and decision-making processes related
to public financial management. Additionally, the project aims to increase the number of municipalities that apply gender
tagging in their budgets through the implementation of improved financial management systems at the subnational level.
40. Citizen engagement. The project will incorporate citizen engagement mechanisms to strengthen transparency,

understanding and use. In addition, under Component 3, the communications and change management strategy will
include targeted outreach and public education campaigns to explain reforms and gather feedback from civil society,
including through virtual consultations and user satisfaction surveys. As part of these efforts, the project will support fullcycle consultations around the Citizen’s Budget, where citizens not only provide proposals but are also informed of how
their feedback was incorporated. These mechanisms will ensure that citizens, civil society organizations, and other
stakeholders can contribute to and influence the reform process.

considerations in the budgeting process, including improving data analytics capacities for green budget tagging and
analyzing data from previous budgets to inform climate-based budgeting for future years. The project will also increase
resilience and improve capacity to respond to climate-induced shocks and natural disasters by ensuring interoperability
and business continuity of information systems. It will contribute to reducing emissions by supporting the development
of methodological tools to screen public investment projects for climate change risk and mitigation and will promote the

project are increased heat waves and more frequent and intense extreme weather events, such as hurricanes and storms,
that might lead to floods and affect the capacity of systems that provide administrative services to continue operating
under such circumstances. To respond to such risks, the project will enhance institutional resilience and service continuity
through the development of an integrated information security and business continuity system. Additionally, the
screening identified that climate hazard risks are not expected to have material impact on the proposed MPA and
operation or its development objectives.
B. Economic Analysis
45. The economic benefits from the project arise from expenditure savings due to improvements in core government
management systems, policy, and spending reviews from the analysis of a large volume of data. Savings are generated
from the rationalization of government operational costs and improvements in efficiency and productivity in the core
systems supported by the project in the areas of human resources, procurement, investment management, and asset
management. The analysis does not consider second-order impacts from the reallocation of public funds to more
productive uses through improvements in the quality and policy alignment of spending or benefits associated with
increased investment project execution resulting from higher quality public investment projects. Nor does the analysis
quantify benefits from the modernization of government functions, such as improvements in the quality of public service,
transparency, and compliance.
46. The net present value of all project benefits, using a social discount rate of 4.5 percent, is estimated to be US$266.7
million over 10 years, with an internal rate of return of 98 percent. The discount rate follows the recommendation of a
recent study on 17 Latin American countries using the social rate of time preference (STP) method16 and is relatively close
to the social discount rates adopted by European countries (ranging between 3.5 and 5.5 percent). The cost-benefit
analysis has been calculated over a period of 10 years, as most of the benefits are only realized starting from the third
year of project implementation.

47. The economic analysis of the proposed reforms encompasses four key components. In Human Resources
Management, strategic workforce planning and payroll audits are expected to reduce payroll growth by 0.02 percent
annually starting in the fourth year, saving Q804.9 million (US$104.7 million) over a decade, while payroll audits could
yield a 0.3 percent wage bill reduction, saving Q255.2 million (US$33.2 million), with a combined net present value (NPV)
of Q785.9 million (US$102.2 million), excluding additional unquantified benefits from improved databases and the
elimination of duplicate benefits. In Procurement, modernization and process simplification are anticipated to cut
government purchase prices by 5 to 10 percent, leading to a 0.5 percent cost saving from the fourth year onward, totaling
Q1,528 million (US$198.8 million) over ten years and yielding an NPV of Q1,110 million (US$144.4 million). Asset
Management reforms, through better utilization and rationalization of state properties, aim to increase revenues and
reduce costs; in 2024 alone, Guatemala spent Q452 million (US$58.7 million) and generated Q608 million (US$79.1 million)
in real estate revenue, while future efficiency improvements are projected to produce a 3 percent net gain totaling Q222.8
million (US$28.9 million) over a decade, with an NPV of Q163.3 million (US$21.3 million). Lastly, enhancements in Public
Investment Management are expected to reduce cost overruns by 3 percent through improved project preparation and
approval processes, resulting in savings of Q445.2 million (US$57.9 million) from the fourth year to year ten, with an NPV
of Q325.9 million (US$42.3 million).

48. Budgeting, accounting and financial reporting arrangements. Bank financing will be incorporated into the MINFIN
institutional budget and will be controlled, recorded and managed through its institutional budgeting, accounting and
financial systems (Sistema Informático de Gestión, SIGES, and SICOIN). MINFIN prepares its annual budget following
national budgetary legislation and regulations; Bank funds will be incorporated into these budgeting instruments. The
Bank will require bi-annual interim unaudited financial reports (IFRs), which will be prepared by the DTP with information
retrieved from its institutional accounting and financial system.
49. Disbursement and Flow of Funds. Loan proceeds will be disbursed to a designated account (DA) to be created at the
National Treasury and opened at the Single Treasury Account in the Bank of Guatemala in US dollars in a segregated
account to be opened as a virtual Subsidiary Account (Cuenta Secundaria), under the Treasury Single Account for
Multilaterals’ Loans in US dollars. Funds deposited into the DA as advances will follow the World Bank’s disbursement
policies and procedures as described in the Loan Agreement and the DFIL. The project may also use the direct payments
method for disbursement. Advances made to the DA will be documented using Statements of Expenditures (SOEs) and
supporting documents as defined in the DFIL. The PIU will ensure that all expenditures reported in the SOEs are properly
recorded, documented, and reconciled with Project accounting and financial records.
