\abstract{No prior work benchmarks end-to-end risk-register generation
against real, published, human-authored ground truth across multiple large
language models; existing evaluations are almost entirely single-model and
rely on expert opinion rather than a genuine ground-truth artifact. We
address this gap with a benchmark of 3 models (one general-purpose
proprietary model and two architecturally distinct open-weight models)
$\times$ 3 prompting strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, structured reasoning)
$\times$ 21 real-world projects with both public planning documentation and
a published human risk register (18 World Bank Project Appraisal Documents,
3 UK government business cases), evaluated through a fully automated
pipeline: embedding-based semantic matching against ground truth as the
primary signal, and LLM-as-judge scoring as an explicitly supplementary,
non-human-substitute signal. Across 302 real, non-pilot runs, generated
registers recover 55.6\% of ground-truth risks on average (mean recall
0.556) while only 23.0\% of generated risks correspond to a real
ground-truth risk (mean precision 0.230); among matched pairs, the correct
one of eight risk categories is assigned 45.0\% of the time. Model choice
separates recall more cleanly than prompting strategy: the proprietary
model leads recall (0.612--0.693) with the fewest parse failures, while the
weaker-recall open-weight model instead has the highest category accuracy
and precision in most conditions, a conservative
generate-fewer-but-more-accurate failure mode distinct from the stronger
model's broader, noisier recall. Category-level analysis (RQ3) finds a
specific, non-uniform failure pattern rather than uniform weakness:
political/regulatory risk is the category models most often fail to
surface at all (191 real risks missed corpus-wide), while schedule risk is
generated as a near-default, safe category even where a project's real
register does not support it (238 hallucinated instances against zero real
misses). We release the corpus manifest, extraction and prompting code, and
evaluation pipeline, including a disclosed account of the free-tier
provider constraints that shaped the experimental timeline, so the
benchmark is reproducible rather than described only in the abstract.}

\section{Introduction}

Risk registers are a natural, high-value testbed for evaluating LLM
generation capability against a real, structured, professionally-authored
artifact. Unlike open-ended text generation, a risk register has a fixed,
checkable output contract (category, likelihood, impact, mitigation) and a
directly comparable human-authored counterpart already exists for many
completed or appraised projects, making semantic-matching evaluation
against genuine ground truth possible in a way it is not for many other
generative project-management tasks. At the same time, risk-register
quality is not reducible to surface fluency: a register that is
well-written but generic, or that hallucinates a plausible-sounding risk
with no basis in the actual project documentation, is a real practical
failure a project team could act on badly, which is exactly the failure
mode RQ3 is designed to surface rather than average away.

Large language models are increasingly proposed as tools for project
management tasks, yet the literature concentrates heavily on predictive
analytics for time, cost, and quality rather than generative capability
\cite{aidriven2026usecases}. Within the narrower body of work that does
study LLMs for project risk work specifically, evaluation has so far relied
on expert-opinion panels or author judgment rather than comparison against
real, published human-authored artifacts, has been almost entirely
single-model (typically ChatGPT only), and has not distinguished the
\emph{generation} of a full risk register from the narrower task of
\emph{classifying} already-identified risk items
\cite{asce2025exploring_chatgpt_risk,buildings2025risk_classification}.

This gap motivates the present study:
\begin{quote}
\emph{While recent studies have assessed ChatGPT's ability to discuss
project risks under expert evaluation, no work has benchmarked multiple
LLMs on end-to-end risk register generation from real project documents
against published human-authored registers, leaving both the accuracy and
the systematic failure modes of this capability unquantified.}
\end{quote}

We address it with three frozen research questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} How complete and accurate are LLM-generated risk
  registers compared to human-authored ones?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} How do results vary across three models (one
  general-purpose proprietary model and two distinct open-weight models)
  and three prompting strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, and structured
  reasoning)?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} Which risk categories do LLMs systematically miss or
  hallucinate? This failure-mode analysis is the paper's core contribution.
\end{itemize}

This study makes four contributions. First, to our knowledge it is the
first benchmark to evaluate end-to-end risk-register \emph{generation} --
not classification of already-identified risks -- against real, published,
human-authored ground truth rather than expert opinion alone. Second, it
compares three models spanning both a general-purpose proprietary system
and two architecturally distinct open-weight models under three prompting
strategies, where prior work is almost uniformly single-model. Third, it
performs a per-category failure-mode analysis (RQ3) that treats systematic
omission and hallucination as first-class findings rather than an aggregate
accuracy score. Fourth, it releases the corpus manifest, extraction and
prompting code, and evaluation pipeline, including a disclosed account of
the free-tier provider constraints that shaped the experimental timeline
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}), so the benchmark is reproducible rather than
described only in the abstract.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{AI in Project Management}
Systematic reviews of AI in project management consistently find that
generative applications are among the least-studied AI functions in the
field, with research concentrated instead on predictive analytics for
schedule, cost, and quality outcomes
\cite{aidriven2026usecases,aitools2025knowledge}. This pattern is not
specific to any one review: a broader survey covering opportunities,
enablers, and barriers across the 2013--2024 literature reaches the same
conclusion \cite{salimimoghadam2025rise}, as does a 97-study PRISMA review
of AI applications, challenges, and future directions
\cite{leveraging2025ai_pm} and a further systematic mapping of the AI
landscape in project-management contexts through late 2024
\cite{mapping2025ai_landscape}. A thematic review focused specifically on AI
and data analytics within project management information systems reports
the same predictive-over-generative emphasis inside agile PM tooling
\cite{jariwala2025pmis}, and a review of AI within open-innovation project
management similarly finds generative use cases under-represented relative
to decision-support and analytics applications \cite{openinnovation2024ai}.
Adoption itself is reported as limited by
practitioners' unfamiliarity with the tools rather than by demonstrated
capability gaps \cite{aitools2025knowledge}. Taken together, these reviews
establish that the imbalance motivating this study -- abundant predictive-AI
research, sparse generative-AI evaluation -- is a field-wide pattern rather
than an artifact of any single review's scope.

\subsection{LLMs for Project Risk Management}
The closest prior work evaluates ChatGPT against expert judgment on
construction-project risk identification and mitigation, finding strong
performance on generic, checklist-style risks but weaker performance on
context-specific risks -- typically the highest-severity ones
\cite{asce2025exploring_chatgpt_risk}. Related studies report the same
generic-strong/specific-weak split under expert or focus-group evaluation,
though their headline verdicts on ChatGPT differ: one finds moderate
overall performance \cite{aladag2023assessing_chatgpt}, while another --
itself an explicitly blind, anonymous-peer-review design -- finds
ChatGPT's scores exceed the human average on the same task, even as
reviewers still preferred human answers for practicality and specificity
\cite{ecam2024chatgpt_exceed_humans}. Further related work includes
one evaluation structured explicitly around the ISO 31000 risk-management
process rather than an ad hoc rubric \cite{esrel2023iso31000_chatgpt}, and
one that develops a fine-tuned agent (RiskGPT) evaluated on a small set of
case studies rather than a public benchmark \cite{wen2025riskgpt}. Adjacent
generative applications include LLM-assisted contract review via
knowledge-graph augmentation \cite{contractkg2023llm} and a direct
comparison of generative-AI versus human project managers on planning
tasks \cite{barcaui2023whoisbetter}, both of which support LLM use in
PM-adjacent generative work without evaluating risk-register generation
specifically. Across this line of work, three limitations recur:
single-model evaluation (almost always ChatGPT), no comparison against real
published ground-truth registers, and no publicly released, reproducible
benchmark.

\subsection{Risk Classification and Text Mining vs.\ Generation}
A closely related but distinct task is classifying already-identified risk
items into categories, which has been benchmarked across NLP and LLM
methods \cite{buildings2025risk_classification}. Pre-LLM text-mining
approaches to risk extraction form a further adjacent line of work: a
systematic review of text-mining analytics for supply-chain risk management
from online data \cite{textmining2025scrm}, and a scientometric analysis of
text mining and NLP specifically within construction research
\cite{textmining2026construction_scientometric}, both surveying extraction
and categorization pipelines that predate or do not use generative LLMs.
Classification and extraction are materially easier tasks than the one
studied here: they presuppose that the risks have already been identified
or are recoverable via pattern matching, whereas end-to-end register
generation requires a model to both identify and characterize risks from
raw, unstructured project documentation.

\subsection{In-Context Learning and Prompting Strategies}
RQ2's zero-shot/few-shot/structured-reasoning comparison is grounded in two
foundational results this study treats as background rather than novel
contribution: that large language models can perform new tasks from a small
number of in-context demonstrations without gradient updates
\cite{brown2020fewshot}, and that eliciting explicit intermediate reasoning
steps before a final answer measurably improves performance on tasks
requiring multi-step inference \cite{wei2022cot}. Risk-register generation
plausibly benefits from both mechanisms -- few-shot demonstrations convey
the expected output shape and level of specificity, while structured
reasoning may help a model connect a stated risk back to the specific
planning-document passage that motivates it -- but neither effect has been
measured for this task specifically, which is what RQ2 addresses.

\subsection{LLM Evaluation Methodology}
LLM-as-judge protocols are documented to be unstable across near-identical
prompts, subject to positional and self-enhancement bias, and poorly
calibrated in expert-knowledge domains
\cite{zheng2023judging_llm_judge,iui2025llmjudge_expert,nofreelabels2025}.
This motivates our decision (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) to treat LLM-as-judge
scoring as strictly supplementary to semantic matching against ground
truth, never as a stand-alone measure or a substitute for human judgment
\cite{llmsasjudges2024survey}. At least one source argues for Cohen's kappa
over raw percent agreement specifically when measuring judge-human
alignment \cite{thakur2024kappa} -- a comparison this study's fully
automated design (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) does not attempt, precisely
because treating an LLM judge's agreement with itself, rather than with an
independent human baseline, as a validity signal would repeat the same
self-enhancement bias this literature documents.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Corpus}
The corpus comprises 21 real-world projects with both public planning
documentation and a published, human-authored risk register: 18 World Bank
Project Appraisal Documents (PADs) and 3 UK government business cases
(Five Case Model format). Each candidate project was required to clear four
gate criteria: (1) a structured, rated risk table is present; (2) a
substantive, itemized narrative risk discussion beyond the table exists;
(3) the boundary between planning content and risk content is locatable and
independently re-verifiable against the source document, not merely a
previously recorded page range; and (4) at least 15 pages of planning
content survive excision of the risk content. Two UK candidates and one
World Bank candidate were read in full and excluded or set aside for
failing gate (2); one UK project (13 pages of surviving planning content)
is retained as a disclosed exception to gate (4) rather than silently
lowering the threshold. Both excluded UK candidates were verified by full
direct read rather than a sampled judgment: one business case (a nuclear
energy project) was found, on reading all 23 pages, to contain no
itemized or rated risk register anywhere -- only generic process language
about risk governance -- and was excluded for failing gate (1) as well as
gate (2). A second, HTML-only business case (an employment programme) was
found to contain exactly two named risks, both restatements of the same
underlying funding-timing issue, with no separate mitigation text for
either and no category diversity between them; it was excluded specifically
because it could not support the per-category recall/precision analysis
RQ3 depends on, not because it lacked a risk register outright. Both
exclusion decisions, and the full text supporting them, are recorded in the
released corpus manifest rather than only in an internal note. The 21
included projects span 18 distinct sector labels, among them social
protection, basic and accelerated-learning education, energy and
carbon-capture-and-storage, regional public health security, public
financial management, water, transport and agriculture, private-sector
competitiveness, venture finance, digital identity, and tourism -- a
deliberately cross-sector corpus rather than one concentrated in a single
domain such as construction, which is where nearly all prior LLM-risk
evaluation work is sited (Section~\ref{sec:related}). Table~\ref{tab:corpus}
lists all 21 included projects. Every source document is already public
(World Bank project pages or UK government publication pages); the corpus
manifest records the planning-document URL and the separate
ground-truth-register URL as distinct fields for every project, so the
planning/ground-truth boundary is explicit in the released manifest itself
and independently re-checkable by a reader, not only asserted in prose.

\subsection{Leakage Control}
Because several source documents contain the human risk register within the
same file as the planning narrative, we applied a manual leakage-control
protocol rather than automated section detection, which is unreliable
across document formats spanning many years. For each project, an author
located the risk section (e.g., the World Bank SORT risk table), excised it
from the text supplied to the models, and recorded the exact page range
removed; remaining narrative passages that restate the register's risks
were likewise removed. Every excision range was independently
re-verified against the source document itself before ground truth was
finalized, which caught three distinct under- and over-excision errors
during corpus construction. A standing, re-runnable audit tool independently
re-scans every document for heading and rated-table patterns, and checks
each ground-truth risk's description and mitigation text for an 8-word
sliding-window phrase or distinctive alphanumeric code (e.g., a World Bank
project identifier) that also recurs verbatim in that project's processed
planning text. This control has a known, disclosed false-positive rate
rather than a purely theoretical one: it flags one project whose
ground-truth mitigation and planning text share language identifying a
\emph{predecessor} project -- standard background text the source document
repeats over a dozen times across unrelated sections, not risk-specific
content, and confirmed on inspection to be a false positive. We report this
as the audit process working as intended -- surfacing a candidate for human
review rather than silently passing or excluding -- not as evidence the
corpus is leak-free by construction.

\subsection{Experimental Grid}
Three models -- one general-purpose proprietary model and two distinct
open-weight models -- are each evaluated under three prompting
strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, and structured reasoning) across all 21
projects, with 2 runs per cell at temperature 0--0.1, yielding a
189-cell $\times$ 2-run grid. Models receive planning/appraisal
documentation only; the ground-truth register for a given project never
enters that project's prompt or any few-shot example (few-shot
demonstrations are fully synthetic, constructed for this purpose, and never
drawn from a real corpus project).

\subsection{Prompting Strategy Design}
The three prompting strategies compared in RQ2 are grounded in the
in-context-learning and chain-of-thought literature
(Section~\ref{sec:related}): \emph{zero-shot} supplies only the task
instruction and output schema; \emph{few-shot} additionally supplies
worked demonstration examples \cite{brown2020fewshot}; \emph{structured}
additionally instructs the model to emit visible step-by-step reasoning
before a final answer \cite{wei2022cot}. Few-shot demonstrations are
synthetic, purpose-built for this study, and never drawn from a real
corpus project, so that no project's few-shot example can leak information
about that same project's held-out ground truth. The structured condition
is parsed by taking everything following a literal
\texttt{FINAL JSON:} marker and decoding one or more sequential top-level
JSON values from it, stopping cleanly once the remainder no longer starts a
decodable value; multiple bare risk objects with no enclosing array are
treated as the risks list, a bare top-level array is accepted in place of
the required object, and trailing prose after an otherwise-complete JSON
value no longer causes a failure.

An earlier version of this parser instead anchored a single greedy regular
expression to the end of the response, silently discarding every JSON value
after the first and rejecting a bare array outright. This was caught, not
assumed benign, after the \texttt{opensource}/\texttt{structured} cell
showed an implausible 100\% parse-failure rate (42 of 42 runs), which we
treated as a signal to audit the parser rather than a genuine model
collapse. Root-causing those raw responses against the schema found the
failure was structural, not specific to one model; applying the fix as a
backfill against the same immutable raw response text already on disk --
never regenerating or altering a real model call -- recovered 39 of 88
parse failures grid-wide (down to 49) and cut that cell specifically from
42 of 42 failures to 7 of 42. We report this in detail because, left
uninvestigated, it would have produced a materially wrong RQ2/RQ3 finding
-- a fabricated total collapse in one cell -- from a measurement artifact
rather than a real model behavior. The results in
Section~\ref{sec:results} reflect the corrected parser throughout; the
remaining 49 grid-wide parse failures were checked individually and are
genuine content issues (invalid categories outside the fixed taxonomy,
missing required fields, or, for one project specifically, output
truncation consistent with the hidden-reasoning-token behavior already
disclosed above), not further parsing artifacts.

\subsection{Free-Tier Infrastructure and Its Methodological Consequences}
All three models are accessed through providers' free usage tiers by
default, a direct consequence of the funding failure above; this has real
methodological consequences we disclose rather than confine to an internal
log (full detail: \texttt{docs/methodology\_log.md}). Free-tier daily
request quotas (Table~\ref{tab:quotas}) are small relative to the grid: the
full $189$-cell $\times$ 2-run grid ($378$ calls) requires repeated daily
invocations over roughly three weeks rather than a single run, so the
pipeline appends run metadata rather than overwriting it and a resumption
routine tops each cell up to its target run count rather than accumulating
duplicates. An earlier candidate open-weight provider (Groq) was rejected
during construction because its free tier's hard 8{,}000 token-per-request
ceiling is roughly four times smaller than this study's real prompts
(30{,}000--34{,}000 tokens) -- a negative provider finding worth disclosing
for other free-tier benchmark designs. Free-tier responses from more than
one provider were also observed to consume part of the output-token budget
on hidden, non-visible reasoning content before visible output, silently
truncating early runs at a 4{,}096-token budget until it was raised -- easy
to mistake for a model-quality difference rather than an output-budget
artifact, so disclosed here explicitly. Finally, because the open-weight
provider's daily cap binds how quickly the grid can be collected, the
pipeline falls back to a second provider (OpenRouter, routing to the
identical model identifiers) on a transient rate-limit error; unlike the
rest of the lineup this is a real, small paid service (US\$0.002--0.006/call
at observed pricing), and its first live use made 46 real calls before
hitting a genuine payment-required response -- a minor, disclosed
departure from the free-tier design, authorized after the fact.

\subsection{Output Schema}
Each generation is validated against a fixed JSON Schema before being
accepted: a top-level object naming the project and an array of risk
objects, with \texttt{additionalProperties} disallowed at both levels so
that a model cannot satisfy the schema by adding extra, unscored fields in
place of a required one. Each risk object requires seven fields: a stable
identifier unique within the register; a free-text description; exactly one
of eight fixed categories (technical, financial, schedule, stakeholder,
political/regulatory, environmental, organizational, external); likelihood
and impact, each on a 1--5 integer scale; a mitigation, required in
generation mode (unlike ground-truth extraction, where an explicit
``none stated'' is permitted); and an evidence field, capped at 25 words,
naming the specific planning-document passage that motivated the risk. A
generated risk with no evidence traceable to the supplied planning text is
the operational definition of a hallucination for RQ3 -- a model can only
satisfy the evidence field by pointing at real input text, not by asserting
plausibility. The structured reasoning condition additionally emits visible
step-by-step reasoning before a \texttt{FINAL JSON:} marker
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}); the zero-shot and few-shot conditions emit the
JSON object only, with no reasoning preamble to strip before validation.

\subsection{Evaluation}
Two methods are used, an automated ground-truth signal and a supplementary
model-based signal, so that the headline finding rests on a real,
independently authored artifact rather than on either instrument's known
weaknesses alone (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

\textit{Design note:} the original design additionally called for a third,
human-expert method -- blinded practitioner ratings on a 45-register
sample, with agreement measured via Fleiss' $\kappa$ -- as a check
independent of both the automated methods below. That protocol (sampling,
blinding, and the $\kappa$ computation itself) was fully built and tested,
but recruiting the 3--5 practitioner raters it depended on did not prove
feasible within this study's timeline, and we report that plainly rather
than substitute an LLM judge for the human baseline it was meant to
provide. This paper is accordingly scoped as a fully automated evaluation
study; the descoped human-rating design is discussed as a limitation
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) and a direction for future work
(Section~\ref{sec:future-work}), not silently dropped. One consequence of
this scoping is a genuine reproducibility property the original
three-method design did not have: Method A and Method C are both
re-runnable end-to-end from released code and already-public data alone,
with no recruiting step gating replication.

\textbf{Method A (primary): semantic matching.} Generated and ground-truth
risks within the same project are embedded with a sentence-embedding model
(\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) and matched greedily, highest-similarity-first,
one-to-one, above a similarity threshold validated against a hand-labeled
set of risk-description pairs. Matched pairs yield per-category recall
(share of ground-truth risks a model's register recovers) and precision
(share of generated risks that correspond to a real ground-truth risk);
generated risks with no match above threshold, and no evidence field
traceable to the source planning text, are treated as candidate
hallucinations for RQ3. Because the threshold was validated before any real
model output existed (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), it is revisited
against real generations once the full grid completes, not assumed correct
by construction.

\textbf{Method C (supplementary only): LLM-as-judge.} The same three
dimensions originally planned for human rating -- completeness,
accuracy/plausibility, and mitigation actionability, each 1--5 -- are
additionally scored by an LLM judge, given the same planning documentation
and generated register a human rater would have seen. Without a human
baseline to validate against, Method C is reported strictly as a
descriptive, secondary signal alongside Method A, explicitly never as an
independent quality measure and never as a proxy for the human judgment
Section~\ref{sec:methods}'s design note above describes -- a direct
consequence of the LLM-as-judge reliability concerns in
Section~\ref{sec:related}, not a claim that an LLM judge resolves them.

\subsection{Implementation}
The pipeline is organized as five stages, each a separate, independently
runnable script: extraction (PDF/HTML source documents to plain-text
planning documentation, with excision enforced at this stage rather than
left to a later filtering step); generation (prompt construction and
per-provider dispatch for all three models under all three prompting
strategies, with the retry and cost-guard behavior described above);
matching (Method A's embedding-based semantic matching between generated
and ground-truth risks); metrics aggregation (per-model, per-prompt, and
per-category recall/precision roll-ups, plus the pretraining-cutoff
subgroup analysis); and judging (Method C's supplementary LLM-as-judge
scoring). Each stage reads only the previous stage's output and its own
configuration, so that, for example, re-running Method A with an updated
similarity threshold never requires re-issuing any real model calls.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
Every generation call is logged to an append-only configuration record
capturing the exact model version string, run date, sampling temperature
and whether the provider actually honored it, and the SHA-256 hash of the
prompt file used, alongside the path to that call's raw output; raw outputs
themselves are never overwritten, only added to, so that a partially
completed experimental grid (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) can be resumed
without risk of silently duplicating or discarding a prior real call. The
corpus manifest, extraction and prompting code, output schema, and
evaluation pipeline are released alongside this paper so that the full grid
is independently reproducible, subject to each provider's own model-version
rollover policy (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

As of 2026-07-29, \texttt{results/raw\_outputs/} contains 392 real
generations and every one of the 189 (project $\times$ model $\times$
prompt) grid cells has at least 2 real runs. Method A (semantic matching
against ground truth) is reported below for RQ1--RQ3.

All figures below exclude the 5-project short-register subgroup unless
stated otherwise (Section~\ref{sec:methods}); that subgroup is reported
separately in Section~\ref{sec:rq1-results}, since its precision behaves
differently enough that folding it into the corpus-wide mean would obscure
both numbers. A parse failure -- the response could not be validated
against the output schema (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) -- counts as
\texttt{recall}~$=0$ for that run but is excluded from that run's precision
and category-accuracy, since neither is well-defined with zero generated
risks to score; this asymmetry means precision and category-accuracy means
are computed over fewer runs than recall means in any cell with parse
failures, and are reported as such rather than silently pooled.

\subsection{RQ1: Completeness and Accuracy vs.\ Human Registers}
\label{sec:rq1-results}
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=10.5cm]{../analysis/figures/fig_rq1_recall_precision.png}
\caption{Mean recall and precision, corpus-wide vs.\ the short-register subgroup, shown separately rather than pooled into one number (Section~\ref{sec:rq1-results}).\label{fig:rq1}}
\end{figure}
Corpus-wide (302 runs, excluding the short-register subgroup), generated
registers recover 55.6\% of ground-truth risks on average (mean recall
0.556) and 23.0\% of generated risks correspond to a real ground-truth risk
(mean precision 0.230); among matched pairs, the generated risk is filed
under the correct one of eight categories 45.0\% of the time (mean category
accuracy 0.450). Read plainly: across all three models and all three
prompting strategies, a generated register recovers roughly half of what a
human risk register actually contains, and roughly three-quarters of what
it generates does not correspond to anything in the human-authored
register at all. This is a more cautious picture than the blind expert-review study
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related}, whose reviewers scored ChatGPT's
output above the human baseline on the same task
\cite{ecam2024chatgpt_exceed_humans} -- a difference
we attribute to evaluation design rather than a contradiction: expert
review scores plausibility and completeness as perceived by a reader, while
semantic matching against a specific, real, independently authored register
penalizes a generated risk for not corresponding to anything in that exact
document, even when it would read as sensible in isolation. Both signals
are informative; this study's contribution is showing what the
ground-truth-matched picture looks like specifically, which no
prior work in Table~\ref{tab:gap} reports.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1} plots both groups' recall and precision side by side.

The 5-project short-register subgroup (90 runs) tells a sharper version of
the same story: mean recall is 0.682 -- \emph{higher} than the corpus-wide
figure -- while mean precision collapses to 0.095. Models do not
meaningfully shorten their output for a project whose real human register
is genuinely short; they generate a broadly similar volume of risks
regardless of the real register's size, so recall holds up while precision
craters. This is evidence against a simple ``models under-generate''
account of the recall gap above, and toward a ``models do not calibrate
output volume to the real risk surface of a specific project'' account
instead -- a distinction that matters for RQ3 below.

\subsection{Method C: LLM-as-Judge (Real Pilot, $n=5$)}
On 2026-08-18 we ran \texttt{src/judge.py} for the first time against real
generated registers, rather than the mocked responses it had previously
only been tested against. This was a small, deliberately bounded feasibility
pilot ($n=5$), not a full grid run: the same free-tier daily-quota
constraint already disclosed for the main generation grid
(Table~\ref{tab:quotas}) applies identically to judge calls, since the
configured judge model (\texttt{JUDGE\_MODEL\_LABEL=claude} in this
repository's own \texttt{.env}, i.e.\ the same Gemini model used as the
proprietary model under test) is subject to the same 20-request/day cap. The
five pilot files were drawn only from \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}- and
Llama-3.3-70B-generated registers, deliberately excluding
Gemini-generated ones: \texttt{judge.py}'s own source comments flag that
using one of the three benchmarked models as judge risks self-preference
bias on that model's own output, and a first pilot is a low-cost place to
sidestep that confound entirely rather than absorb it silently. A full-scale
run would still face it for the Gemini-generated third of the corpus, since
no fourth, non-benchmarked model is currently wired into the dispatch
table -- disclosed here as an unresolved limitation of the pipeline as
built, not papered over.

Of the five real judge calls, two (40\%) returned a valid, parseable score;
three (60\%) did not. Both valid results happen to be
\texttt{gpt-oss-120b}/zero-shot generations, scored completeness 5/5,
accuracy 5/5, actionability 4/5 and 5/5, and overall 5/5 in both cases;
one judge rationale read, in part, ``[the register] comprehensively
captures the primary fiduciary, operational, environmental, and
institutional risks detailed in the Project Appraisal Document.'' With only
two successful scores, both from the same model and prompting strategy,
this pilot supports no model- or prompt-comparison claim whatsoever -- it is
reported only as evidence the pipeline can produce a real, valid score, not
as a finding about model quality.

The three failures are themselves a genuine, disclosed pilot finding rather
than noise to discard. Inspecting the raw judge responses directly: two were
truncated mid-JSON, cut off after several score fields but before the
closing brace and rationale (e.g.\ \texttt{\{"completeness": 4, "accuracy":
4, "actionability": 2}, 53 characters total, no further content), and one
returned an entirely empty string. This is consistent with the same
hidden-reasoning-token output-truncation phenomenon already disclosed in
Section~\ref{sec:methods} for the main generation grid, but here striking
\texttt{judge.py}'s own separate, smaller, hardcoded 512-token output
budget rather than the main grid's. A 60\% truncation-driven failure rate
in a 5-call pilot indicates that budget would need to be enlarged before a
full-scale Method C run could be considered reliable; we disclose this as
an actionable infrastructure finding rather than attempt a larger run under
a budget already shown, in this same pilot, to fail most of the time. The
five raw pilot outputs (\texttt{results/scored\_pilot/}) are released
alongside this paper's other pipeline outputs so this finding is directly
inspectable rather than only asserted in prose.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Pretraining Contamination}
The corpus is drawn from public documents, which the evaluated models may
have encountered during pretraining -- including the risk registers held
out as ground truth. Excision controls what enters the prompt but cannot
control what a model already learned. We mitigate this in two ways: the
corpus deliberately spans both older and more recent documents by
publication date, and a supplementary analysis (opt-in, not part of the
headline metrics) compares performance on documents published before versus
after each model's training-data cutoff. This has been only partially
resolved as of this draft. The Gemini-slot model actually dispatched at
runtime (\texttt{gemini-flash-latest}, a rolling alias that resolved to
\texttt{gemini-3.6-flash} during the real experiment run) has a confirmed
knowledge cutoff of \textbf{March 2026}, per the model's official DeepMind
model card -- notably recent, only 4--5 months before the experiment run
itself, which makes contamination against already-public World Bank/UK
planning documents considerably more plausible than an older cutoff would.
One open-weight model's cutoff is confirmed as December 2023 (Meta's own
model card for Llama 3.3 70B). The other open-weight model
(\texttt{gpt-oss-120b}) has no training cutoff stated in any of OpenAI's own
sources checked directly; an independent academic benchmark
(LLMLagBench, P\k{e}zik et al.) reports three non-agreeing candidate values for
this model -- OpenAI's own declared figure of July 2024 (relayed via that
paper's citation, not independently confirmed against an OpenAI source
here), the paper's own behavioral estimate of approximately September 2023,
and the model's unreliable self-report of September 2021 -- see
\texttt{docs/model\_cutoffs.md} for the full sourcing. Rather than pick a
single number, we ran the supplementary comparison twice, holding Gemini's
and Llama's confirmed cutoffs fixed and setting \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}'s
cutoff first to the paper's own behavioral estimate (September 2023) and
then to OpenAI's declared figure (July 2024) -- excluding the September 2021
self-report from this range, since LLMLagBench itself flags it as
unreliable. The two runs produced \emph{identical} pre-/post-cutoff buckets
(71 pre-cutoff, 114 post-cutoff runs): no dated project in this corpus was
published between September 2023 and July 2024, so this study's contamination
comparison happens to be insensitive to exactly where within that range
\texttt{gpt-oss-120b}'s real cutoff falls -- a real, checked robustness
property of this specific corpus, not an assumption. Pooled across all three
models at their respective cutoffs, pre-cutoff runs average lower recall
(0.435) and higher precision (0.278) than post-cutoff runs (recall 0.585,
precision 0.219) -- the opposite direction from a simple contamination story,
in which a model that had memorized a pre-cutoff document's real register
would be expected to show \emph{higher}, not lower, recall on exactly those
documents. This pattern is consistent with the corpus's older World Bank
documents (2015--2017 publication dates, all pre-cutoff for every model)
differing in some other way -- format, planning-document verbosity, or era-
specific writing conventions -- that happens to correlate with lower
measured recall, rather than with contamination inflating pre-cutoff scores;
we report it as a finding against the contamination hypothesis for this
corpus, not as proof contamination did not occur, since a 10-of-21-project,
71-run pre-cutoff subset is not powered to rule it out definitively.
Per-model, the pre-/post-cutoff split is driven almost entirely by
Gemini's very recent cutoff (35 pre / 30 post, since only one dated project
postdates March 2026) and by \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}/Llama-3.3-70B's much
older cutoffs (18 pre / 42 post each, since only the corpus's three
oldest, 2015--2017 documents predate either). A systematic gap
between groups would be a contamination signal, though a suggestive one,
not dispositive proof either way. \texttt{publication\_date} is populated
for only 10 of the 21 included projects at present, which limits this
supplementary analysis to that subset. We considered, and rejected, an
automated backfill for the remaining 11: World Bank appraisal documents
carry a mechanically extractable ``Expected Approval Date'' field, but
cross-checking it against the 10 already-dated projects showed it records a
different concept entirely (a drafting-time projection of a future Board
decision, which may precede, follow, or never match the real approval
outcome) from what \texttt{publication\_date} is meant to capture (when the
document was actually posted). Substituting one for the other under the
same field name would misrepresent a genuine methodological gap as a solved
one; we report the missing dates as a limitation instead.

\subsection{Provider API Behavior}
The experiment runner records a \texttt{temperature\_applied} flag on every
generation rather than silently assuming uniform temperature control across
all three models; a real deviation would be reported as such, not hidden.
An earlier proprietary model considered for this study (since replaced --
Section~\ref{sec:methods}) was documented to reject the
\texttt{temperature} parameter outright in its reasoning mode; the models
actually used in the current lineup have not been sourced to share that
constraint, but the flag remains in place as a general-purpose safeguard.
Transient provider errors (rate-limit and server-side failures, as opposed
to malformed requests or missing credentials) are retried up to three times
per call with exponential backoff before being recorded as a failed run; a
non-transient error is never retried, since repeating a call that cannot
possibly succeed only wastes free-tier daily quota that a different,
viable cell could have used instead. A cost guard separately halts any
invocation whose estimated spend exceeds a fixed threshold before a single
paid call is made, though this guard is inactive by construction while the
pipeline runs entirely on free-tier providers (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).

\subsection{Free-Tier Provider Data Usage}
The Gemini-slot provider's free-tier terms of service state that unpaid-tier
input and output may be used to develop and improve the provider's own
products, and that human reviewers may read submitted content -- a
protection against training-data use that its paid tier separately
provides. The two open-weight models' free-tier provider (SambaNova Cloud)
does not state, in its own published privacy policy, whether submitted
prompts or outputs are used for model training or human review for its
serverless API -- this is reported here as an open question rather than
either a confirmed protection or a confirmed risk. All planning documents
sent as model input in this study are already-public World Bank or UK
government documents, which substantially limits the practical sensitivity
of this exposure; nonetheless, this is disclosed here as a real property of
the experimental setup rather than omitted.

\subsection{Method B Descoping}
\label{sec:rater-status}
Method B's protocol -- sampling design, blinding mechanism, and the Fleiss'
$\kappa$ computation itself -- was fully implemented and tested against a
hand-derived worked example. Recruitment of the 3--5 practitioner raters
the protocol depended on did not prove feasible within this study's
timeline, and rather than let a not-yet-complete recruitment effort
indefinitely gate submission, we formally descope Method B from this
paper's evaluation design (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) and report the
decision plainly rather than leave a placeholder implying it is still in
progress. The built protocol, sampling code, and blinded packet-generation
pipeline are released alongside this paper regardless, so that a future
replication with real recruiting capacity does not have to rebuild it
(Section~\ref{sec:future-work}). In its place, the paper's evaluation now
rests entirely on Method A and Method C, both of which any reader can
re-execute against the released corpus and pipeline without recruiting
anyone -- a scalability property worth stating plainly rather than
treating the descoping as pure loss.

\subsection{Ethical Considerations}
All planning documents used as model input are already-public World Bank
Project Appraisal Documents or UK government business cases; no
confidential, proprietary, or personally identifying material enters any
prompt. The free-tier provider data-usage disclosures in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} apply to already-public source material for
this reason, which substantially limits their practical severity even where
the usage terms themselves are unfavorable or unstated. No human-subject
data is collected in this paper's evaluation design (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).

\subsection{Human Ground Truth Is Not a Perfect Oracle}
Method A measures overlap with one specific published human register;
treating a low Method A recall as straightforwardly ``the model missed a
real risk'' presumes the human register is itself complete, which is not
guaranteed -- published registers are themselves a product of time
constraints, institutional templates, and reviewer judgment, and can omit
risks a careful independent review would surface. Without Method B's
independent human-judgment signal (Section~\ref{sec:rater-status}), this
study cannot distinguish a low Method A recall caused by a genuine
generation gap from one caused by a genuine gap in the ground truth
itself; we report this as an open limitation of the fully automated design
rather than assume the published register is complete by construction.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
Method A's similarity threshold was validated against a hand-labeled set of
risk-description pairs rather than a real experiment run, since no real
model output existed at the time of validation; it should be revisited
against real generations if the corpus or embedding model changes. Method C
is deliberately never treated as an independent quality measure, for the
reasons discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:related}. The Method C pilot's 60\% parse-failure rate
(Section~\ref{sec:results}) is a threat to that pilot's own usefulness
specifically, not to Method A: inspection of the raw responses traced it to
\texttt{judge.py}'s fixed 512-token output budget being too small for this
judge model's response length, the same class of output-truncation issue
already disclosed for the main generation grid, rather than to a defect in
the judge model itself. This narrows what needs fixing before a full-scale
Method C run (enlarging that budget) but does not, on a 5-call pilot,
establish that doing so would be sufficient. Finally, the free-tier infrastructure
constraints described in Section~\ref{sec:methods} mean the full grid is
collected over an extended, multi-week period of daily re-invocation rather
than a single run; because model version strings and sampling temperature
are logged per call (Section~\ref{sec:methods}), any provider-side model
rollover mid-collection would be visible in the logged data rather than
silently conflated with a stable model, but a rollover occurring would
still be a real threat to the comparability of runs collected on either
side of it.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}
Three directions follow directly from what this study's own results and
limitations expose, rather than from generic extension of the design.

\textbf{Closing the recall gap with retrieval or structured knowledge.}
RQ1--RQ3 show a specific, not-uniform failure pattern: models
systematically under-surface political/regulatory risk
(Section~\ref{sec:rq3-results}) and do not calibrate output volume to a
project's real risk surface (Section~\ref{sec:rq1-results}), rather than
failing evenly across the board. Both the classification benchmark and the
case-study agent discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related} point toward
retrieval-augmented or taxonomy-grounded prompting as a plausible fix for
exactly this kind of category-specific gap; this study's design (fixed
zero-shot/few-shot/structured comparison) does not test that direction, and
a natural next step is a fourth condition that grounds generation in a
structured risk taxonomy or retrieved prior registers, evaluated with the
same semantic-matching pipeline already built here.

\textbf{Reviving Method B with real recruiting capacity.} Method B's
protocol, sampling design, and blinded packet pipeline are built, tested,
and released, but not run in this paper (Section~\ref{sec:rater-status}).
A natural, low-cost follow-up is completing that recruitment and reporting
where the resulting human ratings agree and disagree with Method A at the
level of individual registers, not only in aggregate -- for example,
whether the low-precision, high-recall pattern found in the short-register
subgroup (Section~\ref{sec:rq1-results}) is one human raters would penalize
heavily, mildly, or not at all, which Method A and Method C together
cannot answer without a human baseline.

\textbf{Contamination-controlled replication as model rosters change.} The
free-tier model lineup used here (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) is a
disclosed, real constraint on this study's model roster, not a claim that
these three models are the ideal or final comparison set. As pretraining
cutoffs move past this corpus's publication dates and as model access
economics change, re-running this same released pipeline
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}) against a different or larger model roster --
particularly once the pretraining-contamination analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} can be completed on a full
\texttt{publication\_date}-labeled corpus rather than the 10-of-21 subset
available now -- would test whether the recall/precision/category-accuracy
pattern found here is a property of these specific models or a more general
property of current-generation LLMs applied to this task.

\section{Conclusions}

This paper benchmarked three large language models -- one general-purpose
proprietary model and two architecturally distinct open-weight models --
under three prompting strategies on end-to-end risk-register generation
from real project planning documentation, evaluated against published,
human-authored ground truth across 21 World Bank and UK government
projects. To our knowledge this is the first such benchmark to compare
generation, rather than classification of already-identified risks, against
real ground-truth registers across multiple models, addressing a gap in
which prior work has been almost entirely single-model and evaluated
against expert opinion rather than a genuine published artifact
(Section~\ref{sec:related}).

The Method A results answer the paper's three research questions plainly.
On RQ1, generated registers recover about half of what a human-authored
register actually contains (mean recall 0.556) while roughly three-quarters
of what they generate does not correspond to anything in that project's
real register (mean precision 0.230); the short-register subgroup shows
this is a calibration failure, not a volume failure -- models do not shrink
their output for a project whose real risk surface is genuinely small
(Section~\ref{sec:rq1-results}). On RQ2, model choice separates recall more
cleanly than prompting strategy: the proprietary model leads recall in
every condition, while the lowest-recall open-weight model instead has the
highest category accuracy and, in most conditions, the highest precision --
a conservative, fewer-but-more-accurate failure mode distinct from the
higher-recall model's broader, noisier output. Structured prompting is the
one prompting-strategy effect that holds across all three models, improving
category accuracy consistently even though it does not consistently improve
recall (Section~\ref{sec:rq2-results}). On RQ3, the failure pattern is
specific rather than uniform: political/regulatory risk is the category
models most often fail to surface at all, while schedule risk is generated
as a near-default, safe completion regardless of whether a project's real
register supports it (Section~\ref{sec:rq3-results}) -- evidence that
closing this gap needs targeted intervention (Section~\ref{sec:future-work}),
not simply larger or more capable models applied to the same
zero-shot/few-shot/structured comparison.

This study is scoped as a fully automated evaluation: a blinded
human-expert rating design was built and tested -- sampling, a
plain-language instrument for project-management practitioners, and
Fleiss' $\kappa$ across 3--5 raters -- but recruiting those raters did not
prove feasible within this study's timeline, and we report that descoping
directly (Section~\ref{sec:rater-status}) rather than substitute an LLM
judge for the human baseline it was meant to provide. Running that
protocol for real, and reporting where its human judgments agree or
disagree with Method A at the level of individual registers
(Section~\ref{sec:future-work}), is the paper's most direct remaining next
step. Beyond that, this study contributes a disclosed, reproducible account
of what it actually took to run a real, multi-week, free-tier-constrained
LLM benchmark -- provider quota limits, a parser bug that would have
fabricated a full model collapse if left uninvestigated
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}), and a pretraining-contamination check that
turned out to run against the contamination hypothesis for this specific
corpus (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) -- alongside the corpus manifest,
extraction and prompting code, and evaluation pipeline, released so that
the benchmark is a reproducible artifact rather than a set of numbers in a
table.
