\section{Introduction}

Large language models are increasingly proposed as tools for project
management tasks, yet the literature concentrates heavily on predictive
analytics for time, cost, and quality rather than generative capability
\cite{aidriven2026usecases}. Within the narrower body of work that does
study LLMs for project risk work specifically, evaluation has so far relied
on expert-opinion panels or author judgment rather than comparison against
real, published human-authored artifacts, has been almost entirely
single-model (typically ChatGPT only), and has not distinguished the
\emph{generation} of a full risk register from the narrower task of
\emph{classifying} already-identified risk items
\cite{asce2025exploring_chatgpt_risk,buildings2025risk_classification}.

This gap motivates the present study:
\begin{quote}
\emph{While recent studies have assessed ChatGPT's ability to discuss
project risks under expert evaluation, no work has benchmarked multiple
LLMs on end-to-end risk register generation from real project documents
against published human-authored registers, leaving both the accuracy and
the systematic failure modes of this capability unquantified.}
\end{quote}

We address it with three frozen research questions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} How complete and accurate are LLM-generated risk
  registers compared to human-authored ones?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} How do results vary across three models (a frontier
  proprietary model, a competing proprietary model, and an open-weight
  model) and three prompting strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, and
  structured reasoning)?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} Which risk categories do LLMs systematically miss or
  hallucinate? This failure-mode analysis is the paper's core contribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{AI in Project Management}
Systematic reviews of AI in project management consistently find that
generative applications are among the least-studied AI functions in the
field, with research concentrated instead on predictive analytics for
schedule, cost, and quality outcomes
\cite{aidriven2026usecases,aitools2025knowledge}. Adoption itself is
reported as limited by practitioners' unfamiliarity with the tools rather
than by demonstrated capability gaps \cite{aitools2025knowledge}.

\subsection{LLMs for Project Risk Management}
The closest prior work evaluates ChatGPT against expert judgment on
construction-project risk identification and mitigation, finding strong
performance on generic, checklist-style risks but weaker performance on
context-specific risks -- typically the highest-severity ones
\cite{asce2025exploring_chatgpt_risk}. Related studies report similar
patterns using GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 under expert or focus-group evaluation
\cite{aladag2023assessing_chatgpt,ecam2024chatgpt_exceed_humans}, and one
develops a fine-tuned agent (RiskGPT) evaluated on a small set of case
studies rather than a public benchmark \cite{wen2025riskgpt}. Across this
line of work, three limitations recur: single-model evaluation (almost
always ChatGPT), no comparison against real published ground-truth
registers, and no publicly released, reproducible benchmark.

\subsection{Risk Classification vs.\ Generation}
A closely related but distinct task is classifying already-identified risk
items into categories, which has been benchmarked across NLP and LLM
methods \cite{buildings2025risk_classification}. This is a materially
easier task than the one studied here: it presupposes that the risks have
already been identified, whereas end-to-end register generation requires a
model to both identify and characterize risks from raw project
documentation.

\subsection{LLM Evaluation Methodology}
LLM-as-judge protocols are documented to be unstable across near-identical
prompts, subject to positional and self-enhancement bias, and poorly
calibrated in expert-knowledge domains
\cite{zheng2023judging_llm_judge,iui2025llmjudge_expert,nofreelabels2025}.
This motivates our decision (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) to treat LLM-as-judge
scoring as strictly supplementary to semantic matching against ground truth
and blinded human expert rating, never as a stand-alone measure
\cite{llmsasjudges2024survey}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Corpus}
The corpus comprises 21 real-world projects with both public planning
documentation and a published, human-authored risk register: 18 World Bank
Project Appraisal Documents (PADs) and 3 UK government business cases
(Five Case Model format). Each candidate project was required to clear four
gate criteria: (1) a structured, rated risk table is present; (2) a
substantive, itemized narrative risk discussion beyond the table exists;
(3) the boundary between planning content and risk content is locatable and
independently re-verifiable against the source document, not merely a
previously recorded page range; and (4) at least 15 pages of planning
content survive excision of the risk content. Two UK candidates and one
World Bank candidate were read in full and excluded or set aside for
failing gate (2); one UK project (13 pages of surviving planning content)
is retained as a disclosed exception to gate (4) rather than silently
lowering the threshold.

\subsection{Leakage Control}
Because several source documents contain the human risk register within the
same file as the planning narrative, we applied a manual leakage-control
protocol rather than automated section detection, which is unreliable
across document formats spanning many years. For each project, an author
located the risk section (e.g., the World Bank SORT risk table) and excised
it from the text supplied to the models, recording the exact page range
removed; the remaining planning text was then checked for narrative
passages that restate the register's risks, which were likewise removed.
Every excision range was independently re-verified against the source
document itself before ground truth was finalized -- not merely re-checked
against the previously recorded range -- which caught three distinct
under- and over-excision errors during corpus construction. A standing
audit tool independently re-scans included documents for heading/table
patterns and greps ground-truth text against processed planning text for
verbatim or near-verbatim recurrence, run on every corpus change.

\subsection{Experimental Grid}
Three models -- a frontier proprietary model, a second proprietary model,
and an open-weight model -- are each evaluated under three prompting
strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, and structured reasoning) across all 21
projects, with 2 runs per cell at temperature 0--0.1, yielding a
189-cell $\times$ 2-run grid. Models receive planning/appraisal
documentation only; the ground-truth register for a given project never
enters that project's prompt or any few-shot example (few-shot
demonstrations are fully synthetic, constructed for this purpose, and never
drawn from a real corpus project).

\subsection{Output Schema}
Each generated risk is a fixed JSON object: a stable identifier, a
description, one of eight categories (technical, financial, schedule,
stakeholder, political/regulatory, environmental, organizational,
external), likelihood and impact on 1--5 scales, a required mitigation, and
a required evidence field citing the specific planning-document passage
that motivated the risk -- a generated risk with no traceable evidence is
the operational definition of a hallucination for RQ3. The structured
reasoning condition additionally emits visible step-by-step reasoning before
a \texttt{FINAL JSON:} marker; the zero-shot and few-shot conditions emit
the JSON object only.

\subsection{Evaluation}
Three methods are used. \textbf{Method A (primary):} sentence-embedding
semantic matching (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}) between generated and
ground-truth risks within the same project, greedy highest-similarity-first
one-to-one matching above a validated threshold, yielding per-category
recall and precision. \textbf{Method B:} blinded expert practitioner
ratings on completeness, accuracy, and mitigation actionability, with
inter-rater agreement reported via Fleiss' $\kappa$
\cite{fleiss1971kappa} and interpreted using standard agreement bands
\cite{landiskoch1977agreement}. \textbf{Method C (supplementary only):}
LLM-as-judge scoring on the same three dimensions, reported strictly as a
correlation against Method B once human ratings exist, per the
methodological concerns discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related}.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\subsection{Pretraining Contamination}
The corpus is drawn from public documents, which the evaluated models may
have encountered during pretraining -- including the risk registers held
out as ground truth. Excision controls what enters the prompt but cannot
control what a model already learned. We mitigate this in two ways: the
corpus deliberately spans both older and more recent documents by
publication date, and a supplementary analysis (opt-in, not part of the
headline metrics) compares performance on documents published before versus
after each model's training-data cutoff
(Claude: January 2026; the competing proprietary model: February 16, 2026;
the open-weight model: December 2023). A systematic gap between these
groups would be a contamination signal, though a suggestive one, not
dispositive proof either way. \texttt{publication\_date} is populated for
only 10 of the 21 included projects at present, which limits this
supplementary analysis to that subset; we report this as a limitation
rather than backfilling the field from a different, non-equivalent date
found in the source documents.

\subsection{Provider API Behavior}
One evaluated model's reasoning mode is documented to reject the
\texttt{temperature} parameter outright; the experiment runner detects this
and retries without it, recording \texttt{temperature\_applied=false} on
the affected records rather than silently claiming uniform temperature
control across all three models. Any run where this fires is a real,
disclosed deviation from strict temperature uniformity and is reported as
such rather than hidden.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
Method A's similarity threshold was validated against a hand-labeled set of
risk-description pairs rather than a real experiment run, since no real
model output existed at the time of validation; it should be revisited
against real generations if the corpus or embedding model changes.
Method C is deliberately never treated as an independent quality measure,
for the reasons discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related}.
